German Shepherd Anxiety: Complete Calming Guide

German Shepherds are loyal, watchful, high-energy working dogs. When their needs for exercise, training and calm alone-time practice are not met, some show anxiety through pacing, barking, destruction or reactivity. This guide explains the common symptoms, how to tell separation anxiety from boredom, humane training steps and when to involve a vet.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated November 25, 202610 min read
Quick answer: What Are the Signs of Anxiety in a German Shepherd?

Common German Shepherd anxiety symptoms include pacing, whining, panting, barking or howling, chewing or digging at doors and windows, house soiling, and trouble settling. The ASPCA notes that separation anxiety signs happen when a dog is left alone or separated from their person, not when you are home. The AKC describes the breed as “loyal, confident, courageous, and steady,” so constant restlessness, sudden reactivity or a change in eating is worth a vet check to rule out pain or illness.

  • Watch for: Pacing, whining, destruction at exits, and accidents that happen only when your GSD is alone.
  • Rule out health first: Food refusal, sudden behavior change, or self-injury from licking or chewing means calling your vet early.
  • Start small: Daily exercise and training, plus short, calm absences your dog can handle without distress.

Breed Overview: The GSD Temperament

German Shepherds were developed in Germany in the late 19th century, largely through Captain Max von Stephanitz, as herding and working dogs. Today they work in police, military, search and rescue and guide roles, and many more live as family pets.

Many GSDs are naturally alert and quick to notice changes around them. In a working home that watchfulness is useful. In a quiet house with long, unprepared alone time, it can show up as restlessness, alarm barking or difficulty settling.

The German Shepherd ranks No. 4 in the AKC’s 2024 breed popularity list, so many live in homes very different from the jobs they were bred for. Matching daily life to the dog’s needs matters more than breed labels: individual temperament, early experiences and health all shape anxiety risk.

Why Do Some German Shepherds Get Anxious?

Strong Attachment to Their People

Many German Shepherds attach strongly to one or two people and like to stay close to them. That loyalty is part of the breed’s appeal, but a dog that has never practiced being alone may find departures hard. Some anxious GSDs look frustrated rather than fearful, with pacing, whining or barking at the door. For everyday shadowing and room-to-room following, see why German Shepherds are so clingy and how to build independence without flooding them.

Hypervigilance: A Nervous System That Never Fully Rests

Many GSDs are watchful by nature and notice small sounds and changes in routine. In a busy or noisy home, a dog that is always on alert may struggle to relax. Giving them a predictable routine and a quiet resting spot can help.

The Working Dog Vacuum

Most German Shepherds today are pets, but many still need regular mental work, such as structured training, problem-solving or scent games. Without it, some create their own outlets: fence-line patrolling, alarm barking at every sound, herding family members, or chewing. These behaviors can look like anxiety, and boredom and anxiety often overlap.

Fear-Based Reactivity Warning: In some German Shepherds, fear shows up as growling, lunging or snapping at strangers or other dogs. This is usually fear-based reactivity, not dominance. The AVSAB advises reward-based training and warns that aversive methods are linked to more fear and aggression. If your GSD shows reactive behavior in public, seek a certified professional dog trainer (CPDT) or veterinary behaviorist before the pattern becomes entrenched.

Common Anxiety Triggers for German Shepherds

Owner Departure and Routine Changes

GSDs learn routines quickly, including the cues that come before you leave, like picking up keys or putting on shoes. Some start pacing or whining before you are out the door. This is called anticipatory anxiety, and departure-cue practice (below) can help.

Novel Environments and Unfamiliar People

Some German Shepherds are wary of strangers, especially in unfamiliar places, and that wariness can turn into reactivity if they feel cornered. Vet offices, grooming salons and busy dog parks are common stressful settings.

Noise and Environmental Threats

Thunderstorms, fireworks and construction noise can trigger fear in German Shepherds as in any breed. Signs include pacing, panting, hiding or barking. Noise fear that gets worse over time is worth raising with your vet.

Under-Exercise and Mental Boredom

A German Shepherd that gets little exercise or mental work often has more energy than it can use, which can show up as restlessness, barking or chewing. This is one of the most fixable causes of GSD behavior problems, but more exercise alone will not cure true separation anxiety.

What Are the Symptoms of Anxiety in German Shepherds?

Signs of anxiety in German Shepherds are similar to those in other dogs, but a large, strong dog can do more damage when distressed. Common signs listed by the ASPCA and seen in GSDs include:

  • Persistent pacing, often along the same path (fence-running, circling)
  • Chewing or digging at exits, such as door frames, window sills and crate doors
  • Excessive barking or howling when left alone
  • Reactive behavior toward strangers that seems disproportionate to the trigger
  • Constant alertness and trouble settling, even in a calm home
  • Panting, drooling, trembling, or not eating while you are away
  • Self-directed behaviors: excessive licking of paws or flanks, tail chewing
  • House soiling in a house-trained dog, only when left alone

If these signs happen mainly when your GSD is alone, separation anxiety is more likely than boredom; our separation anxiety guide explains the difference and a step-by-step plan. If your dog stops eating, injures itself, or changes behavior suddenly, see your vet early, because pain and illness can cause similar signs.

Training and Management Strategies

Independence Training: Teaching Calmness as a Skill

German Shepherds need to learn that calm is rewarding and that your absence is non-threatening. Start with "place" training — designate a specific mat or bed and train your GSD to go there and settle on command. Reward calm, quiet stays with high-value treats. This gives them a "job" (holding place) and a positive emotional association with settling.

For departure desensitization: pick up your keys and sit back down. Put on your shoes and watch television. Open the front door and close it without leaving. Repeat these departure cues dozens of times until they lose their predictive value. For many dogs, anxiety at these cues fades as they stop predicting your absence. Pair this with very short real departures your dog can handle calmly, and build up slowly.

Daily Mental Stimulation

Most German Shepherds do best with short training or enrichment sessions every day, not just obedience basics but activities that use their natural drives. Options include:

  • Nose work / scent tracking: Hide treats around the yard or house, then build up to organized scent work. Many GSDs enjoy using their nose.
  • Obedience and trick training: Short, reward-based sessions a few times a day give your dog a job and build calm focus.
  • Schutzhund / IGP sport: For working-line GSDs especially, tracking or other dog sports with a reward-based club can give their drives a constructive outlet.

The Exercise Baseline

German Shepherds are a high-energy breed, and most healthy adults need daily physical exercise plus training to stay calm at home. The right amount depends on age, health and line; puppies, seniors and dogs with joint problems need vet-guided limits. Walks, fetch, sniff walks and swimming are good options. A dog that has had a chance to exercise and sniff is usually better able to settle and learn.

Working Line vs Show Line: German Shepherds come in two primary lineages. Working-line GSDs (often Czech or DDR lines) usually have higher drive and may need more mental and physical activity. If your GSD has intense energy and extreme reactivity, ask your breeder what line your dog comes from — it can help shape your plan.

When to See a Vet

German Shepherd anxiety warrants veterinary consultation when:

  • Reactive or aggressive behavior has escalated despite consistent training
  • Self-directed behaviors (licking, chewing) have caused skin injury
  • Your dog cannot settle for more than a few minutes even after adequate exercise
  • Destructive behavior during absence is putting your dog or property at serious risk
  • Your dog stops eating, seems unwell, or changes behavior suddenly (call your vet early in these cases)

For moderate to severe separation anxiety, your vet may discuss medication. Fluoxetine (Reconcile) and clomipramine (Clomicalm) are FDA-approved for separation anxiety in dogs when used together with behavior modification. The AVSAB notes that medication may be needed for serious fear and anxiety problems, and that a vet should check for medical causes first. Never give human medication without your vet’s guidance.

Frequently Asked Questions: German Shepherd Anxiety

What are the symptoms of anxiety in German Shepherds?
Common German Shepherd anxiety symptoms include pacing, whining, panting, barking or howling, chewing or digging at doors and windows, house soiling and trouble settling. If these signs happen mainly when your dog is alone, separation anxiety is more likely. Sudden changes, food refusal or self-injury mean you should call your vet early to rule out pain or illness.
Are German Shepherds prone to separation anxiety?
Any dog can develop separation anxiety, and there is no reliable figure for how common it is in German Shepherds. The breed is loyal, alert and high-energy, so a GSD that gets little exercise, little training or no practice being alone may struggle more with departures. Individual temperament, early experiences and health matter as much as breed.
Is my German Shepherd's aggression actually anxiety?
Often it can be. Some German Shepherds express fear as reactive behavior, such as growling, lunging or snapping at strangers or other dogs. This is usually fear-based reactivity, not dominance. The usual approach is desensitization and counter-conditioning with a qualified trainer or veterinary behaviorist, not punishment, which can make fear-based reactivity worse.
What size Thundershirt for a German Shepherd?
Measure the chest at the widest point behind the front legs rather than going by weight. On the ThunderShirt chart, Large fits a 25–30 inch chest, XL 30–37 inches and XXL 37–50 inches. Many female German Shepherds wear L–XL and larger males XL–XXL. If your dog is between sizes, follow the manufacturer's sizing advice.
How much exercise does an anxious German Shepherd need?
German Shepherds are a high-energy breed, and most healthy adults need daily physical exercise plus short training or scent sessions. The right amount depends on age, health and line, so ask your vet for limits for puppies, seniors or dogs with joint problems. Exercise helps a dog settle, but it does not cure true separation anxiety on its own.
Should I crate train my anxious German Shepherd?
It depends on the dog. Some GSDs feel safe in a crate, but dogs with confinement anxiety can injure themselves trying to escape, such as broken teeth or damaged paws. Introduce a crate gradually with rewards over several weeks. If your dog panics in the crate, stop and try a safe room or gated area instead, and talk to your vet or a qualified trainer.
Why does my German Shepherd pace and whine when I leave?
Pacing and whining at departure are early separation anxiety signals. GSDs often start before you've left, reading routine cues like picking up keys or putting on shoes. Practice those cues without leaving, so they stop predicting your absence, and combine this with very short real departures that your dog can handle calmly.
What calming supplements work for German Shepherds?
Evidence for calming supplements is limited. Zylkene (alpha-casozepine) has a few small studies suggesting it may help some dogs with mild anxiety, but it does not treat severe separation anxiety. For moderate to severe cases, ask your vet about FDA-approved options such as fluoxetine (Reconcile) or clomipramine (Clomicalm), used together with behavior modification.
