Australian Shepherd Anxiety: Complete Calming Guide

Australian Shepherds are smart, energetic, work-oriented herding dogs, and many are very attached to their people. Some Aussies struggle when left alone, during storms, or when they have too little to do, but the breed label alone does not decide that. This guide explains the signs of separation anxiety in Aussies, how to tell boredom or herding drive from real panic, how to build alone time step by step, and when to involve your vet.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated November 28, 20268 min read
Quick answer: Do Australian Shepherds Get Separation Anxiety?

Some do, but we have not found reliable evidence that Australian Shepherds as a breed are more prone to separation anxiety than other dogs. The AKC describes Aussies as smart, work-oriented and exuberant dogs that “require vigorous daily exercise” and “thrive on lots of mental and physical exercise,” so an under-occupied Aussie may be restless or destructive for reasons other than anxiety. The ASPCA explains that separation anxiety signs, such as barking or howling, chewing or digging at doors and windows, accidents, pacing and escape attempts, happen when a dog is left alone or separated from their person, and it advises seeing your vet first to rule out medical problems.

  • Watch for: Distress that starts as you get ready to leave or happens only when your Aussie is alone.
  • Rule out health first: Food refusal, a dog who seems unwell, sudden behavior change or new house soiling means calling your vet early.
  • Start small: Daily exercise and brain work, plus short absences your dog handles calmly, building up gradually.

Are Australian Shepherds Prone to Anxiety?

Not as a rule. We have not found good research showing that Australian Shepherds are more anxious than other dogs, and individual Aussies vary a lot. What the breed does have is a working background: the AKC describes the Aussie as a lean, tough ranch dog that is still used as an all-around ranch hand and herder, rates the breed high for energy and mental stimulation needs, and says Aussies excel at agility and obedience training.

That matters because a bored, under-exercised Aussie can pace, bark, chew or herd family members, and those behaviors can look like anxiety. Meeting daily exercise and mental needs is a good first step, but it does not cure true separation anxiety on its own.

Why Is My Australian Shepherd So Clingy? Velcro Aussie vs Separation Anxiety

Following you from room to room is often normal for an affectionate, people-focused dog; the AKC describes Aussies as affectionate with their people and eager to be useful. Clinginess becomes a concern when your dog panics when you leave, cannot settle while you are home, or the behavior appears suddenly. A sudden change is a reason to call your vet. For day-to-day independence work, see our Aussie clingy behavior guide.

What Are the Signs of Separation Anxiety in an Australian Shepherd?

According to the ASPCA, common signs of separation anxiety include:

  • Urinating or defecating indoors only when left alone
  • Barking or howling after you leave
  • Chewing, digging or destruction, often around doors and windows
  • Trying to escape from a room or crate, sometimes causing injury
  • Pacing in a fixed pattern when alone

The key detail is timing: these signs happen when your dog is alone or separated from their person, not when you are home. Recording a short absence is the easiest way to see what really happens. Our separation anxiety signs guide explains more.

Is It Boredom, Herding Drive or Separation Anxiety?

  • Boredom: Chewing or restlessness that also happens when you are home, often eases with more exercise, training and food puzzles.
  • Herding behavior: Circling, staring or nipping at heels of children, pets or moving objects. This is herding drive, not separation anxiety. Supervise children, and get help from a qualified positive-reinforcement trainer if your dog nips.
  • Separation anxiety: Distress tied to your departure or absence, such as vocalizing, escape attempts or accidents only when alone.

How Much Exercise Does an Australian Shepherd Need?

The AKC says most Aussies require vigorous daily exercise and thrive on mental as well as physical exercise. There is no single number of minutes that fits every dog; age, health and fitness matter, so ask your vet before increasing exercise for a puppy, a senior or a dog with joint problems. Training sessions, scent games and enrichment activities count as brain work. Dog sports like agility, obedience or herding classes are optional outlets, not a treatment for separation anxiety.

How Do I Help My Australian Shepherd Be Alone?

The ASPCA describes two approaches:

  • Mild cases: Counterconditioning, which links being alone with something good, such as a food toy given just before you leave.
  • Moderate to severe cases: Gradual desensitization and counterconditioning, starting with absences so short your dog stays calm and building up slowly. The ASPCA notes this is complex and recommends guidance from a Certified Applied Animal Behaviorist or a board-certified veterinary behaviorist.

During training, the ASPCA advises not leaving your dog alone longer than they can handle, so plan other care, such as a sitter, daycare or a friend for longer days. A crate can help some dogs, but if your Aussie pants heavily, drools, tries to escape or howls in the crate, the ASPCA suggests using a room behind a baby gate instead. Never punish your dog for anxious behavior. See how long an Australian Shepherd can be left alone and our separation anxiety guide for a step-by-step plan.

What About Storms and Noise Fear?

Some Aussies are afraid of thunderstorms, fireworks or other loud noises, and some are not. Noise fear is a separate problem from separation anxiety, although a dog can have both. Our noise phobia guide covers safe spaces, gradual sound work and when medication may be worth discussing with your vet.

When Should I Call the Vet?

Call your vet early, before starting a training plan, if your Australian Shepherd:

  • Stops eating, refuses food when left alone, or seems unwell
  • Shows a sudden change in behavior, clinginess or house soiling
  • Hurts themselves trying to escape, or chews or licks themselves raw
  • Spins, chases shadows or does other repetitive behaviors that are getting worse
  • Nips or bites in a way that breaks skin

The ASPCA says medication can be very helpful, especially for severe separation anxiety, but always consult your veterinarian or a veterinary behaviorist before giving your dog any medication for a behavior problem.

Do Calming Products Help Australian Shepherds?

Products are optional extras, not a cure. They work best alongside training, and results vary between dogs. Ask your vet before using any supplement or calming aid, especially if your dog takes medication or has a health condition.

Frequently Asked Questions: Australian Shepherd Anxiety

Do Australian Shepherds get separation anxiety?
Some do, but we have not found reliable evidence that Australian Shepherds as a breed are more prone to it than other dogs. Separation anxiety signs, such as barking or howling, chewing at doors and windows, accidents, pacing and escape attempts, happen when your dog is alone, not when you are home. Record a short absence, build alone time gradually, and call your vet early if your dog stops eating or seems unwell.
Why is my Australian Shepherd so clingy?
Many Aussies are affectionate and like to stay close to their people, so following you around is often normal. It is a concern if your dog panics when you leave, cannot settle when you are home, or becomes clingy suddenly. A sudden change is a reason to see your vet.
Why does my Australian Shepherd herd children or other pets?
Australian Shepherds are herding dogs, so circling, staring or nipping at moving children or pets is usually herding behavior, not separation anxiety. Supervise children, give your dog daily exercise and training, and get help from a qualified positive-reinforcement trainer if your dog nips.
How much exercise does an Australian Shepherd need?
The AKC says most Aussies require vigorous daily exercise and thrive on mental as well as physical exercise. There is no single number of minutes for every dog, so ask your vet about the right amount for your dog's age and health. Exercise helps, but it does not cure separation anxiety on its own.
What size ThunderShirt does an Australian Shepherd need?
It depends on your dog's chest girth, not the breed. Measure around the widest part of the chest behind the front legs and check the manufacturer's size chart. A ThunderShirt is optional, evidence for it is mixed, and it does not replace alone-time training.
When should I take my anxious Australian Shepherd to the vet?
Go early if your dog stops eating, seems unwell, suddenly changes behavior, starts having accidents, or hurts themselves trying to escape. The ASPCA advises ruling out medical problems before behavior work, and only giving medication for a behavior problem after talking to your vet or a veterinary behaviorist.
