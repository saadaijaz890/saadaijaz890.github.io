How to Prepare for a Vet or Behaviorist Visit About Separation Anxiety

Prepare for a separation anxiety vet visit with a clear timeline, short video clips, a daily log, and useful questions about your dog's care plan.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 24 Oct 202611 min read
Quick answer

Bring a clear behavior timeline, an honest absence log, timestamped video highlights, and a complete list of foods, products, supplements, and medications. Start with your veterinarian to investigate medical causes and assess whether the behavior is separation-related; do not delay care to collect perfect records.

  • Record what happens, when it starts, and what changes between absences.
  • Bring short video highlights and keep the original footage available.
  • Call your vet promptly about injuries, severe distress, or sudden changes.

It can be difficult to explain what happens when you leave, especially when you only see the aftermath. A few organized observations can help your veterinarian understand your dog's experience without relying on memory or labels such as “clingy” or “destructive.”

We are dog owners, not vets or certified trainers. This guide helps you prepare for an appointment about separation anxiety; the veterinary team evaluates health, interprets the history, and decides what care is appropriate.

Why preparing for the visit matters

The first task is to investigate medical contributors and determine whether the behavior is actually separation-related. Barking, accidents, appetite changes, and destruction can have several explanations.

A clear timeline and timestamped video often reveal more than a general description. “Paced between the door and couch three minutes after departure” is more useful than “acted anxious all morning.”

AVSAB's advice on how to tell if your dog has separation anxiety emphasizes video of behavior during absences, including ordinary and off-routine departures. That footage helps professionals understand what happens before obvious barking or damage.

Preparation also makes room for your practical concerns: work schedules, caregiver availability, neighbor complaints, and what your household can realistically manage.

Who to book: vet, veterinary behaviorist, or trainer

Start with your general veterinarian, particularly if the behavior is new or accompanied by physical changes. They can examine your dog, review health history, and decide whether further investigation or referral is needed.

A veterinary behaviorist is a veterinarian with advanced specialist training in behavior. In the US, a board-certified veterinary behaviorist can address the medical and behavioral aspects of a complicated case.

A qualified, reward-based behavior professional or separation anxiety trainer can help implement and adjust training. They do not replace veterinary medical evaluation or make prescribing decisions.

Ask about qualifications, specific separation anxiety experience, methods, and collaboration with your vet. The word “behaviorist” alone does not establish veterinary credentials.

When booking, say the appointment concerns behavior during absences. Ask whether a longer appointment, questionnaire, or advance video submission is preferred.

What to record and why

Collect observations for one to two weeks if your dog is safe and the appointment timing allows. Missing days stay blank; do not reconstruct details you cannot remember.

How to film your dog when you leave

Place the camera where it can show your dog's usual resting spot and the exit they approach. Include sound if possible. Secure equipment and cables out of reach.

Start recording before departure so the professional can see preparation and the transition to being alone. Keep at least the first 30 minutes when available (AVSAB suggests the first 30 to 60), along with later events that matter.

That is a recording goal, not an instruction to leave a distressed dog for 30 minutes. Do not create or prolong panic for footage. Use existing recordings or ask the clinic how to document safely.

Note timestamps of key changes, then trim short highlights while preserving the originals. Include context before the behavior rather than only its most dramatic moment.

Ask how files should be sent and check that links open. Our Best Pet Camera for Separation Anxiety guide explains recording options and limitations.

Build a simple timeline

Start with when you first noticed the behavior. Distinguish a confirmed date from an estimate: “first recorded in June” is different from “may have started earlier.”

Add relevant changes:

  • Adoption, moving, or a new household schedule.
  • A new pet, a household member leaving, or a loss.
  • Illness, injury, or changes in sleep and mobility.
  • A frightening event during an absence.
  • Changes in confinement or caregiver arrangements.

Record periods when your dog seemed comfortable too. Note whether distress happens with every departure or only particular circumstances.

These events provide context. Their timing does not prove that one caused the behavior.

List what you've already tried

Include training, care arrangements, confinement changes, enrichment, background sound, and calming products. Record when each began, how it was used, and what you actually observed.

Replace “nothing worked” with specifics: “A food toy was offered on three recorded departures; eating stopped when the door closed.”

Be honest about methods that frightened your dog or advice you now question. The appointment is an opportunity to improve the plan, not a test of your ownership.

Also describe what has helped, even temporarily, such as a familiar sitter or a different room.

Food, supplements and medications

List everything your dog currently receives and relevant products used previously:

  • Main food, treats, chews, and enrichment fillings.
  • Supplements, calming products, and topical products.
  • Prescription and nonprescription medications.
  • Flea, tick, and other preventive products.

Bring packaging or clear photographs. Copy names, ingredient information, strengths, and amounts already given exactly as recorded on the packaging or your instructions. Note frequency, start dates, and observed effects.

Do not guess an unknown amount; mark it unknown. Do not start something new for the appointment or change existing treatment without veterinary direction. The vet decides what is appropriate.

Keep actual observations separate from reports: “neighbor reported barking” is useful, but is not the same as video-confirmed continuous barking.

Do not delay an appointment because the log covers fewer than two weeks.

What to bring on the day

Bring your completed intake form, a short timeline, the checklist, log, product list, and selected clips. Keep originals accessible and have a backup if internet access fails.

Include previous veterinary records if the clinic does not already have them. Ask whether the veterinarian wants any samples or additional records rather than assuming.

Write down your main priorities, such as preventing injury, arranging workday care, or understanding the first training step.

If your dog is stressed at the clinic itself, contact the team beforehand and read our Vet Visit Anxiety guide. That is a separate issue from preparing evidence about behavior at home.

Questions to ask at the appointment

  1. What medical problems could contribute to these signs, and what evaluation is appropriate?
  2. What does the history suggest, and what remains uncertain?
  3. What should we do immediately to keep our dog safe?
  4. Which absence or confinement arrangements should we avoid for now?
  5. What information or footage would help you assess the pattern further?
  6. What should an initial training plan include for this dog?
  7. Would referral to a veterinary behaviorist or qualified separation anxiety professional help?
  8. How should we handle necessary workday absences while care is being arranged?
  9. What changes should we monitor, and when should we contact you sooner?
  10. When should follow-up happen, and who should receive updates?

If treatment options are discussed, ask the vet to explain their purpose, monitoring requirements, and what to do if concerns arise. Do not leave unclear instructions unresolved.

After the visit

Ask for a written summary of the assessment, immediate management steps, and follow-up arrangements. Confirm which professional is responsible for each part of the plan.

Keep using the same log so changes are easier to compare. Record what was implemented and when, including any difficulties following the plan.

AAHA's Separation Anxiety in Pets page emphasizes individualized professional support and notes that video can help the veterinary team understand behavior during absences. Follow the plan made for your dog rather than combining several conflicting programs.

Our Separation Anxiety Training Plan can help you understand gradual practice, while How Long Can a Dog Be Left Alone? covers care planning. Neither replaces your dog's individualized instructions.

When not to wait

Call your vet now about self-injury, dangerous escape attempts, refusal to eat or drink, or sudden behavioral change. Explain whether appetite and drinking are affected only during absences or also while you are home.

Active injury, collapse, breathing difficulty, or suspected poisoning requires urgent veterinary attention. Do not continue filming instead of helping.

If confinement triggers frantic escape behavior, stop using that arrangement and ask for a safer interim plan. You do not need a completed checklist before requesting care.

Bottom line

Bring an honest timeline, representative video, a simple log, and a complete health and product history. Clear observations help the professional ask better questions and plan appropriate care.

Preparation does not need to be perfect. Start with what you have, keep your dog safe, and let the veterinary team guide the next steps.

Frequently Asked Questions: Preparing for the Visit

Should I see a vet or a trainer first for separation anxiety?
Start with your veterinarian, especially when behavior is new, severe, or accompanied by physical changes. A qualified, reward-based trainer can help implement behavior work, while a veterinary behaviorist offers specialist medical and behavioral assessment. These professionals may work together.
How much video should I bring?
Ask the clinic what it prefers, then provide a few short, representative highlights with timestamps and keep the originals available. Preserve the first 30 minutes of an ordinary absence when safely available, plus relevant later events. Never prolong distress to obtain a particular recording length.
What if I can't film my dog?
Bring a written timeline, an honest log, and clearly identified reports from neighbors or caregivers. An old phone or tablet may offer a practical recording option, but video is not a condition for requesting help. Ask the clinic what alternatives it recommends.
Should I stop training before the appointment?
Do not continue exercises that provoke panic, escape attempts, or injury. Ask the clinic whether to continue your existing comfortable practice while waiting, and describe exactly what you have been doing. Avoid starting several new methods that make the history harder to interpret.
Will the vet prescribe medication?
That depends on the veterinary assessment and your dog's individual needs; a prescription is not automatic. Bring an accurate list of everything already given and let the vet explain appropriate options. Do not start, stop, or change medication without veterinary direction.
