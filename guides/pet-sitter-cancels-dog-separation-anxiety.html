When Your Pet Sitter Cancels Over the Holidays: A Backup Plan for a Dog With Separation Anxiety

When your holiday pet sitter cancels, use a ranked backup list, a clear handover sheet and practical plan changes to keep your anxious dog supported.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 31 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Confirm exactly which care hours are uncovered, then call familiar backups in order. If suitable care is unavailable, change the outing or travel plan rather than extending your dog's alone time beyond what they can comfortably manage.

  • Confirm replacement care before leaving, including overnight coverage where needed.
  • Give every stand-in clear instructions and a backup contact.
  • A camera or brief visit cannot replace needed company.

A cancellation message can turn holiday plans upside down, especially when your dog cannot comfortably wait alone. You may be balancing travel deadlines, family expectations and the sudden need to find someone your dog trusts.

We are dog owners who researched this topic, not vets, trainers or product testers, and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. This guide focuses on the immediate problem: covering the missing care, choosing a suitable stand-in and changing plans when a safe replacement is not available.

Why a sitter cancellation is hard on a dog with separation anxiety

The missing booking creates more than a scheduling gap. Your dog may face an absence they have never managed, an unfamiliar caregiver or a new care setting at short notice.

Holiday noise, visitors and changing routines can add difficulty; our Christmas and New Year's plan covers those. A person being available does not automatically mean your dog will feel comfortable with them.

The ASPCA's page on separation anxiety describes separation anxiety as distress when dogs are left alone or separated from the people they're attached to, and explains that treatment focuses on gradually teaching the dog to be comfortable alone.

A cancellation cannot compress that learning into an afternoon. Protect the level your dog currently manages while you arrange help. The immediate goal is dependable care, not a last-minute test of independence.

The first hour after a cancellation

First, clarify what has changed. Is the whole booking canceled, one visit missing or the final overnight uncovered? Ask what the caregiver can still do without pressuring someone who is ill or unable to work.

If they suggest a replacement, interview that person yourself. A referral is a starting point, not confirmation that the arrangement suits your dog.

Next, write down the uncovered hours, including travel time and possible delays. Note whether your dog needs continuous company, frequent visits or a particular familiar person.

Call your ranked backups with a specific request: “Can you stay here from departure until the evening handover?” is clearer than “Could you help with the dog?”

Confirm arrival time, access and what happens if the replacement cannot stay longer. Keep your dog with a responsible person while you make calls.

Finally, choose according to your dog's needs. If coverage remains uncertain, postpone departure or change the event plan. Check the current terms of any affected booking rather than assuming a refund, replacement or extension is available.

Build a ranked backup list before the holidays

Rank options by your dog's comfort, the coverage required and practical availability.

Travel for work regularly? Our business trip guide shows how to set up one regular carer plus an introduced backup, so a cancellation before a business trip is easier to cover.

Start with family or friends your dog already knows. Record who can stay overnight, who can cover an afternoon and who is only available for emergencies.

Next, identify a second sitter and arrange an introduction before holiday bookings become urgent. Ask whether backup availability requires a separate agreement; do not treat a name in your phone as a confirmed booking.

Keep suitable daycare, boarding and a veterinary clinic that offers boarding on the list. Each needs checking for your individual dog, including staffing and time spent alone.

A walker may cover a missed potty break for a dog who otherwise rests comfortably. They are not a substitute for continuous company when your dog panics between visits. Our daycare vs dog walker guide compares the two.

Put your own fallback beside the care options: one adult stays, departure is delayed or the trip is shortened. Everyone involved should know when that fallback becomes necessary. Our care options guide compares sitters, daycare, boarding and family care in more depth.

Booking boarding or daycare at short notice

Ask whether the facility can meet your dog's needs, not simply whether a space exists.

Explain the distress honestly. Ask who is present during the day and overnight, how much time dogs spend alone and what staff do if a dog cannot settle.

Check vaccination and health requirements, and have records ready. Ask about rest areas, group activity, escape prevention, updates and the procedure for veterinary concerns.

A veterinary clinic that boards may be an option, but do not assume it provides continuous company or a quieter environment. Confirm the actual arrangement.

Arrange a short visit or trial before committing whenever possible. If there is no opportunity to assess a new setting and your dog has severe distress, changing your plans may be the better choice.

Use our boarding guide for a fuller assessment. The holiday deadline does not make every available space suitable.

Changing your plans so your dog isn't left alone

Consider changes in order of what is practical for your household. Could you leave later, return earlier or attend only part of the event?

Two adults may travel separately so one remains with the dog until care begins. For a local gathering, take turns attending.

Hosting instead of visiting can work if your dog copes with guests and has a quiet place with company. It is not a useful swap if the gathering itself overwhelms them.

Bringing your dog may work when the entire destination and journey are suitable. Confirm permission and supervision arrangements first. Do not rely on leaving the dog alone in unfamiliar lodging.

Never use an unattended car, outdoor space, a full day alone or an unsupported overnight as the fallback.

Stand-in handover sheet

Give the stand-in one clear sheet and talk through the essentials. Our pet sitter preparation guide covers fuller introductions.

  • Dog's name, description and current photo.
  • Your contact number and expected return.
  • Backup family contact if you cannot be reached.

  • Emergency veterinary contact and location.
  • Any clinic-required authorization arrangements.
  • Feeding instructions and food location.
  • Food sensitivities and foods to avoid.
  • Fresh-water routine and bowl locations.
  • Potty schedule and usual signals.
  • Walk preferences and suitable routes.
  • Leash, harness and waste-bag locations.
  • Current comfortable alone-time limit and how it was observed.
  • Whether continuous company is needed.
  • Early distress signs and known triggers.
  • What helps the dog settle.
  • Handling, visitors or barriers the dog finds difficult.
  • Resting-area setup and door-security instructions.
  • Safe toy instructions and items to remove.
  • Medical records and care-supply locations.
  • Camera location, consent and update arrangements.
  • What requires an immediate call or veterinary advice.
  • Exact arrival, departure and next handover times.
  • Backup plan if the stand-in becomes unavailable.

Keep keys and entry instructions secure. Ask the person to repeat the coverage times and emergency plan so misunderstandings surface before you leave.

If nobody is available

There is no device that turns missing care into a supported absence. If nobody suitable can help, keep absences within what your dog already manages and change your own plans.

Someone stopping by may cover a short gap for a dog who is comfortable between visits. It is not enough for a dog who becomes distressed as soon as the person leaves.

Use a camera to check a manageable absence only when you or another person can respond promptly. Do not watch escalating panic remotely while continuing an event.

If you are already away, coordinate a safe handover before the current caregiver leaves. Ask a traveling household member to return when necessary and revise onward plans.

Avoid experimenting with longer absences during the crisis. Once stable care is restored, return to comfortable practice and review the backup list.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

Contact your vet early if your dog refuses food, seems unwell or shows a sudden change. Strong distress deserves veterinary attention rather than being treated as a scheduling inconvenience.

Injury, repeated escape attempts or panic that does not settle need prompt help. Serious injury, collapse or breathing difficulty require emergency veterinary care. Our separation anxiety red flags guide explains which signs are urgent.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

House-soiling only during absences should be discussed with the vet alongside potty breaks and recordings of your dog alone. Do not assume every accident is behavioral.

A qualified reward-based behavior professional can help when there is no progress or your dog cannot settle with alternative caregivers. Never punish barking, damage or accidents, and never shut a panicking dog in a crate or room and leave.

Professional support can also help you build more workable care options before the next holiday.

Frequently Asked Questions: When Your Pet Sitter Cancels

What do I do if my dog sitter cancels at the last minute?
Confirm the exact uncovered hours and call familiar backups with a specific request. Check replacement caregivers yourself and confirm access and handover times. If suitable coverage is unavailable, delay departure or change your plans.
Can I leave my dog with separation anxiety alone if my sitter cancels?
Only consider an absence already comfortably within your dog's current ability. A cancellation does not make a longer gap manageable, and a camera cannot replace company. Do not use a full day or overnight alone as the fallback.
How do I find a last-minute dog sitter over Thanksgiving or Christmas?
Start with a second sitter met beforehand, trusted family or friends, and referrals you can personally check. Ask about anxious-dog experience and the exact hours they can stay. Arrange an introduction where possible and check the current terms of any booking.
Is boarding OK for a dog with separation anxiety at short notice?
It may suit some dogs, particularly somewhere they already relax, but availability alone is not enough. Ask about overnight staffing, time alone and responses to distress, and trial the setting if possible. Use another arrangement if the facility cannot meet your dog's needs.
How do I make a backup plan so this doesn't happen again?
You cannot prevent every cancellation, but you can reduce the care gap. Meet a second caregiver, keep records and a handover sheet ready, and confirm backups before major holidays. Include a household fallback for changing travel when nobody suitable is available.
