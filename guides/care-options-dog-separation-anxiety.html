Who Can Watch My Dog? Care Cover Options for a Dog With Separation Anxiety

Arrange care for a dog with separation anxiety while you work. Compare sitters, family help, daycare, and swaps, then build a reliable weekly plan.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 24 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Arrange a trusted person or suitable care setting for absences longer than your dog currently handles comfortably. Trial each arrangement first: some dogs relax with any familiar person, while others struggle without a particular person; walkers, cameras, and food toys do not replace needed company.

  • Care cover supports training; needing help does not mean you have failed.
  • Check that your dog actually relaxes with the chosen caregiver.
  • Plan handovers, commuting time, and backup care before the week begins.

When your dog cannot comfortably stay alone, ordinary workdays and errands become complicated. Care cover gives you a practical way to meet those responsibilities while avoiding absences that overwhelm your dog.

We are dog owners, not vets or trainers, and we have not tested these services ourselves. This guide helps you compare arrangements and organize a week of support; your dog's comfort, safety, and professional care plan should guide the choice.

Your care cover options

Family or friends

A familiar person may be the simplest starting point, especially if your dog already rests comfortably with them.

Agree on actual coverage hours and whether they can stay throughout. Someone working remotely at your home may be helpful if the arrangement suits both of them.

A trusted neighbor

A nearby neighbor may cover a meeting, appointment, or handover gap. Familiarity and short travel time can make this practical.

Be clear whether you need company throughout or a brief visit. Do not assume proximity means they can respond whenever needed.

A professional sitter at your home

An in-home sitter preserves familiar surroundings and can provide extended company. Ask specifically for continuous coverage if your dog cannot tolerate gaps.

“House sitting” does not always mean the person remains home all day. Confirm other bookings, errands, and breaks before booking.

Drop-in visits or a dog walker

A walker helps with toileting, movement, and a break in the day. Drop-ins may suit dogs comfortable with the intervals between visits.

They do not cover long stretches of separation distress. A lunchtime walk cannot prevent panic that begins shortly after your morning departure.

Doggy daycare

Daycare can suit social dogs who enjoy the environment, accept staff, and rest there.

It may overwhelm noise-sensitive, fearful, or dog-selective dogs. Ask about quiet rest, staffing, group management, and any time spent alone in kennels or rooms.

Working from home or bringing your dog to work

Temporary remote work, a split schedule, or an approved dog-friendly workplace can reduce uncovered time.

Check whether your dog can settle during calls, meetings, and breaks. Office permission alone does not establish that the setting is comfortable.

Dog-friendly errands

Combine errands, use delivery or pickup, or choose places where your dog can accompany you safely and legally.

Car travel should suit your dog, with appropriate restraint and comfortable conditions. Plan to keep your dog with you or a trusted companion; never leave them in a hot car. A parked car is not dependable care cover.

Swapping care with another owner

A reciprocal arrangement can provide affordable company if both homes and dogs are suitable.

Dogs do not need to share space if that causes tension. Agree on handling, food separation, emergencies, and what happens when either owner is unavailable.

How to choose for your dog

Start with the least disruptive arrangement your dog finds comfortable. A familiar home and familiar person may be easier than a busy new facility.

Consider people preferences, other animals, noise sensitivity, travel tolerance, toilet needs, mobility, and handling. Ask whether the caregiver can follow your dog's routine without forcing interaction or confinement.

Watch for relaxed resting, ordinary movement, and the ability to disengage from the door. Quietness alone can hide tension; eating briefly does not establish comfort throughout the stay.

Questions to ask a sitter or daycare

  • Have you cared for dogs who become distressed when their owner leaves?
  • What reward-based handling methods do you use?
  • Can you provide the continuous coverage we need?
  • When might my dog be alone, including breaks or other bookings?
  • Who else will handle or supervise my dog?
  • How do you recognize distress and respond to it?
  • Can we arrange gradual introductions and a trial?
  • What happens if my dog cannot settle?
  • Are crates, kennels, or group rest areas used?
  • Can my dog have quiet rest away from other dogs?
  • How do you prevent escapes at doors and handovers?
  • What is your emergency and backup staffing plan?
  • What health requirements, references, insurance, and service terms apply?
  • How will we agree on updates and camera privacy?

Trial run

  1. Meet while you remain present. Choose a quiet setting and let your dog approach voluntarily. The caregiver should avoid looming, reaching, or forcing affection.
  2. Rehearse ordinary care. Try familiar activities such as sitting together, offering water, or a gentle walk if appropriate.
  3. Check the environment. Review doors, gates, other pets, resting space, and anything your dog could swallow or damage.
  4. Try a very brief separation only if comfortable. Keep it within what your dog appears able to manage and remain available to return.
  5. Observe the response. Use an agreed camera view or a short caregiver video. Watch settling and recovery, not merely whether barking stops.
  6. Adjust before extending. If your dog remains vigilant or distressed, return and seek help planning an easier introduction.
  7. Confirm a realistic booking. Gradually trial the actual care arrangement before relying on it for a full workday. Agree on when the caregiver should contact you.

Do not turn the trial into a test of how much distress your dog can endure.

Your backup plan

Choose a primary backup and another option where possible. Introduce them before a cancellation becomes urgent.

Keep contact details, access instructions, the care sheet, and veterinary numbers together. Decide who can respond if you are delayed and how arrival will be confirmed.

Ask professional providers what happens during illness or staffing shortages. A replacement person still needs to be suitable for your dog.

Review the plan before holidays, travel, or changes in work hours. A camera alert is useful only when someone can act on it.

Budget-friendly ideas

Options range from free to costly, and a mixed schedule may be more sustainable than one paid service every day.

Ask a trusted remote-working friend about a planned care swap, share coverage across household schedules, or combine family help with paid blocks where available. Offer clear hours rather than open-ended requests.

Consolidate errands, use delivery when practical, and discuss temporary workplace flexibility. Reciprocal arrangements should remain fair and dependable.

Do not choose an unsuitable setting solely because it is cheaper. Tell your vet or behavior professional about budget constraints so the plan reflects real resources.

When care cover is not enough

Company helps only if your dog actually becomes comfortable. Persistent pacing, drooling, escape attempts, or inability to settle with the caregiver calls for reassessment.

Start with your vet, particularly for sudden changes or physical symptoms. A qualified reward-based behavior professional or veterinary behaviorist can help tailor introductions and training.

Use our Separation Anxiety Red Flags guide for safety concerns. Injury, dangerous escape behavior, or serious illness signs should not wait for another trial.

Care cover manages absences while the broader plan develops. It does not replace assessment or mean training is no longer needed.

Bottom line

Choose care your dog can comfortably accept, trial it gradually, and cover the whole absence—including travel and handovers.

A dependable weekly schedule and backup arrangement make training more manageable. Needing that support is a practical response to your dog's current needs.

Frequently Asked Questions: Care Cover for Dogs With Separation Anxiety

What do I do with a dog with separation anxiety while I work?
Arrange suitable company through a familiar person, an in-home sitter, a trialed daycare, or a workable household schedule. Match coverage to the time your dog cannot comfortably manage alone, including commuting. Confirm the arrangement helps your dog settle rather than assuming any company is enough.
Is doggy daycare good for separation anxiety?
It can provide useful cover for dogs who enjoy the setting and relax with staff. Other dogs find group activity, noise, or confinement overwhelming. Trial it gradually and ask about rest periods, supervision, and any time spent alone.
Does a dog walker help?
A walker can meet toilet and exercise needs and provide a daytime break. It does not prevent distress during the remaining hours if your dog cannot cope alone. Some dogs need continuous care rather than a single midday visit.
Can I leave my dog with another dog for company?
Another dog does not reliably replace human company, particularly when distress relates to a specific person's absence. Observe whether the arrangement genuinely helps and ensure both dogs are safe together. Do not acquire another dog as an assumed solution.
How long until my dog can be alone again?
There is no reliable date that applies to every dog. Progress depends on individual needs, health, training, and how well absences are managed. Review comfortable behavior with your professional before reducing care coverage, rather than working toward a calendar deadline.
