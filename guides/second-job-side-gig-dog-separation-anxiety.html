Working a Second Job or Side Gig With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety

Plan a two-job week around your anxious dog, with care for the second shift, useful breaks at home, flexible backup and protected rest days for you both.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 1 Nov 20269 min read
Quick answer

Plan your dog's care across the entire day, including both jobs and commuting. A brief stop at home does not reset their tolerance for being alone, so arrange familiar company for unsupported gaps and confirm care before adding or extending a gig shift.

  • Count every absence across both jobs and all commuting.
  • Use the home stop for care and calm time.
  • Confirm second-shift company and backup before heading out.

Working two jobs can leave a narrow window between arriving home and heading out again. When your dog struggles alone, that short stop needs to provide more than a quick greeting and a refill of the water bowl.

We are dog owners who researched this topic, not vets, trainers or product testers, and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. This guide focuses on building a workable two-job week, including evening cover, changing gig hours and rest for both you and your dog.

Why a two-job week is hard on an anxious dog

Your dog may face a daytime absence followed by another departure during the hours they normally spend with you. Walks, meals and bedtime can shift, while your own energy is already stretched.

The ASPCA's page on separation anxiety describes separation anxiety as distress when dogs are left alone or separated from the people they're attached to, and explains that treatment focuses on gradually teaching the dog to be comfortable alone.

A second shift cannot take the place of that gradual practice. Your dog may need company while you work, even if you are building alone-time skills separately.

Changing gig hours add uncertainty. A flexible weekly care plan is more useful than repeatedly hoping your dog will manage one more evening.

Count the whole day, not each job

Write down when you leave, when you return between jobs and when you finally get home. Include commuting, preparation away from home and likely delays.

Then mark who is with your dog during each part. Track both the length of each unsupported absence and the total time without company across the day. There is no universal total that makes the schedule suitable for every dog.

A brief visit home does not create a fresh allowance for another long absence. Your dog still needs walks, potty breaks, company, rest and a comfortable response to each departure.

Use recent video to establish what they handle now. Record a typical two-job day with your helper's knowledge, including the second departure and any gap after a visit. Look for relaxed resting, not just silence.

Pacing, barking, howling, panting, drooling or scratching at exits mean the arrangement needs review. Our how long can a dog be left alone guide explains the wider care needs behind the question, and our working full-time guide covers care for the day job itself.

Make the stop at home count

Plan the break before the day begins. Have walking equipment and food ready so the entire visit is not spent searching for supplies.

Give your dog a potty opportunity and a comfortable sniff walk if time allows. Keep meals near their usual time, then leave space for quiet company and settling before the next departure.

Avoid trying to exhaust your dog to make the second absence work. Exercise meets important needs, but does not treat panic.

If the break is too short to provide proper care, arrange for a helper to cover some tasks before you arrive. That can leave you time to sit with your dog rather than rushing through everything.

Have the second-shift helper arrive before you leave when company is needed. A direct handover avoids an unsupported gap between your departure and their visit.

Care for the second shift

Book evening help as deliberately as daytime care. A trusted family member, neighbor or sitter may be able to provide quiet company at home.

Trial the arrangement first. Start with time together while you are present, followed by a comfortable short departure. Check whether your dog settles after you leave rather than assuming a friendly greeting proves they will.

A walker can provide exercise and a potty break, but leaves gaps before and after the walk. If your dog panics during those gaps, arrange longer company.

Daycare may suit a dog who enjoys the setting, provided collection is covered before your second shift ends. A familiar person could collect the dog and stay until you return.

Our daycare vs. dog walker guide compares these choices. Whatever you choose, never bring your dog along in the car while driving for a gig, or use the car or outside as fallback care.

When gig hours change every week

Keep one weekly calendar showing both jobs, travel, home stops and confirmed care. Update it whenever a shift changes. If one of your jobs already rotates, our shift work guide covers planning around a changing roster.

Distinguish a possible booking from an agreed one. Confirm the helper's arrival, departure and ability to extend before relying on the arrangement.

Before adding a gig shift, check the full day again. An extra evening may affect meals, the next morning's care and your own rest. Check your own work rules when considering changes.

Keep a backup person who knows your dog and can enter your home. Agree who acts if you cannot answer your phone; the dog should not wait while everyone tries repeatedly to reach you.

If no suitable cover is available, shorten, move or decline the additional outing where possible. A camera cannot fill a care gap.

Protect rest days for both of you

A day off does not need to become a catch-up marathon of exercise, errands and training. Make room for ordinary walks, sniffing, comfortable company and sleep.

Is your extra commitment unpaid volunteering rather than a second job? Our volunteering shifts guide covers weekly or monthly shifts and a calm return.

Keep meals and potty opportunities familiar. If you need another long outing on a rest day, arrange care rather than assuming your dog has recovered enough to manage it.

Continue tiny, comfortable training sessions when you have the energy to observe and respond. Do not use a free afternoon to push toward the length of a second shift.

If distress appears, return calmly or have the helper respond, and make the next practice easier. Keep real work absences covered while skills develop. Our separation anxiety training plan provides the fuller process.

Your own rest belongs in the plan too. Asking someone else to handle a walk or an evening of company can make a demanding week more sustainable.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

Contact your vet early if your dog refuses food, seems unwell or shows a sudden change in behavior. Do not assume a difficult workweek explains every change.

Strong panic, injury, escape attempts or potty accidents only when alone need veterinary discussion. Share short clips and notes showing the timing of both departures and the care provided.

A qualified reward-based behavior professional can help if your dog struggles despite support or gradual practice is not progressing. If the dog remains distressed with a helper present, review that arrangement too. Our separation anxiety red flags guide explains which signs are urgent.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

Never punish barking, howling, damage or accidents; they are signs of distress, not spite. Never shut a panicking dog in a crate or room and leave. Increase support while you work out a better plan.

Bottom line

Plan the whole two-job day rather than treating each shift separately. Use the home stop for meaningful care, arrange familiar evening company and confirm a backup before hours change.

Protect rest days and let training follow your dog's comfort. Dependable support gives both of you more room to manage a demanding week.

Frequently Asked Questions: Two Jobs With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety

Can I work two jobs if my dog has separation anxiety?
It may be workable with dependable care covering the absences your dog cannot comfortably handle. Assess the whole day, including travel and evening departures. Trial helpers and arrange backup before relying on a two-job schedule.
How long can my anxious dog be alone across two shifts?
Use recent observations of comfortable alone time rather than a general hourly allowance. Consider each absence, total time without company and your dog's daily care needs. A short stop at home does not automatically make another long absence manageable. Contact your vet early if your dog refuses food or seems unwell.
What should I do in the short break between jobs?
Provide a potty opportunity, a comfortable walk or sniff activity, and calm company. Keep meals familiar and avoid rushing straight into another departure. If the break is too short, ask a helper to handle some tasks beforehand.
Should I hire a dog walker for my second shift?
A walker can help if your dog comfortably manages the gaps around the visit. If your dog panics alone, a walk is not enough on its own; arrange someone to stay for the needed period. Trial the arrangement and review video.
What if my gig hours change every week?
Update one shared care calendar whenever your hours change and confirm each booking. Keep a familiar backup with access and instructions for delays. If suitable care is unavailable, revise the extra shift where possible rather than extending unsupported alone time.
