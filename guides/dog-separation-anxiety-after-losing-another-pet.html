Dog Separation Anxiety After Losing Another Pet or Person

Help your dog after losing a companion with steady routines, gentle alone-time practice and care cover. Learn which changes need veterinary attention.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 26 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

After losing a companion, some dogs struggle with newly unfamiliar alone time. Keep routines steady, check health changes with your vet and rebuild comfortable absences gradually, using care cover for longer gaps; do not assume another dog will resolve the distress.

  • Changes after loss can reflect adjustment, illness or difficulty being alone.
  • Start from what your dog manages now, not their previous longest absence.
  • Ask for practical help; you are adjusting to this loss too.

After a pet or family member dies or moves away, the home can feel different in countless small ways. Your dog may wait by a familiar door, sleep elsewhere or stay closer to you. You may be grieving too, with less energy for routines that once felt easy.

You do not need to interpret every behavior or solve everything immediately. We are dog owners, not vets or trainers. This guide offers practical ways to check your dog’s health, maintain familiar care and gently rebuild alone time without claiming to know what dogs understand about death.

What changes you may see after a loss

Some dogs appear quieter, sleep more, eat less, search familiar places or follow the remaining people. Others show little obvious change.

Losing a person from the household can unsettle a dog too. Our guide to dog separation anxiety after a breakup or shared custody covers the first week after someone moves out and how to keep two homes consistent.

Many begin settling over days to weeks as a new routine forms, but there is no timetable that applies to every dog. Changes that persist, worsen or affect health need attention.

Notice what still feels comfortable: an ordinary walk, quiet company or resting in a familiar spot. Offer those opportunities without forcing play, greetings or affection.

When to call the vet

Do not attribute every change to loss. The ASPCA’s separation anxiety advice recommends ruling out medical issues first.

Contact your vet about reduced appetite, weight loss, vomiting, unusual lethargy or sudden behavior changes. If your dog has eaten nothing for about a day, call rather than waiting another day; seek advice earlier for puppies, medically vulnerable dogs or other symptoms.

Older dogs deserve particular care. Our senior dog separation anxiety guide explains why new alone-time difficulties should include a health assessment.

Why alone time can suddenly be harder

Your dog may previously have had company during every human absence. Losing a companion dog or cat can turn familiar departures into the first experiences of being completely alone.

If a person has died or moved away, caregiving, schedules and household activity may change too. The ASPCA identifies the sudden absence of a resident family member through death or moving away as a possible separation anxiety trigger.

Some dogs relax with another trusted person; others struggle without someone specific. Our separation anxiety vs isolation distress guide explains why that distinction matters for care.

What to film and note

Observe the first 10–20 minutes only if your dog can remain comfortable that long. This is not a required test duration. Return earlier if distress appears, and avoid recreating panic simply to document it.

Dog Separation Anxiety After Losing Another Pet or Person: what to film and note

  • Start recording before departure preparations.
  • Include the exit and resting area.
  • Capture the whole body and sound.
  • Note who was present before departure.
  • Record the time you left.
  • Mark the first sign of worry.
  • Note searching, pacing or exit checking.
  • Record barking, howling or whining.
  • Note panting, trembling or drooling.
  • Record food interest without forcing eating.
  • Note comfortable rest, if any.
  • Record accidents or unsafe escape attempts.
  • Note appetite and behavior when you are home.
  • Record recent routine changes.

Keeping routines steady

Keep feeding, walks, potty opportunities and bedtime reasonably familiar. Predictability can come from a simple sequence rather than exact timing: breakfast, a quiet walk, then rest.

Preserve kind contact without suddenly providing uninterrupted attention and then returning to long absences. You can comfort your dog while also gently rewarding voluntary independent rest.

Let another familiar person help with enjoyable care where possible. Introduce that change gradually rather than handing over every routine at once.

Steady routine checklist

Dog Separation Anxiety After Losing Another Pet or Person: steady routine checklist

  • Keep meals reasonably close to familiar times.
  • Record how much your dog actually eats.
  • Maintain suitable walks and sniffing opportunities.
  • Provide dependable potty breaks.
  • Protect quiet daytime rest.
  • Keep bedtime arrangements familiar where practical.
  • Offer ordinary affection without forcing contact.
  • Reward comfortable independent activities.
  • Introduce helpers gradually.
  • Avoid sudden long absences.
  • Write down necessary schedule changes.
  • Confirm who covers each caregiving task.

A calm set-up for alone time

Choose a familiar resting area with comfortable bedding, water and suitable room conditions. Avoid changing the entire environment at once unless safety requires it.

A blanket that smells of home may be comforting if your dog uses fabric safely. Remove items they shred or swallow.

Do not introduce confinement solely to stop pacing or damage. Never shut a panicking dog in a crate. A dog-proofed room or other familiar space is useful only when your dog remains comfortable there.

Trial any changes while someone is home.

Calm set-up checklist

Dog Separation Anxiety After Losing Another Pet or Person: calm set-up checklist

  • Use a resting area your dog already likes.
  • Provide familiar, safe bedding.
  • Keep drinking water accessible.
  • Check warmth, cooling and ventilation.
  • Remove cords and swallowable objects.
  • Secure trash and household chemicals.
  • Reduce avoidable window or hallway triggers.
  • Inspect any food toy for damage.
  • Keep pathways and exits safe.
  • Confirm comfort with any barrier.
  • Test the camera view.
  • Arrange a helper for gaps beyond current ability.

Start with seconds if necessary. “Below threshold” means keeping practice manageable enough that distress does not build.

Return before worry develops. If it appears, come back calmly and make the next attempt easier; do not wait for silence during panic.

Should you get another dog?

Another dog is not a quick fix. Company may be reassuring for some dogs, but it may not resolve distress about a missing person or being separated from you.

Consider another dog only when the family wants and can support that dog for their own sake. Allow time to assess compatibility, responsibilities and your readiness.

Our second dog guide covers testing canine company and considering alternatives without making adoption a treatment plan.

Looking after yourself too

A simple written care plan can reduce decisions when grief makes ordinary tasks difficult. Ask for specific help: a familiar friend covering an appointment, someone handling an evening walk or another family member organizing meals.

Keep training modest. You do not need to make every difficult day productive, and missing a session is not failure.

Use our separation anxiety log for brief factual notes rather than constant monitoring. You and your dog both need opportunities to rest.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not assume you know what your dog understands about the loss, or compare their adjustment with another dog’s.

Avoid dismissing appetite changes or lethargy as grief. Do not return immediately to the previous longest absence because your dog once managed it with a companion present.

Never punish accidents, damage or vocalizing. Avoid aversive collars, forced confinement and leaving your dog to cry until exhausted.

Do not rush another adoption or expect a food toy to make an overwhelming absence manageable.

When to get extra help

Dogs Trust’s separation anxiety advice explains that physical health can influence behavior and recommends veterinary advice when behavior has recently changed. That remains important even when the timing seems clearly connected to a loss.

Seek prompt help for self-injury, dangerous escape attempts or panic that begins almost immediately. Get a qualified, reward-based professional experienced in separation difficulties involved if your dog is worsening or not improving.

Bring short clips and notes using our vet visit preparation guide. You do not need to wait until you feel completely organized to ask.

Bottom line

After a loss, support starts with health checks, familiar care and manageable expectations. Rebuild alone time from your dog’s current comfort and arrange company for longer gaps. Small, steady steps and practical help can support both of you.

Frequently Asked Questions: Dog Separation Anxiety After Losing Another Pet or Person

Do dogs grieve when another dog dies?
Some dogs show changes we describe as grief-like, including searching, reduced activity or closer following. We cannot know exactly what they understand about death. Observe their needs and involve your vet when appetite, health or behavior changes concern you.
Can a dog get separation anxiety after another pet dies?
New separation distress can appear after losing a companion. A dog who previously always had animal company may now be experiencing complete solitude. Record brief, manageable absences and check health changes before deciding what support is needed.
How long does it take a dog to adjust after losing a companion?
Many begin settling over days to weeks, but there is no reliable deadline for an individual dog. Look for comfortable eating, rest and ordinary activities rather than counting days alone. Persistent distress or illness signs need help rather than more waiting.
Should I get another dog to help my grieving dog?
Another dog may offer company but may not address the underlying difficulty. Adopt only when your household is ready for the commitment and compatibility has been considered. Continue appropriate care and alone-time training either way.
How do I help my dog be alone after a loss?
Keep daily care steady, check concerning health changes and start with separations your dog currently handles comfortably. Increase gradually while using suitable company for longer gaps. Seek professional support if panic appears quickly, causes injury or does not improve.
