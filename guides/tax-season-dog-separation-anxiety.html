Tax Season and Busy-Season Overtime With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety

Prepare your anxious dog for busy-season overtime with early care bookings, manageable absences, steady evenings, restful weekends and backup help.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 1 Nov 202610 min read
Quick answer

Plan care before overtime begins, using your dog's current comfortable alone time rather than your work schedule to set the gaps. Book suitable company, trial the arrangement and keep backup cover for late returns; a midday walk may not be enough.

  • Book and trial care before the busiest weeks arrive.
  • Protect walks, meals and rest when evenings get tiring.
  • Review recordings and adjust support before distress becomes a repeated pattern.

A predictable busy season gives you something useful: advance notice. Whether your workload peaks during tax season or another annual rush, you can arrange dog care before late evenings become the norm.

We are dog owners who researched this topic, not vets, trainers or product testers, and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. This guide focuses on those temporary months of overtime: protecting comfortable absences, keeping daily care manageable and returning to ordinary hours without another abrupt change.

Why busy season is hard on an anxious dog

Longer workdays often mean longer absences, later meals and less energy for walks. What begins as an occasional late return can become the household routine. If your hours are long all year, our working full-time guide covers that steadier routine; this guide is about a temporary peak.

Your dog may cope with the usual departure but struggle when the expected return passes. Evening absences can also feel different from daytime ones.

The ASPCA's page on separation anxiety describes separation anxiety as distress when dogs are left alone or separated from the people they're attached to, and explains that treatment focuses on gradually teaching the dog to be comfortable alone.

A seasonal deadline does not increase that ability. Repeatedly extending absences because work is busy can leave your dog distressed while making the practical problem harder to manage.

The plan should cover the extra hours instead of expecting your dog to absorb them. That also reduces the number of urgent care decisions you must make when already tired.

Plan before the crunch

Begin about 4–6 weeks before your expected peak where possible. Review what your dog comfortably manages today, using recordings of easy absences rather than a past estimate.

Do not deliberately leave until panic starts to discover a limit. Start from a duration already known to be comfortable and observe settling.

Map the likely workweek, including commuting, evening commitments and possible weekend work. Identify every gap that needs care. If the change is a permanent return to the office, see our back-to-office guide; for night or rotating hours, our shift work guide fits better.

Contact familiar walkers, sitters, family helpers or suitable daycare early. Ask about recurring availability, late-return coverage and backup arrangements.

Trial care before the busiest period. Check how your dog responds when the helper arrives, during care and after they leave.

Ask your employer directly whether some home-working days, adjusted starts or other scheduling flexibility are possible. Do not build the care plan around flexibility that has not been confirmed.

Check the current terms of bookings, including changes and extensions.

Keep every absence within your dog's limit

Look at each continuous period alone, not just the total company provided across the day. Our guide to how long a dog can be left alone explains why there is no single safe number.

A midday visit can break up the schedule for a dog who rests comfortably before and afterward. It does not cover a dog who panics immediately after your departure or when the walker leaves.

For that dog, arrange longer sitter presence, a familiar person staying or suitable care elsewhere. A working-from-home family member may help if their actual availability meets the dog's needs.

If splitting the day between helpers, confirm handover times. “Someone will come later” is not enough when the first person's departure creates an unsupported gap.

Home-working days can reduce necessary absences, but keep comfortable short practice separate from focused work. Being home does not require ignoring your dog or keeping them behind a closed door.

Our daycare vs dog walker guide and care options guide compare arrangements in more detail. Choose according to the dog, not simply the name of the service.

Keep evenings and sleep steady

Create a minimum evening routine you can maintain on a demanding day: a potty break, the usual meal arrangement, suitable activity and quiet company.

If your return will be late, have the caregiver handle dinner and a walk rather than keeping your dog waiting for you. Share clear instructions so care remains consistent.

A sniffy walk or easy indoor food search can meet everyday needs without creating an intense late-night activity session. More exercise is not treatment for panic.

Keep the resting space comfortable and familiar. Avoid introducing a new sleeping location or confinement arrangement simply because you need uninterrupted rest.

Prepare food supplies and caregiver notes before the week starts. Ask for practical help with walks when necessary.

Your routine does not need to be elaborate. Predictable care is more useful than ambitious plans that disappear during the busiest evenings.

Use weekends as recovery days

Leave room for your dog—and yourself—to rest. After a demanding week, constant visitors, long outings and a full calendar may add more disruption.

Offer enjoyable walks and sniffing at a pace suited to your dog's health. Keep meals and bedtime recognizable without making the whole weekend a rigid schedule.

Continue a little comfortable alone-time practice when your dog is relaxed. Avoid switching between nonstop company all weekend and a difficult absence on Monday.

Do not use errands as an accidental endurance test. Arrange care if they exceed your dog's current comfort.

If you must work during the weekend, treat those hours as another care booking rather than assuming the usual helpers are available.

Recovery means meeting needs with less pressure, not trying to make up for the week through exhausting activity.

Record a typical day

Choose a representative day with the care plan already in place. Do not create a longer absence just to collect footage.

With everyone's consent, record the departure, the period before the helper arrives and what happens after they leave. Keep equipment and cords out of reach.

Look for comfortable resting and ordinary movement rather than judging only whether your dog barks. Repeated pacing, panting, drooling, exit scratching or inability to settle need attention.

A dog who eats a toy and then becomes distressed is not comfortable throughout the absence. A silent, tense dog may also be struggling.

Share useful observations with the caregiver. Adjust arrival times or company where necessary, and make sure someone local can respond if distress appears.

A camera may lose power or connection and cannot provide care itself. Keep the human backup independent of the recording.

Easing back after the deadline

Return gradually to the usual pattern of meals, walks and time together. Keep familiar caregivers involved if they are still needed.

Before reducing support, record easy absences and review current comfort. Do not cancel all care because your office hours have shortened.

If you take time off after the deadline, continue small comfortable practice so the next workday is not another sudden contrast.

Review any setbacks without blame. Earlier worry, more door watching or difficulty resting may mean returning to easier steps and seeking support.

Keep notes on the arrangements that worked: useful caregiver hours, successful handovers and gaps that were difficult. Update the plan while the details are fresh.

The goal is a sustainable ordinary routine, not immediately recovering every minute of pre-season alone time.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

A dog with strong distress should see the vet before busy season where possible. Share recordings and explain the coming schedule.

Contact your vet early for food refusal, if your dog seems unwell or for sudden behavior changes. Do not automatically attribute new accidents or restlessness to overtime.

Persistent panic, escape attempts or self-injury need prompt help. Serious injury, collapse or breathing difficulty require emergency veterinary care. Our separation anxiety red flags guide explains which signs are urgent.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

A qualified reward-based behavior professional can help adjust practice and care when your dog cannot settle or progress stalls.

Never shut a panicking dog in a crate or room and leave. Never punish barking, howling, damage or accidents; review health, the difficulty of the absence and available support.

You do not need to wait until the deadline passes to ask for help.

Frequently Asked Questions: Tax Season and Overtime With an Anxious Dog

Can I work long hours in tax season with a dog who has separation anxiety?
It may be workable with suitable care covering the hours your dog cannot manage alone. Confirm recurring help and backup for delays before overtime starts. Do not plan a full workday alone or rely only on a camera.
How do I find a midday dog walker before busy season?
Contact familiar or recommended caregivers early and explain your dog's behavior honestly. Arrange an introduction and trial, then check how your dog settles before and after the visit. If those gaps cause panic, arrange more company rather than relying on a short walk.
How long can my anxious dog be alone on a long workday?
Use their currently observed comfortable duration, not the length of your shift. Consider every continuous gap between caregivers. Arrange cover before any gap exceeds what your dog can manage.
Will my dog's separation anxiety get worse during busy season?
Longer unsupported absences and disrupted routines may make coping harder, but worsening is not inevitable. Review recordings and adjust care promptly. Contact your vet early for sudden changes, food refusal or if your dog seems unwell.
What should I do after the busy season ends?
Restore ordinary routines and review easy recorded absences before cutting care cover. Keep comfortable practice going during any time off. Save notes about what worked so the next busy season starts with a better plan.
