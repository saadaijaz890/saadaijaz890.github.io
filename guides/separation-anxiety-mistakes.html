Separation Anxiety Mistakes Owners Make (and What to Do Instead)

Learn which separation anxiety mistakes can set your dog back and what to do instead, from shorter practice to camera checks and calm care cover.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 27 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Common mistakes include leaving a worried dog alone too long, rushing practice and relying on products without addressing the fear. Record what happens, use absences your dog can handle, arrange company for longer gaps and ask your vet or a qualified professional for help.

  • Return to short practice your dog can manage comfortably.
  • Use video to identify stress signs instead of guessing.
  • Arrange care cover while you build alone time gradually.

If you have tried a new toy, a different room or a longer walk and your dog still panics when you leave, you are not alone. Separation anxiety can be difficult to recognize and even harder to fit around work and daily life.

This guide is about adjusting the plan, not blaming you. We are dog owners, not vets or trainers. The goal is to identify what may be making things harder and choose a kinder, more useful next step.

Why these mistakes are so common

Most owners discover the problem after it has already happened: a neighbor reports barking, or they come home to an accident or a damaged door. Without video, it is natural to guess whether the dog was bored, frightened or reacting to a sound.

Advice can also be confusing. One person suggests a crate, another recommends a food toy, and someone else says to wait it out. A product or setup that helps one dog may make another dog more uncomfortable.

Start by looking at the individual dog. Our separation anxiety signs guide can help you recognize possible distress, while our separation anxiety guide explains the broader problem.

Going too fast

A dog who can cope with you stepping outside for a few seconds is not necessarily ready for a trip to the store. The next useful step is the one your dog can handle comfortably, even when that feels very small. The ASPCA describes treatment as helping a dog tolerate being alone by gradually getting used to short absences.

Use a camera and remain close enough to return promptly. If your dog starts pacing, panting, whining or scratching, return calmly rather than waiting for silence. Make the next practice shorter or easier.

One good day is encouraging, but repeat comfortable attempts before increasing. Mix easier gaps into practice and note what happens after a food toy is finished. A dog may eat briefly and then become worried.

Setbacks can follow a longer absence, illness, a move or a change in routine. Step back to an easier level for a few sessions and look for patterns rather than treating the setback as failure.

Punishment and big emotions

Coming home to damage or an accident is frustrating. Your dog is not doing it out of spite, and scolding after the event does not teach them to feel safe alone.

The ASPCA's separation anxiety advice explains that punishment is not effective for separation anxiety and can make it worse. Check your dog for injuries, clean up calmly and use a recording to understand what happened.

You do not need to ignore your dog or withhold affection. A brief, kind goodbye and a gentle greeting are fine. If drawn-out departures seem to increase anticipation, simplify the routine and observe whether that helps.

Return calmly when your dog is distressed. Comforting them and making the next step easier are more useful than expecting them to cope without support.

Leaning on gear instead of training

A food toy, calming music or a comfortable bed may make a routine more pleasant. A camera can show what happens. None of them teaches a dog to tolerate an absence by itself.

Some anxious dogs eat while you are nearby but stop when you leave. Others finish the toy and then begin pacing. Food use is one clue; it is not proof that the entire absence was comfortable.

Introduce toys under supervision and choose an appropriate size and strength. Our how to stuff a KONG guide offers ideas for dogs who enjoy food enrichment.

If keys, shoes or a coat already trigger worry, the departure starts before the door opens. Practice a manageable cue and then remain home, watching your dog's response. Our departure cues guide explains how to work on this gradually.

Crates and second dogs

A crate can be a familiar resting place for some dogs. For others, confinement adds fear and may lead to frantic escape attempts or injury.

Never shut a panicking dog in a crate, pen or room and leave. Consider a dog-proofed space the dog already likes, and introduce any barrier while you are home. Our crates for anxious dogs guide discusses how to assess comfort.

Another dog may reassure a dog who struggles with being physically alone. But a dog distressed about being apart from a particular person may still panic with canine company. A second dog is a long-term commitment, not a dependable treatment plan.

If you already have another dog, observe both animals. The worried dog may crowd their companion, and food toys can create tension. Give food toys in separate spaces so neither dog feels a need to guard them.

Skipping the vet and the camera

Video can reveal whether worry begins at the keys, immediately after the door closes or later in the absence. It can also show quiet signs, such as trembling, rigid stillness or hiding.

Start recording before departure preparations. Keep the first observation short, watch live and return before your dog becomes distressed. If worry appears, return calmly; there is no need to keep filming a panic episode.

Dogs Trust's separation anxiety advice explains that changes in behavior are often influenced by physical health, so see your vet if your dog's behavior has recently changed. A vet check is also sensible if the behavior is severe or comes with signs of illness.

Bring short clips and a factual timeline to the appointment. Our recording guide and vet visit preparation guide can help.

No care cover while you train

Short practice is difficult to protect if the dog must also face a long unsupported absence every workday. Plan the real-life gaps as carefully as the training sessions.

While you work on it, the ASPCA's separation anxiety advice suggests avoiding leaving your dog alone except during training, for example by having a family member, friend or sitter stay with them, or using doggy daycare.

Choose company your dog actually finds reassuring. Some dogs settle with another familiar adult; others remain upset when a particular person leaves. Trial the arrangement and make a backup plan.

A walker may provide useful exercise and a toilet break, but a single visit does not cover a dog who panics before and after it. Our care options guide compares arrangements.

Quick self-check: am I making these mistakes?

Check any statement you would answer yes to. Each checked item is a place to adjust the plan, not a reason to blame yourself.

  • Have I continued a practice session after signs of worry appeared?

  • Am I expecting my dog to settle without checking a recording?

When to get extra help

Contact your vet promptly if your dog injures themselves, attempts a dangerous escape, refuses food persistently or shows other signs of illness. Our separation anxiety red flags guide explains when to seek urgent help.

A qualified, reward-based behavior professional can help if your dog panics quickly, cannot manage tiny steps or is getting worse despite an easier plan. You do not need to wait until the situation becomes unmanageable.

Share recordings, your log and the care arrangements you can realistically provide. A good plan should fit both your dog's needs and your household's circumstances.

Bottom line

If the plan has not been working, look for the earliest moment your dog becomes uncomfortable and make practice easier. Use video, arrange suitable company for longer gaps and involve your vet or a qualified professional when needed. Progress may be uneven, and small comfortable steps count.

Frequently Asked Questions: Separation Anxiety Mistakes

What makes dog separation anxiety worse?
Repeated absences beyond a dog's comfort, rushing training and punishment can all make things worse. Pain, illness or changes in routine may also affect behavior. Use recordings and speak with your vet about recent changes.
Should I punish my dog for destroying things when left alone?
No. Scolding after you return does not resolve the fear that may have caused the damage. Check for injuries, clean up calmly and review video so you can adjust care and practice.
Is it bad to say goodbye to my dog when I leave?
A brief, kind goodbye is fine. If a long or emotional routine seems to increase your dog's anticipation, try keeping it low-key. The important part is whether your dog can cope with the absence that follows.
Will getting a second dog fix separation anxiety?
Another dog may provide reassuring company for some dogs, but a dog distressed about a particular person leaving may still panic. A second dog also brings a long-term care commitment. Continue an individual training and care plan rather than relying on another dog as treatment.
Why did my dog's separation anxiety come back after it got better?
A longer absence, illness, a move or another routine change may make an old training step difficult again. Return to an easier comfortable level and review your notes. Contact your vet or a qualified behavior professional if the change is sudden, severe or continues.
