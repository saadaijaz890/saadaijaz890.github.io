How to Stuff a KONG for an Anxious Dog

Stuff a KONG with your dog's regular food and safe extras. Learn easy layers, frozen recipes, sizing, cleaning, and how to use it during departures.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 24 Oct 202611 min read
Quick answer

Start with your dog's regular food, loosely layered inside a correctly sized KONG, and add a small, safe soft-food topping. Introduce it unfrozen while you supervise, then increase difficulty gradually; it can support comfortable departures but does not resolve separation anxiety.

  • Use meal portions, check ingredient labels, and keep rich extras small.
  • Start loose and unfrozen; harder stuffing is not automatically better.
  • Ignoring food after departure can signal distress, not a disappointing recipe.

A stuffed rubber toy can give your dog a manageable job: lick, investigate, and work out how to reach familiar food. The most useful version is one your dog enjoys comfortably, rather than one packed so tightly that they abandon it.

We are dog owners, not vets or certified trainers, and we have not tested these products ourselves. This guide focuses on safe stuffing and sensible use; our Separation Anxiety Training Plan covers the actual work of building comfortable absences.

Why a stuffed KONG helps an anxious dog

Licking, chewing, and food searching provide an activity that some dogs find settling. A familiar food toy can make quiet time more pleasant and offer something enjoyable during suitable departure practice.

However, a KONG for separation anxiety is support, not treatment. Food does not make an overwhelming absence safe or comfortable. Some dogs eat briefly, then panic; others cannot eat once their person leaves.

Offer stuffed toys during ordinary time at home too. If one appears only before difficult departures, it may become another warning that you are leaving.

Choosing the right KONG

Choose by your dog's mouth size and chewing behavior, using the current product size chart. A toy small enough to fit entirely inside the mouth can become a choking or swallowing hazard.

  • KONG Classic: red natural rubber with some give, suitable for many moderate chewers.
  • KONG Extreme: black rubber intended for stronger chewers; a sensible alternative when Classic is not durable enough.
  • KONG Puppy: softer rubber designed for puppy mouths and age-appropriate chewing.

No rubber toy is beyond destruction. Supervise new toys and inspect them before every use. If your dog bites pieces off, that particular toy is unsuitable for unattended use.

Avoid choosing a smaller toy just because its cavity holds the desired food portion. Use the correct toy size and fill it partly instead. Our Best Chew Toys for Aggressive Chewers guide covers durability and chewing styles.

How to stuff a KONG in layers

KONG's stuffing guidance describes a small-opening plug, layered food, and a soft topping. Adapt the firmness to your dog's experience.

  1. Measure the food first. Set aside a portion of your dog's meal and choose one familiar extra, if appropriate. This prevents a stuffed toy from becoming an unplanned additional meal.
  2. Inspect the clean toy. Check the rubber, openings, and interior for damage or old food. Select an easy, unfrozen version for a beginner.
  3. Manage the small hole. If needed during preparation, lightly seal it with a small edible smear to reduce leakage. Avoid a hard, wedged plug. Clear the small opening before offering the toy; generic toys may have different instructions.
  4. Add a loose layer. Drop in some kibble so food comes out readily when the toy moves. Early success helps your dog understand the activity.
  5. Add a softer packed layer. Spoon in usual wet food or softened kibble. For beginners, press lightly rather than compressing it into a dense mass.
  6. Finish with an inviting plug. Add a thin topping of familiar wet food at the large opening. Keep it accessible instead of pushing every appealing ingredient deep inside.
  7. Serve and observe. Offer it on a washable mat while you remain nearby. Make the next version easier if your dog repeatedly gives up, becomes tense, or starts attacking the rubber.

Do not leave preparation tools inside the toy.

Move up only if your dog remains engaged without frustration. A nervous dog may benefit most from Level 1 or 2 indefinitely.

A lick mat can provide easier access when reaching inside a cavity is discouraging. Supervise it closely, particularly with dogs who chew silicone.

7 KONG recipes

Use your dog's own food as the main ingredient. Introduce extras individually so you can identify anything that causes digestive upset.

  1. Everyday meal mix Ingredients: regular kibble, a little water, and usual wet food. Soften some kibble and mix loosely, leaving dry pieces near the opening for an easy reward.
  2. Pumpkin meal layers Ingredients: regular food and a small amount of plain pumpkin puree. Alternate loose food with thin pumpkin layers; avoid a solid, tightly packed center.
  3. Carrot dinner Ingredients: usual wet food, kibble, and plain cooked mashed carrot. Stir the carrot through a meal portion and add an accessible wet-food topping.
  4. Chicken comfort Ingredients: regular food and a little plain cooked, finely shredded chicken. Distribute the chicken near accessible areas rather than burying it beneath difficult stuffing.
  5. Banana breakfast Ingredients: regular kibble, usual wet food, and a small smear of mashed banana. Use banana as a topping rather than filling the entire cavity.
  6. Yogurt meal swirl Ingredients: regular food and a little plain unsweetened yogurt, if tolerated. Stir through softly; skip yogurt if dairy causes loose stool or conflicts with your dog's diet.
  7. Peanut butter starter Ingredients: regular kibble, familiar wet food, and a thin smear of xylitol-free peanut butter. Keep the peanut butter modest and use it to introduce an otherwise familiar meal.

Any recipe can remain unfrozen. Novelty is optional; a dog who enjoys their ordinary food does not need a complicated menu.

Freezing a KONG

Start unfrozen so your dog learns that food is accessible. Once they comfortably empty it, try a lightly frozen version before a fully frozen one.

Allow a few hours in the freezer for a firm filling; larger toys and wetter recipes may need longer, sometimes overnight. There is no universal freezing time because food composition and freezer conditions differ.

Use a clean container to catch drips. Before serving, check the toy, remove any preparation aids, and clear the small opening.

If the frozen filling is too difficult, let it soften and make the next batch less dense. Do not add harder challenges merely to extend occupation.

Refrigerate or freeze prepared perishable fillings promptly. Discard food left sitting warm, and follow storage instructions for the ingredients used.

Using a KONG for alone time

First confirm your dog enjoys the toy while you are home and handles it safely. Then introduce it during a departure practice your dog already manages comfortably.

Give your dog space to engage without hovering. Watch on camera after leaving, including after the food runs out. A relaxed start does not establish comfort for the whole absence.

Keep offering the toy during non-departure rest periods too. Our Departure Cues guide explains how departure-related objects and routines can acquire emotional meaning.

Do not use a toy to justify a longer absence or replace a potty break. Arrange company when necessary absences exceed your dog's current tolerance.

For actual absence progression, follow our Separation Anxiety Training Plan rather than trying to make each recipe last longer.

If your dog won't touch the KONG when alone

Compare behavior before and after you leave. Does your dog stop licking immediately, carry the toy to the door, or only start eating when you return?

That pattern may indicate separation distress. It is useful information, not evidence that your dog is stubborn or that you need richer food.

Other explanations include fullness, an unfamiliar recipe, excessive difficulty, mouth pain, or general appetite loss. Try an easier familiar filling while home and consider the wider picture.

If refusal occurs with pacing, panting, drooling, whining, or exit scratching, shorten the absence and seek guidance. Do not respond by packing the toy more tightly or leaving your dog to struggle.

Cleaning and replacing

Wash after each food use. Soak to loosen residue, scrub the cavity and openings with a bottle brush, rinse, and dry thoroughly.

KONG states that most rubber toys can go on the dishwasher's top rack, but check your specific product's instructions. Other brands and attached materials may differ.

Replace the toy if rubber is missing, cracks deepen, openings tear, or the surface becomes sticky or degraded. Stop use if your dog can remove chunks.

Do not patch damaged rubber with glue or keep using a toy because most of it remains intact.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Starting with tightly packed, fully frozen stuffing.
  • Using rich extras instead of meal portions.
  • Assuming all peanut butter is safe.
  • Adding seasoned leftovers or cooked bones.
  • Offering a new toy unattended immediately.
  • Treating food refusal as a reason to prolong departure.
  • Leaving perishable leftovers warm for later.
  • Assuming a finished toy means an anxiety problem is resolved.

Keep the goal simple: a safe, enjoyable activity your dog can manage comfortably.

When to get extra help

Talk to your vet about sudden appetite changes, mouth discomfort, vomiting, diarrhea, or dietary restrictions.

Suspected toxic-food ingestion needs immediate veterinary advice. Trouble breathing, choking, or suspected swallowed rubber also requires urgent attention.

For panic during absences, involve your vet and a qualified separation anxiety professional. Food enrichment can support the plan, but cannot replace gradual training.

Bottom line

Stuff a correctly sized toy with familiar meal food, keep the first attempts easy, and add difficulty only when your dog enjoys it.

A stuffed KONG can make quiet time more pleasant. If your dog stops eating when you leave, use that observation to improve the absence plan rather than searching for a stronger distraction.

Frequently Asked Questions: Stuffing a KONG

Is peanut butter safe in a KONG?
A small amount may suit dogs who tolerate it, provided it contains no xylitol, also called birch sugar. Check the label every time and keep portions modest because peanut butter is rich. Ask your vet first if your dog has pancreatitis, weight concerns, allergies, or dietary restrictions.
How long should a frozen KONG last?
There is no required duration; size, stuffing, freezing, experience, and chewing style all matter. A shorter, enjoyable activity is preferable to a long, frustrating one. Record your dog's response and adjust difficulty without treating duration as a measure of anxiety improvement.
Can I give a KONG every time I leave?
You can include a safely introduced toy in suitable departures, using food from the daily allowance. Offer it during ordinary time at home too so it does not exclusively predict separation. It should never justify leaving beyond your dog's comfortable limit.
My dog ignores the KONG when I leave, why?
Stress may suppress interest in food, especially if your dog eats comfortably before departure or after you return. Difficulty, fullness, and medical issues are other possibilities. Review camera footage, simplify the activity, and address separation distress when other signs accompany refusal.
Can puppies have a stuffed KONG?
Puppies can enjoy an appropriately sized, puppy-suitable toy with familiar food under supervision. Begin with easy, unfrozen stuffing and account for the food within their meals. Check the toy frequently and avoid using it to stretch absences beyond their toilet or emotional needs.
