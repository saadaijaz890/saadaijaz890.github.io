When Your Teen Leaves for College: Helping a Dog With Separation Anxiety

Help your dog adjust when your teen leaves for college with shared care, steady routines, gentle alone-time practice and calmer holiday goodbyes.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 31 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Begin sharing your teen's dog-care routines with family members before move-in day. Keep daily life predictable afterward, record manageable absences and arrange company if your dog becomes distressed; changes in appetite, sleep or energy need a veterinary check.

  • Share walks, meals and bedtime care during the summer.
  • Keep someone home with your dog on college move-in day.
  • Maintain useful routines during visits and after the next goodbye.

The packed boxes may be exciting and difficult for everyone, including the dog who usually sleeps beside your teen or waits for their afternoon walk. When that person leaves for college, several familiar parts of the day can disappear together.

We are dog owners who researched this topic, not vets, trainers or product testers, and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. This guide focuses on preparing over the summer, supporting the family at home and making the next reunion and goodbye easier to manage.

Why a college move-out can unsettle your dog

Your teen may be your dog's favorite person, but they may also be the person who provides exercise, meals, play or company during otherwise quiet hours.

After move-out, the dog experiences those practical changes alongside a quieter house. Parents and siblings may be adjusting too, making routines harder to maintain.

The ASPCA's page on separation anxiety describes separation anxiety as distress when dogs are left alone or separated from the people they're attached to, and explains that treatment focuses on gradually teaching the dog to be comfortable alone.

A dog can struggle when one particular person leaves even while others remain. Alternatively, they may cope with family company but find the newly empty house difficult.

Waiting at a bedroom door does not diagnose separation anxiety. Look at whether your dog can eat, rest and engage with the remaining family, and what happens during actual absences.

The summer before move-in

Start sharing care before the final packing rush. A parent might join the usual walk, then gradually lead it without the teen. A sibling can help with meals or familiar play, with adult supervision where needed.

The teen should remain kind and involved. There is no need to withdraw affection or reject the dog to prepare them.

Introduce any necessary bedtime change weeks before move-in. Make another resting spot comfortable and spend calm time nearby rather than suddenly closing the bedroom door on departure day.

If your dog will be alone more often, begin tiny practice absences while there is still time to arrange help. Start where the dog stays relaxed, record sessions and increase only when comfortable.

Practice the teen leaving while another trusted person stays, separately from practicing an entirely empty house. Those situations may feel different to your dog.

If the departure is approaching quickly, prioritize familiar company and a practical care schedule rather than rushing practice.

Move-in day

Arrange for someone familiar to stay home with the dog while others handle the college move. Decide this before the car is packed.

Keep meals, potty breaks and the usual walk as normal as possible. A quiet sniff walk and ordinary time together are more useful than trying to exhaust the dog.

Let the teen say a calm, friendly goodbye without repeated departures and returns. The family member staying home can continue an activity the dog already enjoys.

Do not make move-in day a long alone-time test. Packing, unusual activity and multiple family members leaving may already make the day difficult.

Keep boxes and open doors managed during loading. Once the family leaves, allow the dog to rest or seek company without forcing either.

The first weeks after they leave

Owners may notice the dog waiting near the door, sleeping in the teen's room or following another person more closely. Describe these changes without assuming what your dog understands about the absence. Our velcro dog guide explains when shadowing a person is normal and when it points to distress.

Keep familiar meals, walks and bedtime routines steady. Offer enjoyable sniff walks and simple enrichment suited to your dog's health and interests. Extra activity supports daily needs, but it does not treat panic.

Record a short, manageable absence to see whether your dog settles. Watch for pacing, howling, panting, drooling, exit scratching or house-soiling associated with being alone.

Comfortable adjustment may look like returning to normal activities, accepting care from others and resting more easily. A silent dog who remains tense or frozen may still be struggling.

Avoid leaving for progressively longer periods just to see what happens. If your dog cannot manage necessary absences, arrange a familiar person, sitter or suitable care setting while you seek help.

Changes in eating, sleep or energy need a vet check rather than a guess or a label such as depression; contact your vet early if your dog refuses food or seems unwell.

Sharing care at home

Write a simple schedule that names who handles each task and who fills in. “Someone will walk the dog” is easy to miss when everyone assumes another person has done it.

For example, one parent might handle breakfast and the morning walk, while another person covers afternoon activity and the evening potty break. Keep the plan realistic around work and siblings' commitments.

Let the dog build pleasant relationships through familiar activities without forcing closeness. Short play, easy training games or quiet company can all be part of shared care.

Do not expect a younger sibling to replace the college student's entire role. Adults remain responsible for safe handling, care cover and training decisions.

Family care-sharing checklist

  • Agree who reviews camera recordings.

Video calls, belongings and visits home

Some dogs seem interested in a familiar voice on a call. Others search for the person, become restless or appear confused.

If you try a call, have someone beside the dog and keep it brief. Watch what happens afterward. Stop using it as a dog activity if it increases searching or worry; the family can still talk without involving the dog.

An unwashed T-shirt on the dog's bed is fine if the dog enjoys it and does not shred or swallow fabric. Familiar belongings may offer comfort, but they do not replace company or gradual practice.

Sleeping on the teen's bed may reflect familiar comfort. If access is safe and works for the household, it does not automatically need changing.

Let the student receive updates without becoming responsible for monitoring the dog constantly from college. Someone at home must handle care and respond to problems.

Thanksgiving and winter break

A reunion can be warm without becoming overwhelming. Let the dog approach, allow pauses and keep doors secure as people and bags arrive.

During the visit, preserve some of the routine established after move-out. Parents or siblings can continue meals and walks while the student joins in.

Avoid switching abruptly to nonstop company followed by a long empty-house day when the visit ends. Continue comfortable short practice and keep care cover where needed. Our after-vacation guide explains how to ease back after days of nonstop company.

Before the return to campus, agree who stays with the dog and what the following day looks like. Repeat a calm goodbye and ordinary daily care.

For travel and caregiver decisions surrounding family holidays, see our Thanksgiving and holiday travel guide. Here, the main goal is keeping the dog's home routine recognizable through both the visit and departure.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

Contact your vet early if your dog refuses food, seems unwell or has substantial changes in sleep or energy. Do not wait for a presumed adjustment period to pass.

House-soiling only during absences, destruction or persistent distress should also be discussed. Share recordings and information about potty breaks and recent routine changes.

Escape attempts and injuries need prompt veterinary attention. Serious injury, collapse or breathing difficulty require emergency veterinary care. Our separation anxiety red flags guide explains which signs are urgent.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

If distress is not easing after a few weeks, ask for qualified reward-based behavior support. Seek help sooner for strong panic or safety concerns.

Never punish barking, damage or accidents, and never shut a panicking dog in a crate or room and leave; adjust the care arrangement while working with your vet and behavior professional.

Frequently Asked Questions: When Your Teen Leaves for College

Does my dog miss my kid who left for college?
Your dog may notice the absence of a familiar person and the activities they shared. Waiting, searching or seeking more company can be observations worth noting, but they do not tell us exactly what the dog understands. Watch eating, sleep, energy and comfort during absences.
Why is my dog sleeping on my son's or daughter's bed since they left?
The bed may be a familiar, comfortable place with recognizable smells. That behavior alone does not diagnose separation anxiety. Contact the vet if it comes with significant changes in appetite, sleep, energy or other signs of illness.
How can I help my dog adjust when my teen goes to college?
Gradually share care before departure and keep meals, walks and bedtime predictable afterward. Practice manageable absences, record them and arrange company for longer gaps. Seek support if distress is strong or does not ease.
Should my college student video call the dog?
You can try a brief call with someone beside the dog, but watch their response. Some dogs seem comfortable hearing the voice, while others become restless or search for the person. Stop involving the dog if calls make them more unsettled.
Why is my dog anxious again after my kid's winter break visit?
The visit may have changed daily care, sleeping arrangements or how often someone was home. Returning to campus creates another transition, so resume familiar routines and easier alone-time practice with support. Contact the vet for sudden changes or persistent distress.
