Quick answer

There is no single treatment price: your budget may include veterinary care, behavior support and company during unavoidable absences. Use published prices as examples, not national quotes; costs vary by city, clinic and dog, so request a written estimate.

  • A walker or drop-in does not replace continuous company when needed.

Planning a budget for separation anxiety help can feel difficult because the first appointment is only part of the picture. Your dog may need veterinary assessment, training support and someone to stay with them while you build comfortable alone time.

We are dog owners, not vets or trainers, and we have not used these services ourselves. The figures below are published examples from the named sources, not national prices or quotes for your dog. Prices vary by city, clinic and dog; ask for a written quote.

Why separation anxiety costs vary so much

The main variables are your dog's needs, the professional involved and how much ongoing care your schedule requires. A dog who can rest during a short absence needs a different management arrangement from one who panics immediately.

Location and service format matter too. An in-person appointment, a remote training program and a university behavior consultation may include different assessments, follow-up and communication.

Training duration is also uncertain. Avoid building a budget around a promise that your dog will be comfortable alone by a particular date.

Separate your plan into assessment, ongoing support and care cover. Then add any practical expenses, such as travel or recording equipment, using actual quotes. This makes it easier to see which costs are occasional and which repeat.

Start with a vet visit

Start with your regular veterinarian, especially if the behavior is sudden or has recently changed. Pain, urinary problems and other medical issues can affect behavior or resemble parts of separation-related distress.

Forbes Advisor's January 2026 report gives an average vet visit of about $61 and a basic exam range of about $50–$250. Treat those as published context, not the expected total for your appointment.

Explain why you are booking and ask whether a longer discussion or additional appointment may be needed. Request an estimate for the exam and any proposed tests before authorizing non-emergency work.

For medication questions, ask your vet about the cost of any exam, tests and prescription. Generic options and pharmacy choice can affect cost. Do not change or stop a prescribed plan to reduce spending without discussing it with the vet.

Veterinary behaviorist costs

A veterinary behaviorist combines veterinary assessment with specialist behavior care. That can be particularly relevant when severe distress, injury risk or medical concerns overlap.

Tufts University's Foster Hospital publishes $605 for a 90-minute new-client behavior consultation. The San Francisco SPCA lists $595 for an initial behavior consultation and describes intensive treatment over 3–6 months as about $1,500–$3,000.

Those examples should not be combined into a national range. They describe particular services, and your dog's assessment or follow-up needs may differ.

Ask whether the quote includes a written plan, communication with your regular vet, video review and follow-up. Also ask what would trigger an additional charge. A clear estimate helps you budget without assuming the first consultation covers all future support.

Trainer and behavior consultant costs

Rover's dog-training cost guide gives about $100–$150 an hour for private training and about $600–$900 a month for separation anxiety training.

Malena DeMartini's separation anxiety program lists $975 for the first 4 weeks of one-on-one remote training. She created the CSAT certification, but that published program price should not be treated as the price charged by every trainer holding that credential.

Virginia Tech's companion animal behavior service lists $300 for a consultation. Confirm what that appointment includes and who provides subsequent support.

Compare the service, not just the headline price. Ask how often your recordings are reviewed, how practice is adjusted and what communication is available. Remote support may reduce travel, but it is not automatically less expensive.

The hidden cost: care while you train

Care cover can become a recurring expense even when appointments are occasional. Include the full gap created by work, commuting, errands and delays.

Living in a big city? Our guide to dog separation anxiety in a city apartment covers midday walkers, daycare and giving them safe building access.

For moderate to severe separation anxiety, the ASPCA's separation anxiety advice says the dog should not be left alone except during training sessions. It suggests alternatives including a family member, friend or dog sitter staying with the dog, or doggy daycare.

Rover's daycare cost guide reports about $40 a day on average, with facility examples around $35–$50. It also notes that some facilities add $10–$20 a day for anxious or reactive dogs. Ask whether the environment and staffing suit your dog; paying extra does not make group care appropriate.

Rover's published averages of about $21.45 for a 30-minute walk (dog-walking rates) and about $21.80 for a pet sitter's drop-in visit (dog-sitting rates) describe brief visits. They may help with toilet or activity needs, but they do not cover a dog who panics before and after the visit.

For continuous care, request a quote for the actual hours required.

A sample weekly budget

Example only—not a quote: suppose your dog enjoys a suitable daycare and needs care during three office days. On the other workdays, you work from home or a familiar family member stays with the dog.

Before you budget, decide which kind of cover suits your dog: our guide on choosing between daycare, a dog walker and a pet sitter compares the options side by side.

Using Rover's published daycare average of about $40 a day:

Three daycare days × $40 per day = $120 for that week's daycare.

If your facility is one of those that adds $10–$20 a day for anxious or reactive dogs, add that charge to each of the three days before you compare options.

This total is simple arithmetic on Rover's published figure, not a new market price or a quote, and your local daycare may charge more or less. It excludes extra charges, transportation, veterinary care and training.

If your dog dislikes daycare, this example does not fit. Replace it with a written sitter quote for the hours you need. Do not substitute a drop-in simply because it costs less if your dog needs someone present throughout.

Keep appointment costs separate from the weekly care total. A monthly training package and weekly care are different billing periods; use the provider's payment schedule rather than assuming every month has the same number of workdays.

Free and lower-cost options

Tell your vet what you can realistically spend. Ask about referrals, payment plans and which next step would be most useful for your dog.

Local shelters or rescues may offer behavior helplines, educational resources or classes. Check whether the support covers separation distress specifically. A general manners class may not address panic when alone.

Veterinary school teaching hospitals may have behavior services. Ask about availability and scope rather than assuming a teaching hospital is always cheaper.

Practical help can also reduce care expenses. Consider trading dog-sitting with trusted friends, asking family to stay or working from home on some days. Trial each arrangement to make sure your dog is comfortable.

Free educational resources and a do-it-yourself plan can help you organize safe, tiny practice while waiting for support. They are not a reason to postpone veterinary care for sudden changes, injury or severe panic. Our care options guide and working full time guide offer planning ideas.

Does pet insurance cover separation anxiety?

Some policies cover some behavioral treatment, and many do not. Read your policy wording and exclusions rather than assuming a veterinary appointment or training package qualifies.

Ask the insurer whether coverage depends on diagnosis, referral, provider credentials or prior authorization. Check waiting periods, pre-existing-condition exclusions and whether ordinary training is treated differently from medical behavior care.

Ask about exams, tests, prescriptions and follow-up separately. Request a written response and keep itemized invoices. Coverage should be confirmed before you rely on reimbursement in your budget.

Where not to cut corners

Do not skip the vet when behavior changes suddenly or your dog appears unwell. An untreated medical problem can make behavior work harder and leave the actual cause unaddressed.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

Avoid repeatedly buying gadgets instead of obtaining appropriate help. A camera records behavior; it cannot provide company or resolve fear.

Do not pay for a low-cost trainer who uses punishment, bark-control devices or prolonged distress; that is not a useful saving. Ask about methods before paying.

Never shut a panicking dog in a crate or room and leave to avoid care costs. If the budget and care requirements do not fit, tell your vet or behavior professional so you can discuss realistic alternatives.

  • Do I know what the initial appointment includes?
  • Have I asked about tests and prescription costs separately?
  • Are follow-up, video review and messaging included or separate?
  • Do I understand cancellation and renewal terms?

  • Have I checked daycare suitability and opening hours?
  • Have I included possible extra charges and transportation?
  • Can friends, family or home-working days safely reduce paid care?
  • Have I checked insurance wording and exclusions?
  • Have I asked when we will review progress and spending?
  • Do I have a backup plan for delays or unavailable carers?

Frequently Asked Questions: Separation Anxiety Treatment Costs in the US

How much does it cost to treat separation anxiety in dogs?
There is no single total because assessment, training, follow-up and care cover differ by dog. The published examples in this guide describe particular sources and services, not national prices. Request written quotes for the support and hours your dog needs.
How much does a veterinary behaviorist cost?
Tufts University's Foster Hospital lists $605 for a 90-minute new-client behavior consultation, while the San Francisco SPCA lists $595 for a first consultation. These are published examples, not a nationwide range. Ask what follow-up and other services cost before booking.
Is a separation anxiety trainer worth it?
Relevant professional support can help you interpret behavior, adjust practice and avoid repeatedly exceeding your dog's comfort. Value depends on the trainer's qualifications, methods and the support included. Ask how they review progress and coordinate with your vet.
Does pet insurance cover behavioral treatment?
Some policies cover some behavioral treatment, while many do not. Check exclusions, provider requirements and any authorization rules directly with your insurer. Get confirmation before including reimbursement in your budget.
How can I get help for my dog's separation anxiety on a budget?
Start with your vet and explain your financial limits. Ask about payment plans, shelter resources, teaching hospitals and suitable free educational support. Trusted care swaps or home-working days may reduce care costs, but sudden health changes or severe panic still need prompt help.
