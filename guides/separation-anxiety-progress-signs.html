Is My Dog's Separation Anxiety Getting Better? Progress Signs, Plateaus and Setbacks

Learn how to spot real separation anxiety progress on video, review plateaus, recover from setbacks and recognize when your dog's plan needs extra help.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 26 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Look for repeated comfortable behavior on video: easier departures, relaxed resting and fewer stress signs during comparable absences. Judge trends across your log rather than one good session; if worry increases, reduce difficulty and check for health or routine changes instead of pushing through.

  • Measure comfort during absences, not just time away or an enthusiastic greeting.
  • Silence can hide freezing, trembling or vigilance; check the whole body.
  • An easier step after a setback protects progress rather than erasing it.

When you are working on separation anxiety, a quiet departure can feel encouraging—and the next difficult session can make you question everything. Progress is rarely a straight line, and counting minutes alone can miss meaningful changes.

We are dog owners, not vets or trainers. This guide helps you review what your dog actually does, recognize when the plan needs adjusting and respond to setbacks without treating them as failure. Comfortable repetitions matter, even when they do not produce a new longest absence.

What real progress looks like

Real progress means your dog is increasingly comfortable during separation. They may disengage from the door more easily, rest sooner, respond less strongly to your preparations or remain relaxed through a previously difficult moment.

Stuck or sliding backwards? Our guide to common separation anxiety mistakes includes an "Is it working or getting worse?" table and a yes/no self-check to find what to change.

You may see less pacing, whining, barking, panting or exit scratching. Comfortable eating followed by rest can be encouraging too.

These changes should repeat across comparable absences. Over time, comfortable duration may grow, but a longer absence with more distress is not the goal.

The ASPCA’s separation anxiety advice describes gradually building tolerance through short absences. The aim is a dog who is comfortable being alone, not one who simply endures more time.

What to measure on video

The first 10–30 minutes can be especially informative, but never extend a short training absence to fill that window. If your dog comfortably manages only seconds, that is the footage to review.

Watch live so you can return before distress builds. For longer absences, check later periods too; a calm beginning does not establish comfort throughout.

Record actual departure time, first worry, settling and any outside trigger. Compare similar durations and circumstances. A quieter morning and a noisy delivery afternoon are not identical tests.

Your dog’s greeting adds context, but cannot reliably describe what happened while you were away.

What to film and measure

  • Start recording before departure preparations.
  • Include the exit and resting area.
  • Capture the whole body and sound.
  • Record the actual time you left.
  • Note the complete absence length.
  • Mark the first visible sign of worry.
  • Note pacing, vocalizing, panting or scratching.
  • Record approximate duration of those signs.
  • Note comfortable resting and when it begins.
  • Record food interest and behavior afterward.
  • Note outside noises or interruptions.
  • Record any setup or routine changes.
  • Note behavior after your return.
  • Mark missing footage as unknown.

Why a quiet dog is not always a calm dog

A dog may stop barking while remaining frightened. Freezing, trembling, hiding or sitting rigidly near the exit can all look quiet on a small screen.

Look for a loose body, ordinary position changes and the ability to disengage from the door. Compare with how your dog rests comfortably when you are home.

If you cannot tell whether your dog is sleeping or remaining tense, save a short clip for professional review. Our separation anxiety signs guide helps you recognize subtler concerns.

Weekly progress review checklist

  • How many days and absences were actually logged?
  • Which recordings are complete enough to compare?
  • Were the absence lengths reasonably similar?
  • Did departure cues cause less concern?
  • Did comfortable resting become easier?
  • Were stress signs fewer, shorter or less intense?
  • Did worry begin earlier or later?
  • Did comfort continue after food was finished?
  • Did any new stress signs appear?
  • Were there unsupported absences beyond current ability?
  • Did health, sleep or routine change?
  • What should stay the same next week?
  • What single adjustment would make practice easier?

Blank days are not successful days or failures. They are missing information.

Plateaus: why progress stalls

A plateau may mean duration is staying steady while comfort improves. That can still be useful progress.

If comfort is not improving, review whether increases are too large, sessions continue too long or every repetition asks for more. Several demanding sessions without enough recovery may also make practice harder.

Real-life absences beyond your dog’s ability can undermine the easier practice. Check care gaps as carefully as training notes.

Also consider changing conditions: a new barrier, different departure time, disrupted sleep or discomfort. Adjust the relevant factor rather than assuming your dog needs more determination.

Setbacks: why they happen

A long unexpected absence, frightening noise, move, illness or schedule change can make previously manageable departures difficult again. A different room or caregiver may also change the experience.

One difficult session does not erase earlier learning. It does tell you that the next attempt should be easier and that possible causes deserve review.

Avoid interpreting every fluctuation as a major setback. Look for a repeated change, while responding promptly to severe distress or physical warning signs.

How to recover from a setback

First check safety and health. A sudden change should not automatically be explained by training.

Return to a level that was reliably comfortable, or easier if needed. Repeat manageable practice for a few sessions and let observations guide the next increase. You usually do not need to discard the entire plan.

If distress appears, return calmly rather than waiting for silence. Do not remain outside while your dog panics to test whether they recover.

Arrange care for longer gaps. Our care options guide helps protect practice while everyday responsibilities continue.

Setback recovery checklist

  • Check for injuries and physical symptoms.
  • Note what changed before the difficult session.
  • Review footage without repeatedly provoking distress.
  • Reduce absence difficulty.
  • Choose a familiar resting setup.
  • Keep ordinary care and potty needs covered.
  • Protect comfortable rest between sessions.
  • Include easy repetitions.
  • Avoid testing the previous maximum immediately.
  • Confirm company for longer necessary absences.
  • Arrange backup care for delays.
  • Record the next attempt honestly.
  • Seek help if worry persists or worsens.

How long it can take

There is no honest completion date that fits every dog. Progress can unfold over weeks or months, and more complex difficulties may require longer support.

Starting ability, health, household changes, care coverage and the suitability of the plan all matter. Faster progress in another dog does not establish what your dog should achieve.

Set practical goals around comfort: repeat an easy departure, rest during a familiar absence or tolerate preparations without worry. Increasing duration comes when the behavior supports it.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not use the longest absence as your only measure. Avoid comparing unlike sessions or counting missing footage as success.

Do not treat food eaten, silence or lack of damage as proof of relaxation. Never shut a panicking dog in a crate to contain the problem.

Avoid punishment, aversive collars and leaving your dog to cry until exhausted. Worsening distress is information to change the plan, not a necessary stage to push through.

Do not remove all affection or make every session harder in pursuit of faster progress.

When to get extra help

The ASPCA recommends veterinary assessment to rule out medical issues. Dogs Trust also notes that physical health can influence behavior and advises veterinary attention when behavior has recently changed.

Contact your vet for sudden deterioration, appetite changes, pain concerns or persistent worsening. Injury and dangerous escape attempts need prompt attention; see our separation anxiety red flags guide.

A qualified, reward-based professional experienced in separation difficulties can help interpret a plateau and adjust the plan. Bring comparable clips and notes using our vet visit preparation guide.

Bottom line

Judge progress by repeated comfortable behavior, not a single record-breaking absence. Review trends, make practice easier after setbacks and protect progress with suitable care. Asking for help is a sensible adjustment, not evidence that your dog or your efforts have failed.

Frequently Asked Questions: Separation Anxiety Progress

How do I know if my dog’s separation anxiety is getting better?
Look for repeated relaxed behavior during comparable absences: easier departures, comfortable resting and fewer stress signs. Eating followed by continued comfort can be encouraging, but no single sign proves improvement. Compare recordings and weekly notes rather than greetings alone.
How long does separation anxiety training take?
It varies substantially, and progress may take weeks or months rather than following a fixed deadline. Some dogs need longer professional support. Let comfortable behavior determine increases instead of trying to match another dog’s timeline.
Why has my dog’s progress stopped?
Steps may be too large, sessions too demanding or necessary absences longer than your dog can manage. Health, sleep, routine and environmental changes may also contribute. Review comparable footage and seek help if an easier plan does not restore comfort.
What should I do after a separation anxiety setback?
Check safety and health, then return to an easier level for manageable repetitions. Arrange company for longer gaps and record what changed. You do not usually need to abandon all previous work, but persistent worsening needs professional review.
Is it normal for separation anxiety to get worse before it gets better?
Fluctuations can happen, but worsening distress is not a required stage of successful training. Do not push through panic or wait for exhaustion. Reduce difficulty and involve your vet or a qualified professional when deterioration continues or safety is affected.
