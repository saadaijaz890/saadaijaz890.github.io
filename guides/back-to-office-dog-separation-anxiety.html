Going Back to the Office: How to Prepare a Dog With Separation Anxiety

Returning to the office with an anxious dog? Plan gradual practice, rehearse mornings and arrange care cover for a calmer switch from working at home.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 25 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Start weeks ahead if possible: record a brief absence, practice within your dog’s current comfort level and rehearse work mornings without leaving. Arrange care for office-day gaps they cannot handle yet; your return date should not become a deadline for tolerating a full day alone.

  • Base office-day care on current observations, not what your dog managed months ago.
  • Keep gentle independence practice on home days without withdrawing affection.
  • A midday walker cannot cover panic that starts minutes after you leave.

Your work bag is back by the door, but your dog’s routine still revolves around having you nearby. After remote work or extended leave, an office day can change walks, meals, daytime company and rest all at once.

You do not need to solve everything before your first commute. You need a realistic transition plan that combines manageable practice with reliable care. We are dog owners, not vets or trainers; this guide helps you organize that switch around what your dog can handle now.

Why the switch back to the office is hard for dogs

A dog used to company may have had few recent opportunities to rest alone. Morning activity, access to you and potty breaks may also change when commuting resumes.

Still working from home most days? Our guide to dog separation anxiety when you work from home shows how to build tiny alone-time practice into the home workday, from a closed office door to short trips out.

Is it the kids going back to class, not you going back to work? Our guide to back to school and dog separation anxiety covers the quiet house after summer, the school morning rush and calm after-school returns.

The ASPCA’s separation anxiety advice identifies changes in when or how long a dog is left alone as possible triggers. That does not mean every dog returning to office life develops anxiety.

Some dogs adjust comfortably, while others show distress. Watch your individual dog rather than assuming that past experience—or being older now—establishes readiness.

How far ahead to start

Start as early as you can, ideally several weeks ahead. Alone-time progress can be slow and uneven, so a weeks-ahead plan gives you time to observe, arrange help and change course.

If you have only a few days, prioritize care coverage and an easy routine. Do not compress training by making larger jumps.

The date on your office calendar determines when coverage must begin. It does not determine how quickly your dog should progress through training.

First, find your dog's starting point

Choose a quiet time after your dog’s ordinary needs are met. Set up a camera showing their resting area and the exit, then try a separation you expect to be easy.

If you already know they panic immediately, avoid another test and seek help. Otherwise, begin very briefly—even seconds—and return before worry builds.

Look for loose posture, comfortable rest and the ability to disengage from the door. Silence, eating or lying still alone does not prove comfort.

The ASPCA advises ruling out medical issues before behavior work. Discuss sudden changes with your vet. Our Best Pet Camera guide covers recording options.

Practice the work morning

Rehearse the alarm, shower, shoes, bag and keys without always leaving. Start with whichever action your dog notices least, then return to an ordinary home activity.

Keep repetitions brief and easy. If putting on shoes causes pacing, practice a smaller part of that action rather than repeatedly running the full routine.

Gradually combine familiar actions once they cause little concern. Our Departure Cues guide explains that process.

Also rehearse the practical sequence: a suitable walk with sniffing, breakfast, a potty opportunity and time to settle. Exercise and enrichment support daily needs, but tiredness alone cannot resolve separation distress.

Care cover for office days

Write down the whole door-to-door absence, including travel, errands and likely delays. Compare that with the duration your dog has repeatedly handled comfortably.

Arrange company for the difference. That may mean a sitter arriving before you leave, a family member sharing the day or a comfortable daycare arrangement.

Trial the person and setting before relying on them. Some dogs relax with any familiar caregiver; others struggle when a particular person leaves.

Hybrid weeks

Home days can include comfortable independent rest and short practice absences. They do not need to become artificial office days spent shutting your dog away.

Keep walks, meals and rest reasonably similar across the week. Offer affection and connection normally; a home day together does not automatically undo progress.

The important distinction is between your presence and your dog’s ability to cope without it. Maintain the skill gently, while covering office absences that remain too difficult.

Back-to-office readiness checklist

Going Back to the Office: How to Prepare a Dog With Separation Anxiety: back-to-office readiness checklist

  • Record the return date and expected office days.
  • Include commuting time in every absence.
  • Identify your dog’s current comfortable starting point.
  • Discuss new behavior changes with your vet.
  • Choose a familiar resting area.
  • Check water, cooling and household safety.
  • Rehearse morning walks and potty opportunities.
  • Practice departure cues without always leaving.
  • Trial each caregiver and handover.
  • Confirm coverage for gaps beyond your dog’s ability.
  • Arrange backup care for cancellations and delays.
  • Test the camera view and connection.
  • Agree who can respond if distress appears.
  • Prepare a short log for observations.

Readiness can mean having excellent care in place while training continues. It does not require your dog to manage the workday alone.

The first office week

Allow enough morning time to avoid rushing from exercise straight into departure. Use familiar food activities and equipment rather than introducing several new products on the first day.

Have the caregiver arrive before your dog’s comfortable limit is exceeded. For dogs who struggle immediately, arrange an overlapping handover.

Check footage when you can respond. If distress builds, return or contact the arranged helper; do not simply watch until it stops.

Ask carers about rest, food, potty breaks and any worry. A quiet clip during one moment does not describe the whole day.

What to do when you get home

Offer a calm, kind greeting. You do not need to ignore your dog to demonstrate that leaving is ordinary.

Provide a potty opportunity and suitable connection, then allow rest. Avoid adding a difficult training session after a demanding day.

Review recordings and caregiver notes before changing the next day’s plan. Log the actual absence, behavior and anything unusual in our Separation Anxiety Log. Missing footage stays unknown rather than being counted as success.

If it isn't going well

Pause increases and return to an easier practice level. Check whether the difficulty came from duration, morning cues, a new barrier, an outside event or an unsuccessful handover.

Dogs Trust’s separation anxiety advice describes training that progresses to very short absences and gradually changes how the dog feels about being left. Use our Separation Anxiety Training Plan for the detailed method.

For tomorrow’s office day, adjust coverage rather than repeating an overwhelming absence. A request for temporary flexibility, an additional sitter or a care swap may buy time while you seek support.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not test a full workday simply because a short errand went well. Our How Long Can a Dog Be Left Alone guide discusses general needs, but a time chart cannot establish readiness for a dog showing distress.

Avoid abruptly ignoring your dog on home days or making every practice session harder.

Never shut a panicking dog in a crate, punish damage or use aversive collars. Do not leave them to cry until exhausted.

Food toys and cameras are useful supports, but neither replaces appropriate company or gradual training.

When to get extra help

Contact your vet if your dog panics quickly, stops eating, injures themselves or shows a sudden change. Dogs Trust specifically advises seeking veterinary advice when behavior has recently changed.

Injuries, dangerous escape attempts or signs of illness need prompt attention. Do not wait for the office transition to settle before calling.

A qualified, reward-based professional experienced in separation difficulties can help when progress stalls or coverage and practice are hard to coordinate. Ask for a plan that fits your household rather than a fixed completion date.

Frequently Asked Questions: Going Back to the Office With a Dog With Separation Anxiety

How do I prepare my dog for me going back to work?
Start several weeks ahead if possible, record a brief absence and rebuild from a comfortable level. Rehearse morning cues, restore practical routines and trial carers. Arrange coverage for any office-day gaps your dog cannot manage yet.
How long does it take a dog to adjust to being alone again?
There is no fixed timetable, and progress may be uneven. Some dogs adjust with modest support, while dogs with separation distress may need a longer training and care plan. Use repeated observations rather than a calendar deadline to decide the next step.
Will my dog get separation anxiety when I go back to the office?
A schedule change can trigger difficulties, but separation anxiety is not inevitable. Short recordings before your return help reveal whether your dog currently rests comfortably alone. Spending time together while working from home is not something you need to feel guilty about.
Is a dog walker enough when I return to the office?
A walker may be enough when your dog comfortably handles the periods before and after the visit and their other needs are met. It is not enough for a dog who panics within minutes of departure. That dog needs more continuous support and an individual training plan.
What if my dog panics on office days?
Contact your arranged helper or return so the distress does not continue unsupported, then change the next day’s coverage. Reduce practice to an easier level and contact your vet or a qualified separation professional. Seek prompt veterinary attention for injury, unsafe escape attempts or illness signs.
