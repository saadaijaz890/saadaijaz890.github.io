Thanksgiving and Holiday Travel With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety

Plan Thanksgiving travel for a dog with separation anxiety: compare care options, prepare for relatives and guests, and keep alone time manageable.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 30 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Choose the holiday arrangement where your dog can stay safe with suitable company, whether that means traveling with you or remaining with a trusted carer. Book early, trial the plan and avoid leaving your dog beyond their comfort in an unfamiliar place.

  • Confirm who stays with the dog during meals out, shopping and travel delays.
  • Trial the sitter or boarding arrangement before the holiday.
  • Protect quiet rest, familiar routines and safe boundaries around food and guests.

Thanksgiving is Thursday, November 26, 2026. For families with a dog who has separation anxiety, the holiday plan needs to cover more than the drive or dinner: it also needs to cover who is with the dog when everyone goes out.

We are dog owners, not vets or trainers, and we have no ties to any sitter, boarding or care service. This guide helps you choose a workable arrangement, prepare for a busy household and return to your usual routine without expecting your dog to cope with too much at once.

Why Thanksgiving week is hard for a dog with separation anxiety

Holiday life can change several familiar things together. Your dog may travel somewhere new, meet a carer, sleep in a different room or experience a house full of people followed by sudden quiet.

Even at home, meals, walks and departures may shift. Visitors can bring noise and excitement, while open doors, luggage and food create extra management needs.

Taking your dog with you does not automatically solve the problem. A dog who copes with a short absence at home may struggle in an unfamiliar house. Likewise, a dog who enjoys a sitter's visit may not yet be comfortable when you leave.

The useful question is: who will be with the dog, where will they rest and what happens if the plan changes?

Bring your dog, hire a sitter, or board?

Choose according to your dog's observed comfort, not the option that looks simplest on paper. Consider travel, the destination, your planned outings and the availability of familiar company.

An in-home sitter preserves the surroundings but may still need an introduction. Boarding can work for some dogs who enjoy the environment and staff contact; others find the noise or time alone difficult.

A trusted friend or relative may be a good fit if they can provide the actual level of company required. Being nearby is different from being available throughout the hours your dog needs support.

If you bring your dog, agree on a care plan with your hosts before traveling. Our general travel guide and boarding guide explore those choices in more detail.

Book early and do a trial run

Thanksgiving is a busy travel period, and sitters and boarding facilities can fill early. Start inquiries as soon as your plans take shape, especially when your dog needs someone to stay rather than make brief visits.

Describe the behavior honestly: what your dog does, when it starts and what company helps. Confirm opening hours, arrival and collection arrangements, emergency procedures and a backup for delays.

Do a trial before the holiday. Begin with a short sitter visit while you remain available, then assess an easy handover. For boarding, a suitable day stay may help you assess the setting before considering an overnight trial.

Ask for specific observations: eating, resting, pacing, vocalizing and recovery after you leave. A trial is information, not a pass-or-fail test. Change the plan if your dog struggles.

Visiting relatives with your dog

Ask your host to prepare a quiet area before arrival. Bring familiar bedding, usual food, bowls and a toy your dog safely enjoys. After a toilet and sniffing opportunity, allow time to explore and settle with you nearby.

A quiet bedroom can provide relief from the gathering, but it should not become a place to shut a panicking dog away. Arrange for someone the dog is comfortable with to stay there when needed.

Plan the big meal outing, Black Friday shopping and other dog-free activities in advance. Take turns staying, choose dog-friendly plans or arrange a suitable local carer. Do not leave your dog alone in a strange house because the outing is expected to be short. If the drive itself is stressful, see our car travel anxiety guide.

Introduce other pets cautiously and provide separate feeding and resting areas. Adults should supervise children around the dog; children should not hug, crowd or disturb a resting or stressed dog.

Never leave your dog alone in a parked car.

Hosting guests at home

If you are hosting, set door rules before guests arrive. Choose an adult to manage the dog during arrivals, and tell visitors not to open gates or outside doors without checking.

Create a calm room or resting area away from the busiest traffic. Let the dog choose whether to interact. A familiar person can sit with them during noisy periods instead of expecting them to remain alone behind a closed door.

Tell guests to leave the dog's bed and food toy undisturbed. If your dog guards food or space, keep interactions safely managed and seek professional guidance rather than forcing contact.

Keep walks, meals and toilet opportunities reasonably familiar. A busy party is not a substitute for suitable activity or sleep. Tell everyone that treats must come through the person responsible for feeding the dog.

Holiday food dangers and who to call

Keep serving dishes, plates and trash out of reach. Ask guests not to sneak food, even if they believe a small amount is harmless.

Turkey bones can splinter or cause choking or obstruction. Fatty scraps can cause serious digestive trouble. Onions, grapes and raisins, and products containing xylitol are also risky for dogs.

If your dog eats something concerning, contact your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435 promptly (a consultation fee may apply). Keep packaging or ingredient information and note roughly what was eaten and when. Do not wait for symptoms or try to induce vomiting unless a veterinary professional instructs you.

Trouble breathing, collapse, choking or other severe signs need emergency veterinary care. Save the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital's details for your destination before traveling.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

Keep alone time within what your dog can handle

Holiday plans do not change your dog's current ability to cope. Include travel delays, restaurant waits and shopping time when planning care.

The ASPCA's separation anxiety advice recommends avoiding absences outside training while working on the problem, using arrangements such as a family member, friend or sitter staying with the dog, or suitable doggy daycare.

Do not assume comfort at home transfers to the destination. Use easier steps and suitable company in the new setting. If tiny practice absences are appropriate, watch live video and remain close enough to return promptly.

If distress appears, return calmly and make the plan easier. Do not wait for silence or use a food toy to carry the dog through an absence they cannot handle; a food toy is enrichment, not a treatment for panic.

Getting back to normal after the holiday

After travel or a busy household, give your dog time for familiar meals, walks and undisturbed rest. Unpack hazards and restore their usual resting area.

If a winter storm could delay your trip home, a winter plan for a dog with separation anxiety covers lining up a nearby backup person before the forecast turns.

Staying home for December? Our guide to a Christmas and New Year's plan for a dog with separation anxiety covers holiday parties, visitors, Christmas Day and New Year's Eve fireworks.

Check how your dog handles short departures before returning to a longer routine. Being comfortable before the trip does not guarantee the same starting point afterward.

Use easier practice if needed and keep care cover available. A setback does not mean all previous progress is lost. Our after-vacation guide covers rebuilding the routine after a period of extra company.

  • On arrival: Offer a toilet break, gentle exploration and quiet company.

  • Before returning: Confirm pickup or handover plans and backup arrangements.
  • The weekend after: Restore familiar routines and check short departures on camera.

When to get extra help

Contact your vet well before the trip if your dog panics quickly, has injured themselves or is not coping with the proposed care arrangement. Follow any existing veterinary plan or prescription and ask about holiday arrangements in advance.

Do not assume new food refusal, lethargy or other changes are simply stress. Call the vet for advice, particularly if your dog is not eating, seems unwell or has suddenly changed behavior.

Injury, dangerous escape attempts or possible ingestion of risky food need prompt attention. A qualified, reward-based behavior professional can help you choose a safer care plan if your dog cannot settle with the available options.

Shortening or changing the holiday is sometimes the practical choice. It is reasonable to prioritize an arrangement your dog can manage.

Frequently Asked Questions: Thanksgiving and Holiday Travel With an Anxious Dog

Should I take my dog with separation anxiety to Thanksgiving?
Bringing your dog may work if travel, the destination and planned activities suit them. Confirm that someone can stay when the dog cannot join an outing. If the trip itself or the household would be overwhelming, consider a familiar carer instead.
Is boarding or a pet sitter better for a dog with separation anxiety?
It depends on the dog and the care actually provided. An in-home sitter preserves familiar surroundings, while some dogs enjoy an appropriate boarding environment. Trial the option and ask exactly when the dog would be alone.
Can I leave my dog alone at a relative's house?
Do not assume your dog can manage the same absence as at home. An unfamiliar house may make separation harder, so arrange suitable company unless your dog has demonstrated comfort with the specific situation. Never leave them to panic or use a parked car as an alternative.
How do I keep my dog calm with holiday guests?
Preserve familiar routines, allow voluntary interaction and provide a quiet resting area with company when needed. Manage doors and supervise children and other pets. Ask guests to leave the dog undisturbed and avoid offering food without checking.
How far ahead should I book a sitter for Thanksgiving?
Start as soon as your travel plans are taking shape, because holiday availability can fill early. Leave enough time for introductions, a trial and a backup arrangement. A sitter's willingness to accept the booking is not a substitute for checking how your dog copes.
