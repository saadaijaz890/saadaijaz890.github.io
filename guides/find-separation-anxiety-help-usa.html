How to Find a Veterinary Behaviorist or Separation Anxiety Trainer in the US

Find qualified separation anxiety help in the US: compare veterinary behaviorists, CAABs and trainers, check credentials and ask useful questions.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 30 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Start with your regular veterinarian to check for medical causes, then choose a qualified professional with separation anxiety experience. Verify their credentials, ask about reward-based methods and veterinary collaboration, and consider virtual help if suitable local support is unavailable.

  • Veterinary behaviorists are veterinarians; trainers and non-veterinary consultants cannot prescribe medication.
  • A credential helps you search, but separation anxiety experience still matters.
  • Ask how they use video, adjust practice and coordinate with your vet.

Searching for “dog behaviorist near me” can produce a confusing mix of veterinarians, trainers, consultants and impressive-sounding titles. When your dog is struggling, it can be difficult to know which person has the skills you need.

We are dog owners, not vets or trainers, and we have not used or tested these services ourselves. This guide explains the main credentials, where to look and what to ask so you can make an informed choice. For the basics first, see our complete separation anxiety guide.

Start with your regular vet

Your regular veterinarian should be the first stop. Pain, urinary problems and other health changes can affect behavior or resemble parts of separation-related distress.

Tell the clinic if the behavior began suddenly, your dog seems unwell or there have been changes in appetite, drinking, sleep or toileting. Share any injuries or escape attempts when booking rather than waiting for a routine behavior consultation.

Your vet can assess health, discuss whether medication may belong in a treatment plan and help you find appropriate behavior support. They do not have to be a specialist to start that conversation.

For preparation, use our vet visit guide and our list of medication questions for your vet. If your dog is hurting themselves or attempting dangerous escapes, see our red flags guide and contact the vet promptly.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

The main types of help, in plain words

A board-certified veterinary behaviorist is a veterinarian with specialty training in behavior. The credential is Diplomate of the American College of Veterinary Behaviorists, usually written DACVB or Dip ACVB. They can assess medical and behavioral problems and prescribe medication when appropriate.

A Certified Applied Animal Behaviorist, or CAAB, is certified through the Animal Behavior Society. ACAAB means Associate Certified Applied Animal Behaviorist. These are applied behavior credentials, not veterinary licenses. A non-veterinary CAAB or ACAAB cannot prescribe and should work with your vet on medical questions.

Certified trainers and behavior consultants help owners carry out training and behavior-change plans. Examples include CPDT-KA, awarded through CCPDT, and certified consultants through IAABC. Their experience and areas of practice vary.

A trainer may also hold a separation-anxiety-specific certification, such as CSAT, meaning Certified Separation Anxiety Trainer. This indicates specialized training through the issuing program; it does not make the person a veterinarian, and it cannot promise a particular outcome.

In most of the US, “dog trainer” and the general title “behaviorist” are not protected professional titles. Someone can use them without holding the credentials above. Ask what qualifications sit behind the description.

Which one fits your dog

For mild-looking concerns, start with your vet, then consider a qualified trainer or consultant who regularly works with separation anxiety. “Mild-looking” still deserves investigation: a quiet dog may be trembling or unable to settle.

If your dog injures themselves, tries to escape, panics quickly or has overlapping health or behavior concerns, ask your vet about a veterinary behaviorist referral. A dog who may need medication needs a veterinarian involved, whether that is your regular vet or a specialist.

The ASPCA's separation anxiety advice says moderate to severe cases need a trained, experienced professional to guide desensitization and counterconditioning—the gradual work of changing a dog's response to being alone. It recommends a CAAB, ACAAB or board-certified veterinary behaviorist.

The ASPCA also says a CPDT may help if those options are unavailable, but owners should check education and experience treating fear with those methods because that expertise is not required by CPDT certification.

You may work with a team: your vet handles medical assessment while a suitable trainer or consultant coaches the daily practice.

How to search US directories

Use the credentialing organization's directory as a starting point, then confirm the person's current credential and services. The ASPCA's Behavioral Help for Your Pet page also explains the main types of behavior professionals.

  • ACVB: Use its Find a Diplomate directory to find board-certified veterinary behaviorists. If you find a resident or another designation, ask about their role and specialist supervision.
  • Animal Behavior Society: Search its CAAB directory for CAABs and ACAABs. Check whether they work with dogs and accept separation-related cases.
  • CCPDT: Use its certified dog trainer and behavior consultant directory. Confirm the exact credential, then ask specifically about separation anxiety experience.
  • IAABC: Search its Consultant Locator for dog professionals in the US. Check the certification category and the person's area of practice.
  • Separation anxiety trainers: The program behind the CSAT credential publishes a list of its certified trainers. It is a name list rather than a location search, so check the trainer's name there and ask them about availability. For other separation anxiety certifications, verify the listing with the program that issued it.

Expand beyond your immediate ZIP code if local options are limited. Ask about virtual availability, waitlists and whether a referral or medical records are needed.

A directory listing is a way to find candidates, not a reason to skip questions about methods, scope and fit.

Virtual consultations for separation anxiety

Remote work can suit separation anxiety because the professional can observe your dog in the place where the difficulty occurs: your home after you leave.

A trainer may watch live video, review saved clips and coach you through very short practice. Ask how you will monitor your dog, how the professional decides when to stop and what happens if the dog becomes distressed.

You do not need to leave long enough to produce panic for an assessment. Explain what you already know and agree on a safe observation plan first.

Medical questions still belong with a veterinarian. Rules about veterinary remote consultations, establishing a veterinary relationship and prescribing vary by state and situation. Ask the veterinary provider what they can offer for your location and how your regular vet will be involved.

Virtual training can widen your choices, but check that the technology, communication style and amount of guidance suit your household.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • What credentials do you hold, and where can I verify them?
  • How much of your work involves separation-related distress?
  • How do you assess whether the problem is separation anxiety or something else?
  • What methods and equipment will you use?
  • How do you keep practice below the dog's stress level?
  • What do you do if my dog becomes distressed during practice?
  • Will you review live video or recordings from home?
  • Will you work with my regular veterinarian?
  • When would you refer us to a veterinary behaviorist?
  • How will we track progress and adjust the plan?
  • What support is available between appointments?
  • What is included in the written agreement and follow-up?
  • Can you help us plan care for unavoidable absences?
  • What happens if the plan is not helping?

You should receive understandable answers, not pressure to commit before you know the approach. It is reasonable to ask for time to read the agreement.

Red flags to avoid

Be cautious about anyone who promises a fast fix or a certain result. A professional should explain uncertainty and how the plan will change with your dog's response.

Avoid anyone proposing punishment, bark or shock collars, spray devices or leaving a distressed dog to cry until quiet. “Just crate him and leave” is not a suitable response to panic.

Shaming you for working, comforting your dog or needing backup care is another warning sign. A useful professional helps you plan around real constraints.

Refusing to work with your vet, dismissing possible medical causes or advising medication changes without veterinary authority also warrants concern. Ask how they recognize the limits of their own role.

What to bring to the first appointment

Prepare short videos showing departure preparations and your dog's behavior during an absence. Include timestamps for important moments, and retain the original recording in case more context is needed.

Bring a simple log: when you left, how long you were gone, when worry began and whether your dog settled. Keep missing observations blank rather than guessing.

Also gather medical history, a list of everything your dog currently receives, previous training approaches and what happened with each. Include your work schedule, household members, available backup care and the setup your dog uses.

Our recording guide and printable log can help. You do not need a polished presentation; clear, honest information is enough.

While you wait for an appointment

Contact your regular vet rather than waiting without support if your dog is panicking or showing new behavior. Ask the specialist's office whether they have a cancellation list or preparation instructions.

Arrange company for absences beyond your dog's current comfort. A family member, friend or suitable sitter may help; daycare is an option only if your dog enjoys and safely manages that environment.

A walker can provide activity and toilet breaks, but a short visit does not cover distress before and after it. Trial any care arrangement and have a backup.

Avoid dramatic tests, forced confinement or pushing through distress while waiting. Our care options guide can help you plan. Discuss any existing practice from our training plan with the professional so it fits their assessment.

Lower-cost ways to get help

Tell your vet about your budget and ask which referral or combination of services best matches your dog's needs. Ask providers to explain the total commitment, what is included and whether follow-up is separate.

Want to budget before you book? Our guide to separation anxiety treatment costs in the US lists published example fees for vet visits, veterinary behaviorists, trainers and care cover, with a printable budget checklist.

Local shelters and rescues may offer behavior helplines, educational resources or classes. Ask whether the support addresses separation distress specifically; a general manners class may not cover it.

Veterinary school teaching hospitals sometimes offer behavior services. Availability, referral requirements and costs vary, so contact them directly.

Virtual care may reduce travel costs, though it is not automatically less expensive. If funds are limited, explain your priorities and ask what can be done safely now and what needs further assessment.

Bottom line

Start with your veterinarian, verify credentials and choose someone with relevant separation anxiety experience who uses reward-based methods and collaborates on medical care. Clear answers, realistic expectations and a plan that responds to your dog matter more than an impressive title alone.

Frequently Asked Questions: Finding Separation Anxiety Help in the US

What is the difference between a veterinary behaviorist and a dog trainer?
A board-certified veterinary behaviorist is a veterinarian with specialty training who can assess medical problems and prescribe when appropriate. A dog trainer coaches learning and behavior change but cannot provide veterinary care unless separately qualified. Check the trainer's credentials and separation anxiety experience.
Do I need a veterinary behaviorist for separation anxiety?
Not every dog needs a specialist, but every dog should start with their regular vet. Severe panic, self-injury, escape attempts or complex medical concerns are reasons to ask about a veterinary behaviorist. Your vet can help decide which support fits.
What is a certified separation anxiety trainer?
It is a trainer who holds a certification focused on separation anxiety, such as CSAT. Ask who issued the credential and verify it through that program. Specialized training does not make the person a veterinarian or promise a particular result.
Can a separation anxiety trainer work with my dog online?
Yes, many offer remote coaching using live video and recordings of your dog at home. Ask how they assess stress, adjust practice and coordinate with your vet. Veterinary remote services have separate requirements that vary by location.
How do I know if a dog trainer is qualified?
Verify their credential with the issuing organization and ask about experience with fear and separation distress. Request a clear explanation of methods, video review, veterinary collaboration and referral limits. Avoid punishment, owner shaming and promises of a quick, certain result.
