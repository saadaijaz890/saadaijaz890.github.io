Long-Distance Relationship Weekends With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety

Plan regular weekends visiting your partner with familiar dog care, trial overnights, steady routines and backup for delayed flights or late drives.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 5 Nov 202610 min read
Quick answer

Arrange familiar daytime and overnight care for the complete weekend absence, including travel and delays. Trial the arrangement before relying on it regularly, alternate travel when practical and keep weekday training separate from real trips; a camera or brief visits cannot replace needed company.

  • Trial familiar care before making weekend trips routine.
  • Cover daytime gaps as well as overnight stays.
  • Prepare backup for a delayed Sunday return.

Friday-evening departures and Sunday-night returns can become a familiar part of a long-distance relationship. For a dog who struggles with separation, those weekends need dependable care rather than an assumption that repetition will make them easier.

We are dog owners who researched this topic, not vets, trainers or product testers, and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. This guide focuses on recurring visits: familiar overnight support, weekends when your partner comes to you, steady weekdays and a gentle return home.

Why regular weekends away are hard on an anxious dog

Your dog may usually share evenings and nights with you. A weekend visit changes that pattern for several days, sometimes immediately after a normal workweek.

The ASPCA's page on separation anxiety describes separation anxiety as distress when dogs are left alone or separated from the people they're attached to, and explains that treatment focuses on gradually teaching the dog to be comfortable alone.

A Friday-to-Sunday trip is not gradual practice. Predictable departures can still be difficult when the unsupported absence exceeds your dog's current comfort. Our guide to how long a dog can be left alone explains why the whole absence counts, travel included.

Travel delays add uncertainty, while repeated weekends can interrupt meals, walks and rest unless someone maintains them. If you also travel for work, our business trip guide covers those trips.

Who stays with your dog

A familiar overnight sitter, trusted neighbor or family member may provide suitable company. Ask exactly when they will be present, including during the day.

An overnight booking does not necessarily cover Saturday afternoon. If your dog cannot comfortably manage that gap, arrange another familiar helper.

A walker can provide exercise and potty breaks alongside someone who stays. Walking visits alone cannot cover distress during unsupported hours.

Boarding is another possibility after a successful trial stay. Familiarity matters, but the useful question is whether your dog actually settles after you leave.

Our care options guide and pet sitter prep guide cover the broader arrangements. For recurring weekends, aim for a dependable main caregiver and a familiar backup.

Try the arrangement out first

Begin with relaxed time together while you are home, followed by a short supported departure. Watch how your dog responds after you leave.

Being friendly with the sitter during introductions does not prove that they settle comfortably afterward. Ask about resting, eating, potty breaks and any pacing, vocalizing or scratching at the door.

Do a trial overnight before relying on care for the whole weekend. Keep usual meals, walks and bedtime as consistent as practical.

If your dog remains distressed despite company, adjust the arrangement and seek help before extending it. Repeating a difficult stay is not a substitute for finding a workable plan.

Leave time to try another familiar person or setting.

Taking turns: when your partner visits

Alternating who travels can reduce the number of weekends you are away. It also gives your dog opportunities to become familiar with your partner without a trip added.

Let introductions move at the dog's pace. Your partner can sit quietly and allow the dog to approach rather than reaching, leaning over or insisting on contact.

Keep walks, meals and resting places familiar during the visit. Your dog does not need to join every interaction or activity.

Do not assume your partner's presence automatically makes your dog comfortable when you leave. Assess that separately through short, supported practice.

Our new partner or roommate guide covers developing shared routines in more detail.

The weekend bag and other departure cues

Your dog may start following when you open the weekend bag, gather clothes or put keys beside the door. Thursday packing can become part of Friday's departure pattern.

On ordinary days, handle one mild cue without leaving. Put an item in the bag, then continue your evening; move the bag briefly, then sit down.

Keep these sessions comfortable. If your dog becomes worried, simplify the activity rather than repeatedly rehearsing the whole departure. Our departure cues guide covers the fuller process.

Pack in calm sessions before travel day. Have the sitter arrive before final loading where possible, allowing time for a quiet handover rather than a rushed goodbye.

Keep weekdays steady between trips

Protect familiar daily anchors: meals, comfortable walks, rest and quiet company. Ask the sitter to follow the same broad pattern during weekends.

Use weekday practice to build comfortable alone time separately from travel; our separation anxiety training plan covers that slower work. Start very small, observe through recordings and increase only while your dog remains relaxed.

If distress appears, return calmly and make the next session easier. Do not wait for silence before helping.

Keep everyday commitments covered while training continues. Avoid treating each weekday as a chance to make up progress before Friday.

A simple note about sleep, eating and recent practice can help you and the sitter spot changes across several weekends.

Never alone for the whole weekend

Never leave your dog alone for a whole weekend, from Friday to Sunday, with food, water and occasional visits. Those visits cannot provide company in between.

Never leave your dog crated alone all day, tied outside, alone in a yard or alone in a car as a substitute for care. A camera does not change that.

Arrange suitable daytime and overnight support for the complete absence. If the arrangement falls through, use the backup or change the visit rather than leaving an uncovered weekend.

When a flight is delayed or the drive runs late

A delayed flight or a late drive home can leave a gap in care, so agree on an extension plan before departure. Ask whether the main sitter can stay longer and who takes over if they cannot.

Contact them when a delay becomes likely, before their agreed coverage ends. Do not assume they can extend indefinitely.

If your dog cannot be alone, the replacement needs to arrive before the first person leaves. A later visit does not cover the gap.

Keep care in place until you actually reach home. For a late drive, use the backup rather than depending on rushing to solve the problem.

Staying in touch with the sitter

Agree on manageable updates about rest, meals, walks and potty breaks. One settling update and a brief daily report may be more useful than constant requests.

Ask for prompt contact about illness or strong distress. Give the sitter clear veterinary details and instructions about who helps if you cannot answer.

A camera can provide useful observations, but the person present needs a plan they can follow independently.

Review difficult moments together after the trip rather than sending repeated instructions throughout the weekend.

Coming home on Sunday night

Greet your dog gently, offer a potty opportunity and allow quiet company. Calm does not mean ignoring them or withholding ordinary affection.

Ask how the weekend went before interpreting an excited welcome as proof that everything was comfortable.

Some owners notice following, door-watching or difficulty relaxing that first evening. Restore familiar routines and keep the next practice absence easy.

If Monday involves work or errands, arrange support based on your dog's current needs. A late return does not leave much time to assess a setback before another departure.

Planning ahead if you move in together

Begin sharing comfortable walks, feeding and quiet time during visits, without assuming your dog will immediately accept a new care arrangement.

Discuss the future household routine: who leaves when, who provides walks and what happens when both people are away.

If a move is planned, prepare familiar bedding and suitable care for the transition. Comfort alone in one home does not establish the same ability in another.

Our guide to traveling with a dog who has separation anxiety and the new partner or roommate guide linked above cover those next steps. For now, keep weekend plans workable without relying on a future move to resolve distress.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

Contact your dog's vet early if they refuse food, seem unwell or show marked changes in sleep, energy or behavior. Strong distress, injury or escape attempts need prompt attention.

Share useful recordings and the sitter's notes, including whether distress continued with another person present.

A qualified reward-based behavior professional can help when comfortable absences remain short or recurring weekends are consistently difficult. If you are wondering about medication for weekends away, ask your vet; never give your dog any medication without their advice. Our separation anxiety red flags guide explains which signs are urgent.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

Never punish barking, howling, damage or accidents; they are signs of distress, not spite. Never shut a panicking dog in a crate or room and leave. Maintain suitable care while seeking help.

Frequently Asked Questions: Long-Distance Relationship Weekends With an Anxious Dog

Can I leave my dog alone for a weekend while I visit my partner?
No. Never leave your dog alone for a whole weekend; arrange suitable daytime and overnight care with a person. Brief visits cannot cover a dog who becomes distressed between them. Confirm every gap before traveling.
Who should watch my dog when I travel to see my partner?
Consider a familiar overnight sitter, trusted family member or suitable boarding tried beforehand. A walker can help alongside someone who covers the remaining company needs. Prepare a familiar backup too.
Should I bring my anxious dog on visits?
Consider travel comfort, the unfamiliar home and who will stay with your dog during plans away from the house. Bringing them is not automatically easier than familiar care at home. Never leave them alone in a car while you visit or go out.
How do I help my dog meet my long-distance partner?
Keep introductions quiet and let your dog choose whether to approach. Maintain familiar routines and avoid pressuring contact. Assess comfort with your partner separately before relying on them as the sole caregiver.
Why is my dog so clingy when I get back on Sunday?
Following you closely can accompany a disrupted routine or difficulty during your absence, but it does not identify the cause by itself. Review the sitter's observations and restore quiet routines. Contact your dog's vet early if eating, sleep, energy or general health changes.
