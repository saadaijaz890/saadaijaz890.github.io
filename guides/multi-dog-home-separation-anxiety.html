Separation Anxiety in Multi-Dog Homes: When Only One Dog Panics Alone

When only one of your dogs panics alone: does the other dog help, filming both dogs, safe feeding apart and gentle alone-time practice for multi-dog homes.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 27 Oct 202611 min read
Quick answer

Another dog's company helps some dogs, but it may not ease distress about a person leaving. Film both dogs, choose a setup they can use safely and build comfortable absences gradually, with human care cover for longer gaps.

  • Watch both dogs to see whether company helps or creates tension.
  • Introduce any separation between dogs gradually, while you are home.
  • Feed high-value toys apart and arrange company during difficult absences.

You might expect two dogs to keep each other company while you are out. Yet one dog may pace at the door or howl while the other settles down for a nap.

That difference can be confusing, but it gives you useful information. We are dog owners, not vets or trainers. This guide helps you observe both dogs, protect their relationship and plan gentle practice for the dog who struggles.

Does the other dog help?

Sometimes canine company appears to make being without people easier. In other homes, the anxious dog continues to search for their person even with a companion nearby.

Dogs Trust's separation anxiety advice notes that getting another dog may provide company, but it won't solve separation anxiety. Our second dog guide discusses that question in more detail; here, the focus is on the dogs already sharing your home.

Why only one dog panics

Dogs in the same household can have different histories, health needs and ways of coping. One may have learned to rest comfortably when people leave, while the other may struggle with separation from a particular person.

Some dogs find it difficult to be physically alone and appear more comfortable with canine company. Others remain distressed even when another dog stays. Our separation anxiety vs isolation distress guide explains why that distinction matters.

A calm companion is not responsible for fixing the anxious dog's fear. If the worried dog repeatedly follows, climbs on or bothers them, the companion also needs space and support.

New behavior deserves a health check. Pain, illness or sensory changes can affect either dog, even when the other dog's routine has stayed the same.

Filming both dogs

Set up a camera where it can show the main resting area and exit. If the dogs use different rooms, you may need a second view or an old phone placed safely out of reach. Check that each dog's body and the spaces around beds and doors are visible.

Start recording before you pick up keys or prepare to leave. For an initial observation, make the absence very short, watch live and stay close enough to return promptly. If either dog becomes distressed, return calmly; do not prolong the absence to get more footage.

Note what each dog does separately:

  • Does either dog pace, pant, drool, bark or scratch?
  • Can the quieter dog move away and rest undisturbed?
  • Does either dog block the other's access to a bed, water or doorway?
  • Does behavior change when a food toy appears or runs out?
  • Do the dogs settle, stay tense or become more agitated?

A single quiet clip does not prove that a longer absence will be comfortable. Review several short observations and share relevant clips with your vet or behavior professional. Our best pet camera guide covers equipment options.

Together or apart when you leave?

There is no universal answer. Choose an arrangement that both dogs have shown they can use safely and comfortably while you are home.

If you cannot safely separate dogs for food without causing panic, skip the food toys for that departure and arrange human help. Safety and comfort for both dogs come first.

Safe set-up for multi-dog homes

Give each dog access to fresh water and a comfortable resting place. Place beds so one dog can move away without being trapped beside the other. Keep pathways clear and remove cords, toxic items and other hazards.

A walk-through gate can create separate areas while allowing a view of the home. It is useful only if both dogs remain comfortable with it. Some dogs jump, push or chew gates, and a barrier can increase distress.

Separate rooms may suit dogs who already relax apart. Introduce them gradually while you are present; a closed door should never be used to leave a panicking dog confined.

If the dogs remain together, remove items that could trigger guarding and ensure both can rest without being crowded. Our dog gate vs exercise pen guide explains room-level choices.

Individual walks, sniffing time and attention can also help you meet each dog's needs. Choose activities suited to each dog rather than assuming the same routine works equally well for both.

  • Keep practice shorter than either dog's comfort limit.
  • Confirm a backup carer for unexpected delays.

Practising alone time with one dog

The ASPCA's separation anxiety advice describes treatment as helping a dog tolerate being alone by gradually getting used to short absences. While you work on it, the ASPCA suggests avoiding leaving the dog alone except during training, for example by having a family member, friend or sitter stay with them, or using doggy daycare.

Start with the arrangement in which both dogs are most comfortable. If they normally rest together safely, practice a tiny departure with both present while you watch live. Return before either dog becomes worried.

Repeat easy attempts before making a small increase. If the anxious dog starts pacing or the other dog becomes tense, return calmly and make the next attempt easier. There is no need to wait for silence.

Practicing separately can also be useful, but only after both dogs have learned to relax apart while you are home. Begin with a very brief, comfortable separation and keep an adult available for each dog if needed. Do not remove one dog's companion suddenly and leave the other to cope.

The anxious dog may find being without their person difficult even when their companion stays. Conversely, the previously relaxed dog may struggle when the dogs are separated. Adjust the plan for both.

Feeding and chews when you leave

A food toy can be an enjoyable activity, but two toys do not automatically prevent conflict. One dog may finish first and approach the other, or guard a resting place even when the food is gone.

Give long-lasting chews and stuffed toys apart, in areas both dogs already accept, and supervise new arrangements. Pick up finished toys before allowing the dogs back together. If separation itself causes worry, provide the activity while a person supervises or omit it during departure.

Some anxious dogs will not eat when their person leaves. Record that observation and make the practice easier; the toy cannot resolve panic. Our how to stuff a KONG guide and enrichment guide offer activity ideas.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not assume the calm dog makes every absence safe, or expect them to manage the other dog's distress. Do not suddenly separate dogs who are used to being together.

Avoid leaving high-value chews together or forcing dogs to share a bed. Never scold or punish growling, which can make fear worse; a warning is information that the dogs need space and help.

Never use bark-control devices, leave a distressed dog to cry until quiet, or shut a panicking dog in a crate, pen or room and leave. A barrier is a management tool only when the dog is comfortable with it.

Do not judge progress from your greeting alone. Video can show whether both dogs actually rested before you returned.

When to get extra help

Dogs Trust's separation anxiety advice explains that changes in behavior are often influenced by physical health, so see your vet if either dog's behavior has recently changed.

Contact your vet promptly for injuries, dangerous escape attempts, persistent food refusal or signs of illness. If either dog guards resources, snaps or repeatedly cannot settle around the other, keep them safely managed and ask for a qualified, reward-based behavior professional.

Seek support if one dog panics quickly, both dogs become distressed or you cannot arrange a setup that keeps each dog safe. Bring short videos and notes showing when behavior begins and which people or dogs are present.

Bottom line

Your dogs may share a home and still have different needs when you leave. Observe both, protect their access to space and resources, and build absences at a pace each can handle. Arrange human company for longer gaps and involve your vet or a qualified professional when distress or tension continues.

Frequently Asked Questions: Separation Anxiety in Multi-Dog Homes

Why does only one of my dogs have separation anxiety?
Dogs can differ in their experiences, health and responses to being apart from people. Sharing a home does not mean they will feel the same way about departures. Record both dogs and discuss new behavior with your vet.
Does having another dog help separation anxiety?
Canine company may reassure some dogs, especially if they struggle with being physically alone. A dog distressed about a particular person's absence may still panic with another dog present. Observe the actual response and continue an appropriate training plan.
Should I separate my dogs when I leave?
Separate areas may be safer if dogs guard resources or interact roughly, but both must be comfortable with the arrangement. Introduce separation gradually while you are home. If either dog panics behind a barrier, arrange company and seek guidance.
Can one dog's separation anxiety affect my other dog?
A distressed dog may crowd, follow or disturb a companion who is trying to rest. Some households may also see tension around space or resources. Film both dogs and provide safe opportunities for each to move away.
How do I train one dog to be alone when I have two?
Begin with tiny absences in the arrangement where both dogs are comfortable, returning before either becomes worried. Practice the dogs being apart separately and gradually while you are home if that is useful. Arrange human company for longer absences and get professional help when needed.
