Does Getting a Second Dog Help Separation Anxiety?

A second dog may offer company but often does not resolve separation anxiety. Learn how to assess a trial, weigh risks, and compare other support.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 24 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Another dog may help some dogs who struggle with being entirely alone, but often does not resolve distress about separation from a particular person. Assess compatible company carefully before committing, continue training, and choose a second dog only if you want and can support both dogs.

  • Friendly play together does not prove comfort when people leave.
  • Trial canine company safely without provoking distress or risking conflict.
  • Plan for two dogs' needs even if anxiety remains unchanged.

Getting your dog a friend can sound like a kind, straightforward answer to lonely workdays. Sometimes canine company makes time at home more enjoyable. But a dog waiting anxiously for a particular person may remain worried with another dog beside them.

We are dog owners, not vets or trainers, and we have not tested products or services ourselves. This guide helps you approach the second-dog decision carefully, with both dogs' welfare and your long-term capacity in mind.

Separation anxiety or isolation distress?

These terms describe useful distinctions, although professionals may use broader separation-related terminology.

Thinking about another dog because your dog is struggling after losing a companion? A new dog is a big decision and not a quick fix. Our guide to dog separation anxiety after losing another pet or person covers what to try first and how to rebuild alone time step by step.

Before you get another dog, find out whether your dog needs company in general or one person. Read our guide to separation anxiety vs isolation distress.

Another big household change is a new baby. Our guide to separation anxiety when a new baby arrives helps you tell normal adjustment from separation stress and plan ahead.

Separation anxiety often involves distress about being apart from a particular person or attachment figure. Another dog may not replace that person.

Isolation distress describes difficulty being entirely alone. Some dogs relax when suitable company is present, which may include a compatible dog. Others need human company.

Boredom is different again: a dog may lack activity without showing panic. Canine company can be enjoyable, but does not replace exercise, rest, enrichment, or human care.

You cannot determine the cause from following behavior alone. Our Velcro Dog guide and Separation Anxiety Signs guide explain what to observe.

When a second dog may help

A compatible companion may help when your dog already enjoys quiet dog company and repeatedly settles better with it during carefully managed observations.

The most useful evidence is comfortable behavior after people leave: loose posture, ordinary resting, and the ability to disengage from the door.

Dogs Trust's separation anxiety advice notes that finding a friend to keep an anxious dog company can be tempting, and that while canine company may be reassuring for your dog, it won't solve the separation anxiety.

Companionship can therefore be supportive without being a treatment. Continue the first dog's plan and check that the other dog is comfortable too.

When a second dog usually does not help

Be cautious when your dog remains distressed despite another person or dog being present, or when concern centers on one person's departure.

A second dog is also a poor starting solution when your dog fears other dogs, guards resources, has unpredictable interactions, or is already overwhelmed by change.

A dog who plays happily with visitors may still panic after the owner leaves. Social compatibility and separation comfort are separate questions.

If distress is severe or the cause unclear, involve your vet and a qualified behavior professional before adding another animal.

Risks to think about

You may end up caring for two stressed dogs. A newcomer can become unsettled by pacing or vocalizing and may copy some anxious behaviors. That does not mean anxiety is inevitably “contagious,” but the household atmosphere can affect both animals.

Conflict, resource guarding, extra barking, and difficulty resting may appear even when introductions initially seem friendly.

A new dog also needs individual attention and alone-time skills. If the dogs become unable to cope apart, veterinary appointments and separate outings become harder.

Adding care demands can reduce the time and money available for the first dog's training. Consider whether you could manage both dogs if the expected benefit never appears.

Test it first: a friend's dog trial

Do not leave unfamiliar dogs loose together to run an experiment. If either dog has aggression, guarding, or severe panic concerns, arrange professional guidance before a trial.

  1. Choose an appropriate friend. Discuss using a calm, healthy adult dog with suitable vaccination status and a known, compatible relationship with yours. Obtain the owner's agreement.
  2. Meet with both owners present. Begin in a suitable neutral setting, such as a quiet parallel walk, without forcing greetings.
  3. Introduce the home gradually. Give each dog space, water, and a separate resting option. Remove shared food, chews, and prized toys.
  4. Observe calm coexistence. Look for voluntary resting and easy disengagement. Repeated avoidance, stiffening, or pestering means the setup needs reassessment.
  5. Plan safe separation between dogs. For any brief human departure, use secure separate areas only if both dogs already accept them comfortably. Do not use a camera as protection against a fight.
  6. Make a tiny observation if safe. Both owners should remain immediately available, watch live, and return before worry develops. If no safe arrangement exists, skip this step.
  7. Compare with existing footage. Use similar circumstances and absence lengths; do not provoke a distressed solo recording simply for comparison.
  8. Review both dogs' responses. Repeat only comfortable observations as appropriate. Stop if either dog struggles, and discuss the findings with your professional.

A supervised daycare trial offers information about comfort with dogs and staff, but cannot isolate the effect of canine company alone.

Fostering or foster-to-adopt may be an option through a rescue with full disclosure and support. A foster dog needs care for their own sake, not simply a temporary test of your resident dog's anxiety.

What to look for on the video

Watch both dogs, including before departure and after any initial activity ends.

Useful observations include resting, ordinary movement, comfortable distance, and recovery after sounds. Concerning patterns include pacing, rigid door watching, drooling, whining, blocking access, stiff approaches, or one dog repeatedly avoiding the other.

Quietness alone is not enough. Neither is a short period of play.

Record what you actually see, when it starts, and what remains outside the camera's view. Our How to Record Your Dog When You Leave guide explains safe setup and honest interpretation.

Choosing a companion dog

If you want a second dog independently of the anxiety question, prioritize compatibility over appearance or an assumed calming personality.

A calm adult may be easier to assess than a puppy, whose needs and behavior will change substantially. However, an adult's temperament still varies by environment.

Consider energy, play style, physical size, health, rest needs, and comfort around resources. Similar size alone does not establish compatibility.

Arrange multiple meetings and ask a rescue about supported introductions and trial terms. Be transparent about existing separation-related concerns and your care arrangements.

The first weeks with two dogs

Provide separate sleeping and retreat spaces, feed separately, and supervise interactions. Give each dog individual time with you.

Offer food toys in separate secure spaces to reduce competition. Do not expect dogs to negotiate access to valued items without help.

Do not leave them alone together until their relationship and safety have been carefully assessed. Separate areas may remain appropriate, provided neither dog becomes distressed behind the barrier.

Continue the first dog's training and introduce comfortable independence for the newcomer. Our Separation Anxiety Training Plan remains relevant even if companionship appears helpful.

Costs and time

A second dog can mean a 10–15-year commitment, sometimes longer, although age and lifespan vary. An adult adoption may involve fewer remaining years but still substantial responsibility.

Budget for routine and unexpected veterinary care, food, equipment, grooming where needed, training, and care during work or travel.

Sitters or daycare may charge for both dogs. Separate walks, feeding, appointments, and training can increase daily time demands.

Ask yourself whether you would still want and support the newcomer if the first dog's anxiety stayed the same.

Before you get a second dog

Does Getting a Second Dog Help Separation Anxiety?: before you get a second dog

  • I want another dog for their own sake.
  • My current dog's health and behavior concerns are being assessed.
  • I understand which observations remain uncertain.
  • My dog has shown comfort around compatible dogs.
  • We have assessed calm resting, not only play.
  • I can provide separate feeding and retreat spaces.
  • I can manage introductions without forcing interaction.
  • I have considered guarding or conflict risks.
  • I can continue the first dog's training.
  • I can teach the newcomer comfortable independence.
  • I can afford care for both dogs if anxiety persists.
  • Workday and travel coverage is realistic.
  • Other household members agree.
  • Any rescue trial arrangement is clear and welfare-focused.

If several answers are uncertain, pause the decision. Waiting can be the more practical choice.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Adopting immediately after one friendly play session.
  • Expecting a puppy to reassure a distressed adult.
  • Leaving unfamiliar dogs together to test the idea.
  • Offering shared chews or food during early trials.
  • Interpreting silence as comfort.
  • Stopping training because another dog is present.
  • Scolding or punishing either dog for distress or conflict.
  • Treating foster care as a risk-free experiment.

A companion deserves a stable place in the household, not responsibility for treating another dog's fear.

When to get extra help

Contact your vet for sudden changes, illness signs, severe distress, or injury. A qualified, reward-based behavior professional can help assess compatibility and interpret trial footage.

Seek help before proceeding if there is guarding, threatening behavior, or repeated inability to settle around other dogs.

For dangerous escape attempts or self-injury, prioritize safety and suitable care rather than another companion trial.

Bottom line

Canine company may reassure some dogs, but another dog often does not address distress about separation from a person.

Assess safely, continue training, and compare simpler care options. Bring home a second dog because you want to care for both, even if the anxiety remains.

Frequently Asked Questions: A Second Dog and Separation Anxiety

Will getting another dog help my dog's separation anxiety?
Another dog may provide reassurance, but often does not resolve distress about being separated from a particular person. Some dogs cope better with suitable canine company, which needs careful assessment. Continue the existing care and training plan either way.
Is it better to get a puppy or an adult as a second dog?
A calm adult may be easier to assess for compatibility, while a puppy brings intensive care and developing behavior. Neither is automatically suitable. Choose around both dogs' needs rather than expecting the newcomer to reduce anxiety.
Can two dogs both get separation anxiety?
Yes, both dogs can experience separation-related distress, and a newcomer may become unsettled by the resident dog's behavior. They may also struggle when separated from each other. Plan individual care and independence practice for both.
How can I test if my dog does better with another dog?
Arrange gradual, supervised introductions with a compatible familiar dog, then seek advice about any safe, very brief absence observation. Do not leave unfamiliar dogs loose together or provoke panic for comparison. Review both dogs' body language and existing footage, not just the noise level.
What can I do instead of getting a second dog?
Use suitable human care cover, a gradual separation training plan, and veterinary or qualified behavior support. Daycare may suit some dogs, while walkers help only when the remaining gaps are comfortable. Household schedule changes can also reduce difficult absences.
