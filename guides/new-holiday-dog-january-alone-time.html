A New Dog Over the Holidays: Getting Ready for the First Day Back at Work

Help your new holiday dog prepare for January with tiny practice absences, a workday countdown, daytime care and a calm plan for your first day back.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 31 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Start tiny, comfortable alone-time practice during the holiday break, rather than waiting for your first workday. Book daytime care before January, and let your dog's behavior—not the return-to-work date—decide how quickly absences increase.

  • Trial daytime caregivers before work and school resume.

A new dog can become part of the holiday routine quickly: breakfast together, children nearby and someone available almost all day. January may bring a very different household, with work bags by the door and everyone leaving at once.

Whether your dog arrived through adoption or as a gift, a holiday dog is a lovely start, and you can prepare without rushing or blaming yourself. We are dog owners who researched this topic, not vets, trainers or product testers, and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. This guide focuses on the holiday-to-work transition and arranging support while your dog learns.

Why a holiday adoption can lead to a hard January

Your new dog has only experienced life with your family as it is now. They may not know that quiet weekdays, departures and returns are part of the routine.

Visitors and holiday excitement can hide that gap. A dog may seem comfortable because someone is always nearby, without having experienced even a brief period completely alone.

The ASPCA's page on separation anxiety describes separation anxiety as distress when dogs are left alone or separated from the people they're attached to, and explains that treatment focuses on gradually teaching the dog to be comfortable alone.

A new dog crying after departure does not automatically have separation anxiety. They may need a potty break, feel uncomfortable or be unsettled. Recording what happens helps you respond to the actual problem instead of assuming they will simply adjust once January starts.

The parties, visitors and New Year's Eve fireworks of the season bring their own challenges; our holiday season guide has a December planner for those, so this guide stays on the path from the holiday break to the first workday.

The first days at home

Begin with a veterinary check, predictable meals, suitable toilet opportunities and a quiet resting spot. Keep introductions manageable and give your dog time to sleep.

Choose a safe area where your dog already appears comfortable. Introduce gates or a pen while you are present, rather than making the first workday their first experience behind a barrier.

Let different trusted adults gradually share feeding, walks and calm company. This makes daytime care less of a sudden handover later.

You do not need to begin formal departures immediately on arrival. First notice whether your dog can rest, eat and explore comfortably. Small movements away can begin where they remain relaxed.

For broader settling support, see our rescue dog anxiety guide and our puppy separation anxiety guide. A young puppy's developmental and toilet needs remain important even when they appear emotionally comfortable alone.

Start tiny alone-time practice during the break

Choose a calm moment after your dog's needs have been met. Start with an easy movement away: a step across the room, briefly out of sight or a moment outside the door.

These are different challenges. Being comfortable while you enter another room does not prove your dog can manage you leaving the house.

Record practice and watch for relaxed posture and a return to ordinary activity. Build from seconds toward minutes only while your dog stays comfortable. Keep departures and returns friendly and low-key.

Everyday tasks can become practice, but only when they fit the current level. Taking out the trash is not a tiny absence if it takes longer than your dog can manage.

Return before worry develops whenever possible. If your dog becomes distressed, return calmly and make the next attempt easier. Do not wait outside for panic to stop.

Book daytime care before January

Contact potential caregivers during the break. Options include family, a sitter, a suitable daycare or a walker as part of a wider plan.

Young puppies need frequent potty breaks and company during the day, so a young puppy should not be left alone for a full workday. Before work and school restart, plan a walker, sitter, daycare or a family member to cover the day. Discuss age, health and vaccination considerations with your vet and prospective caregiver before choosing daycare.

A walker can provide a toilet break and activity. One visit does not cover a dog who panics immediately after everyone leaves, or a puppy who needs more frequent support.

Arrange trial visits while you are nearby. Check that your dog accepts the person and that the caregiver understands their current comfortable absence, toilet routine and distress signs.

Write down who covers each part of the day, including commutes and school activities. Have backup help for cancellations or delayed returns.

The first workday plan

Pack bags and prepare the dog's supplies the night before. Allow a calm morning with a toilet break, breakfast and a suitable sniff walk or age-appropriate sniffing activity. Give your dog time to settle afterward.

Use only the departure steps already practiced comfortably. Your short absence ladder might include moving away, touching the door and stepping outside—but workday morning is not the time to climb to a new duration.

If your dog cannot manage the gap before the caregiver arrives, have that person arrive before you leave. Someone should also check in around midday, with additional or continuous care wherever your dog's needs require it.

Test the camera beforehand and agree who responds if it shows distress. Watching from work cannot replace someone able to reach your dog.

Keep your return calm and warm. Offer a toilet opportunity and ordinary company before reviewing what worked.

The first weeks of January

Keep a simple log of practice duration, caregiver coverage and what happens on video. Note when your dog rests, investigates a toy, watches the door or begins pacing.

Look for trends rather than judging everything by one session. Progress may mean easier departures, less door watching or a quicker return to resting before it means a longer absence.

Eating a food toy is useful information, but not proof your dog stays comfortable once it is empty. A silent dog may also be tense or frozen, so review posture as well as sound.

Increase practice gradually when your dog remains relaxed. Keep easier sessions in the mix, and avoid treating weekends as constant company followed by an abrupt Monday absence.

If a session goes badly, return to a comfortable step and review what changed. Poor sleep, unusual activity or a difficult caregiver handover may affect the day.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

Contact the vet early if your dog refuses food, seems unwell or shows a sudden change. New accidents should not automatically be blamed on anxiety.

House-soiling only during absences is worth discussing alongside footage and toilet records. Young puppies still need appropriate toilet opportunities, so the timing of care matters too.

Strong distress, repeated escape attempts, injury or an inability to settle need prompt help. A qualified reward-based behavior professional can help when practice repeatedly triggers panic or there is no progress. Our separation anxiety red flags guide explains which signs are urgent.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

Never shut a panicking dog in a crate, pen or room and leave. Change the care arrangement while seeking advice. Do not punish crying, damage or accidents after returning.

You do not have to wait until the first workday goes badly to ask for support.

Bottom line

Frequently Asked Questions: A New Holiday Dog and the First Workday

How do I get my new holiday dog ready for me going back to work?
Introduce a predictable daily routine, practice tiny comfortable absences and record what happens. Rehearse the coming morning schedule and trial caregivers before your first workday. Arrange company for any gap your dog cannot yet manage.
When should I start leaving my new dog alone?
Begin easy moments apart once your dog is comfortable enough to rest and explore, without rushing their first days. Start with seconds if necessary and build only while they remain relaxed. A necessary long absence needs care cover rather than an accelerated practice session.
Can I leave a new puppy alone all day when I go back to work?
Do not plan a full workday alone for a young puppy. They need frequent potty breaks and company, so arrange a family member, sitter or suitable daytime care, with a walker as part of the plan where appropriate. Their needs determine the coverage.
Why is my new dog crying when I leave after the holidays?
They may be unsettled by the change, need something practical or feel distressed about separation. Record a manageable departure and check toilet, health and comfort needs. Persistent distress deserves veterinary and qualified behavior support rather than longer absences.
What if my new dog still can't be alone when January starts?
Keep daytime care in place and continue practice at a comfortable level. Ask your vet and a qualified reward-based behavior professional for help if distress is strong or progress has stalled. The work calendar does not determine when your dog is ready.
