Dog Nighttime Anxiety: Causes, Signs & Calming Tips

Your dog settles down for the night, but an hour later, the pacing starts. Perhaps they whine beside your bed, wander between rooms, or repeatedly wake you for reassurance.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 15 Oct 202618 min read
Quick answer

Dog nighttime anxiety describes fear, distress, or difficulty settling during the evening or overnight. Dogs may pace, whine, pant, seek attention, or repeatedly wake up. A predictable bedtime routine, appropriate exercise, a final toilet break, and a comfortable sleeping space can help. However, new or persistent symptoms—especially in senior dogs—should be assessed for pain, illness, or cognitive changes.

Dog nighttime anxiety can affect puppies, adult dogs, and seniors. Sometimes the cause is loneliness, unfamiliar sounds, or a disrupted routine. In other cases, nighttime restlessness is a sign of pain, illness, or age-related cognitive changes.

The important thing is to understand why your dog is struggling before trying to calm them.

This guide explains common causes of nighttime anxiety in dogs, how to create a more comfortable bedtime routine, which calming products may help, and when nighttime behavior deserves veterinary attention.

Why Dogs Get Anxious at Night

A restless dog is not necessarily being difficult or demanding attention. Nighttime behavior can reflect emotional distress, physical discomfort, normal developmental needs, or a combination of factors.

Understanding the pattern can help you identify the next step.

Several causes can occur together. For example, an older dog with arthritis and reduced vision may struggle more at night because moving around is uncomfortable and the environment is harder to navigate.

1. Loneliness and separation anxiety

Some dogs sleep comfortably while their owners are nearby but become distressed when separated.

A recently adopted dog might struggle with unfamiliar sleeping arrangements. Another dog may have a history of separation anxiety that becomes particularly noticeable at bedtime.

Signs can include whining when a bedroom door closes, scratching at barriers, pacing, or settling only when a familiar person returns.

However, wanting to sleep close to you is not automatically separation anxiety.

A dog who happily rests near your bed may simply prefer companionship. The concern is repeated, significant distress during separation.

If anxiety also occurs when you leave the house during the day, read our dog separation anxiety guide for a gradual training approach.

2. Nighttime noises and environmental triggers

The house may seem quiet to you, but your dog can notice sounds from traffic, wildlife, neighboring homes, thunderstorms, or fireworks.

Dogs with noise sensitivity may become particularly alert when unexpected sounds interrupt sleep.

Watch for trembling, scanning toward windows, hiding, repetitive barking, or sudden panting following a noise.

Try reducing outside stimulation with closed curtains, a quieter sleeping location, and gentle background sound, such as a Yogasleep Dohm white noise machine.

Do not force your dog to remain near a frightening sound. If they choose a safe hiding spot, allow them to use it.

3. Changes in daily routine

Dogs often benefit from predictable activity and rest.

Changes in work hours, moving to a new home, a new baby, household guests, or sleeping in an unfamiliar room can temporarily disrupt nighttime behavior.

Some dogs become more active at night when they have had limited daytime enrichment. Others struggle because late-evening activity leaves them too excited to settle.

The solution is not necessarily more exercise immediately before bed.

Instead, provide appropriate activity throughout the day, then establish a gradual evening wind-down.

4. Pain, arthritis, or other physical discomfort

A dog who repeatedly stands up, circles, lies down, and gets up again may be physically uncomfortable.

Arthritis, back pain, dental problems, digestive discomfort, and other medical conditions can interfere with sleep.

This is particularly important in older dogs, but pain can affect dogs of any age.

Watch for stiffness after resting, difficulty climbing stairs, hesitation before lying down, limping, or sensitivity to touch.

VCA Animal Hospitals identifies nighttime restlessness and difficulty getting comfortable among possible signs of pain in aging dogs.

If your dog suddenly begins pacing or crying at night, arrange a veterinary examination rather than assuming the behavior is simply anxiety.

5. Vision or hearing loss

Dogs with declining vision may have difficulty navigating dark rooms or locating familiar sleeping areas.

Changes in hearing can also affect how they respond to their environment. Some dogs startle more easily when approached unexpectedly.

Helpful adjustments include a gentle night light, clear walking routes, and a consistent furniture arrangement.

Avoid frequently moving your dog's bed or creating obstacles in familiar pathways.

A night light may improve orientation, but it will not treat the underlying medical cause.

6. Canine cognitive dysfunction in senior dogs

Some older dogs experience changes in memory, orientation, social behavior, and sleep patterns.

Canine cognitive dysfunction (CCD), sometimes called dog dementia, can cause dogs to sleep more during the day and remain restless at night.

However, nighttime wandering alone does not confirm dementia. Pain, sensory changes, urinary problems, and other illnesses can produce similar symptoms.

We'll explain the distinction below.

Dog Nighttime Anxiety vs. Dementia (CCD)

Dog nighttime anxiety and canine cognitive dysfunction can overlap, but they are not the same condition.

Nighttime anxiety describes distress or difficulty settling. CCD is an age-related condition affecting cognitive function and behavior.

A younger dog who becomes upset when sleeping alone may need gradual independence training. An older dog who becomes disoriented in familiar rooms may need a veterinary cognitive assessment.

Signs that make CCD worth investigating

  • Wandering or pacing without an obvious destination.
  • Getting stuck behind furniture or near doorways.
  • Staring at walls or appearing confused in familiar rooms.
  • Sleeping extensively during the day but staying awake at night.
  • Changes in interactions with familiar people.
  • New house-training difficulties.
  • Reduced ability to follow previously familiar routines.

Veterinarians may use a framework called DISHAA to assess patterns involving disorientation, interactions, sleep-wake changes, house-soiling, activity, and anxiety.

These signs can have other causes, so a diagnosis requires veterinary evaluation.

Nighttime Routine That Actually Helps

A useful bedtime routine should meet your dog's physical needs, reduce avoidable stress, and create a predictable transition from activity to rest.

There is no exact schedule that works for every dog. Use the following sequence as a starting point and adapt it to your dog's age, health, and daily routine.

Step 1: Provide appropriate daytime activity

Dogs need physical exercise, mental enrichment, and opportunities to rest.

Depending on age and health, this might include walks, sniffing activities, gentle games, short training sessions, or suitable food puzzles.

The goal is to meet your dog's needs, not exhaust them.

A senior dog with arthritis may benefit more from several gentle outings than one strenuous walk. A puppy needs age-appropriate activity and frequent rest.

Step 2: Begin winding down before bedtime

Reduce high-energy games and exciting activity as bedtime approaches.

Keep household activity relatively quiet and predictable.

Offer a familiar resting location and allow your dog to settle naturally.

For dogs sensitive to environmental noises, close curtains and consider a quiet interior room.

Step 3: Offer a final toilet opportunity

A last bathroom break can reduce nighttime waking caused by a full bladder.

Keep it calm rather than turning it into an energetic walk.

Puppies may still need overnight toilet breaks, while some senior dogs require additional opportunities because of age-related or medical changes.

Do not withhold normal access to drinking water to prevent overnight urination. Increased thirst or frequent urination deserves veterinary assessment.

Step 4: Prepare a comfortable sleeping space

Choose an area where your dog can rest without excessive noise, heat, cold, or interruptions.

Consider:

  • A comfortable bed appropriate for the dog's size and mobility.
  • Non-slip flooring for older dogs.
  • A suitable night light when vision is reduced.
  • Familiar bedding or a safe resting mat.
  • Easy access to water.
  • A sleeping location that does not create unnecessary separation distress.

An orthopedic bed (such as the FurHaven Deluxe Orthopedic Dog Bed) may be helpful for a dog who struggles with pressure on painful joints, although bedding cannot replace medical treatment. Our calming bed vs orthopedic bed guide covers sizing and washable options.

A crate can be a comfortable sleeping space for dogs already accustomed to it. However, confinement may worsen distress in a dog who fears being enclosed.

Never force a panicking dog into a crate as a nighttime-anxiety treatment.

Step 5: Make bedtime interactions reassuring and consistent

Use a calm voice and familiar cues.

You do not need to ignore a distressed dog to prevent dependency. Gentle reassurance is appropriate when a dog is frightened or uncomfortable.

At the same time, avoid turning every ordinary nighttime awakening into an exciting interaction.

Keep necessary check-ins quiet, brief, and focused on what your dog needs.

If your dog repeatedly becomes distressed when you move away, introduce gradual independence training during the day rather than using bedtime as a prolonged training challenge.

Step 6: Respond thoughtfully to nighttime waking

When your dog wakes and begins whining or pacing, check for immediate needs.

Does the dog need a bathroom break? Are they frightened by a noise? Are they having difficulty getting comfortable?

For a dog with known medical issues, consider whether the behavior reflects discomfort.

If your dog is calm after a quick check, help them return to their familiar resting area.

Avoid punishment, shouting, or forcing them to remain in a location associated with distress.

Step 7: Track patterns over several nights

Keep a simple observation record.

Products such as VetriScience Composure and Zylkene are sometimes considered for anxiety-related behavior.

Puppy Nighttime Crying: What's Normal and What Helps?

Puppy crying at night is common during adjustment to a new home, but it should not automatically be dismissed as attention-seeking.

Young puppies may wake because they need to urinate, feel unfamiliar with their surroundings, are hungry, or have difficulty settling without the companionship they previously experienced.

Practical steps for a crying puppy

Keep the sleeping area comfortable and close enough that you can hear genuine distress or toilet needs.

Offer a calm final bathroom break before bedtime.

Use familiar bedding, a predictable routine, and age-appropriate settling activities.

If your puppy wakes and cries, check whether they need to go outside. Keep the interaction quiet and return them to their sleeping space afterward.

Start practicing very brief, comfortable separations during the day. Increase independence gradually rather than using prolonged nighttime crying as training.

Should you ignore a puppy crying at night?

There is no universal waiting rule.

A puppy may have an immediate physical need or may be frightened by an unfamiliar environment. Respond according to the situation rather than automatically waiting a fixed number of minutes.

Do not force a puppy to remain in prolonged panic.

If crying is persistent, intense, or accompanied by vomiting, diarrhea, unusual breathing, or other symptoms, contact your veterinarian.

Senior Dog Restless at Night: What to Check First

When an older dog suddenly starts crying, pacing, or waking throughout the night, medical causes deserve particular attention.

Senior dogs can experience several overlapping problems.

Pain and arthritis

Older dogs may find it difficult to change positions or get comfortable on a sleeping surface.

Signs such as stiffness, reluctance to stand, difficulty lying down, or repeated position changes should prompt veterinary assessment.

A supportive bed may improve comfort, but treatment should address the cause of pain.

Sensory decline

Reduced vision or hearing can make navigation more challenging, especially in dim rooms.

A gentle night light, clear pathways, and consistent furniture placement may help.

Avoid startling your dog when approaching them in the dark.

Urinary or other medical conditions

Frequent nighttime urination, increased thirst, coughing, digestive discomfort, or changes in breathing may interrupt sleep.

These symptoms should not be managed solely with calming products.

Your veterinarian can assess whether an underlying illness is contributing to restlessness.

Canine cognitive dysfunction

A senior dog who wanders aimlessly, becomes disoriented, stares at walls, or develops a markedly altered sleep-wake cycle may need evaluation for CCD.

A diagnosis should not be based on age or nighttime crying alone.

For a full care plan, read How to Calm a Dog With Dementia at Night.

Veterinarians may discuss environmental changes, nutritional management, and prescription treatment. Selegiline, also known by the brand name Anipryl, is one veterinary prescription option for appropriately diagnosed canine cognitive dysfunction.

It is not an over-the-counter sleep aid, and suitability must be determined by a veterinarian.

Breed-specific senior nighttime concerns

Different breeds have different mobility, exercise, and environmental needs, but nighttime distress should never be assumed to be normal for a particular breed.

When to See Your Vet

A dog who occasionally wakes because of an unfamiliar sound may simply need reassurance and a quieter sleeping area.

However, repeated nighttime restlessness can also signal a medical or behavioral problem that needs treatment.

Arrange a veterinary appointment if your dog:

  • Begins crying or pacing at night without an obvious explanation.
  • Has repeated difficulty lying down or finding a comfortable position.
  • Develops increased thirst or frequent nighttime urination.
  • Shows stiffness, limping, or changes in mobility.
  • Appears confused or disoriented in familiar rooms.
  • Experiences substantial changes in sleep patterns.
  • Develops new anxiety alongside appetite or behavioral changes.
  • Continues to experience distress despite reasonable adjustments.

For a senior dog, new symptoms should be assessed promptly rather than waiting weeks to see whether they disappear.

Seek urgent veterinary care if:

Your dog has difficulty breathing, collapses, experiences seizures, shows severe pain, has a swollen or painful abdomen with unproductive retching, or suddenly becomes severely disoriented or unable to stand.

These signs require medical attention rather than home calming methods.

What information should you bring?

A short video of the behavior, a list of medications and supplements, and notes about sleep, appetite, drinking, urination, mobility, and nighttime triggers can help your veterinarian.

The goal is to identify the underlying problem—not simply make your dog quieter.

The Bottom Line: Find the Cause Before Choosing the Solution

Dog nighttime anxiety is not one single problem with one universal treatment.

For a puppy, the solution may involve reassurance, toilet breaks, and gradual adjustment to sleeping independently.

For an adult dog, a predictable routine, quieter environment, and appropriate enrichment may improve nighttime comfort.

For a senior dog, pain, sensory changes, medical illness, and cognitive dysfunction must be considered before relying on calming products.

A comfortable bed, sound masking, or an optional pheromone product may make the environment easier to tolerate, but none replaces appropriate diagnosis and care.

The goal is not just to stop your dog from crying at night. It is to understand what is keeping them awake and help them feel genuinely comfortable and safe.

Dog Nighttime Anxiety: Frequently Asked Questions

What is dog nighttime anxiety?
Dog nighttime anxiety refers to distress, fear, or difficulty settling during the evening or overnight. Affected dogs may pace, whine, pant, seek reassurance, or repeatedly wake. The cause can involve emotional stress, environmental triggers, developmental needs, pain, or age-related health changes.
Why is my dog anxious at night but fine during the day?
Nighttime can introduce different triggers, including unfamiliar sounds, darkness, separation from family members, and reduced activity. Pain or discomfort may also become more noticeable when a dog attempts to rest. If this is a new or persistent pattern, arrange veterinary assessment.
Why does my dog cry at night?
Dogs may cry because they need a toilet break, are frightened, feel separated from familiar people, or are physically uncomfortable. Puppies commonly need more frequent nighttime care, while senior dogs may have pain, sensory changes, or cognitive issues. Persistent crying should not automatically be treated as attention-seeking.
How to calm a dog at night?
Start with a predictable evening routine, appropriate daytime activity, a final bathroom break, and a comfortable sleeping area. Reduce noise and visual triggers, offer gentle reassurance, and observe what happens before the distress begins. If your dog continues to struggle, seek veterinary guidance.
What causes a senior dog to be restless at night?
A senior dog restless at night may be experiencing arthritis pain, urinary problems, reduced vision, hearing changes, or canine cognitive dysfunction. Several conditions can occur together. Veterinary evaluation is especially important when nighttime pacing or crying is new, persistent, or accompanied by disorientation.
Is puppy crying at night normal?
Some nighttime crying is common while puppies adjust to a new environment and develop bladder control. However, frequent or intense crying may indicate a toilet need, discomfort, fear, or illness. Provide age-appropriate care, reassurance, and gradual independence training rather than forcing prolonged distress.
Can calming chews help with dog nighttime anxiety?
Some supplements may support relaxation in certain dogs, but clinical evidence varies by ingredient and formulation. Calming chews cannot reliably treat pain, cognitive dysfunction, or serious separation anxiety. Ask your veterinarian before using supplements, particularly for puppies, seniors, or dogs taking medications.
When should I take my dog to the vet for nighttime anxiety?
Book a veterinary appointment if nighttime anxiety is new, persistent, worsening, or associated with pain, excessive thirst, urinary changes, confusion, or difficulty settling. Senior dogs deserve particular attention because age-related medical conditions can affect sleep. Sudden severe distress, breathing difficulty, collapse, or major neurological changes require urgent care.
