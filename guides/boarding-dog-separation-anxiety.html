Boarding a Dog With Separation Anxiety: Is a Kennel a Good Idea?

Considering boarding a dog with separation anxiety? Compare kennels and sitters, ask about overnight care, plan a trial stay and pack familiar comforts.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 24 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Boarding can work for some dogs with separation anxiety if they feel comfortable with caregivers and receive enough company, including overnight. Others cope better with an in-home sitter; choose through short trials, and rethink any arrangement that causes persistent distress or unsafe escape attempts.

  • Ask who stays overnight and how much time your dog spends alone.
  • Try a short daytime visit before an overnight or longer stay.
  • Choose care around your dog’s comfort, not the facility’s appearance.

Booking a trip gets complicated when your dog struggles without you. A boarding facility offers caregivers and a daily routine, but it may also bring unfamiliar dogs, noise and long stretches alone after staff leave.

The best choice depends on what your dog finds difficult and what the facility actually provides. We are dog owners, not vets or trainers. We have not assessed these facilities ourselves, and the products below are chosen from research, not from personal testing. This guide helps you ask useful questions, organize trials and recognize when another arrangement would be kinder.

Can a dog with separation anxiety go to boarding?

Sometimes, yes. A dog who struggles mainly with being physically alone may relax with a familiar caregiver. A dog distressed by separation from one particular person may still struggle despite company.

Not sure whether to board your dog or bring them along? Our guide to traveling with a dog who has separation anxiety helps you decide, with a table of places to stay and a first-day settle-in plan.

The ASPCA’s separation anxiety advice notes that most dogs with separation anxiety are fine as long as someone is with them. During treatment, it advises not leaving the dog alone except during training sessions, and suggests alternatives such as a family member, friend or dog sitter staying with your dog, or taking your dog to a sitter’s house or a doggy daycare.

That does not establish that any particular kennel will suit your dog. Staff being somewhere on the property is different from someone being with them.

When boarding may work

Boarding is worth considering when your dog comfortably accepts other caregivers, rests in unfamiliar places and tolerates the facility’s surroundings.

Look for individualized care: quiet rest, suitable exercise, optional rather than compulsory group play, and a clear response if your dog struggles.

Overnight arrangements matter especially. Ask exactly when staff leave, who remains on site and whether anyone can provide the company your dog needs.

When boarding is usually not a good fit

Rethink boarding if your dog repeatedly panics behind barriers, cannot rest around barking dogs or becomes frightened by unfamiliar handling.

A busy facility may be overwhelming even for a dog who enjoys meeting people. Likewise, enthusiastic play during the day does not establish comfort alone in a run at night.

Severe distress deserves veterinary and behavior support before a long stay. See our Separation Anxiety Signs guide if you are unsure what to watch for.

How to choose a boarding facility

Describe your dog honestly before booking. Explain what happens when you leave, whether another person helps, and any history of confinement distress or escape attempts.

Tour the sleeping accommodation as well as play areas. Look for comfortable temperatures, accessible water, clean bedding, secure exits and places to rest away from activity.

Ask about vaccination requirements and health checks well ahead of time, then confirm suitability with your vet. Get the care arrangement and emergency plan in writing.

Questions to ask a boarding facility

  • Have you cared for dogs with similar separation difficulties?
  • Who will handle my dog each day?
  • Who is physically on site overnight?
  • How long is my dog alone between visits?
  • Can someone provide company if they cannot settle?
  • Where exactly will my dog sleep?
  • Is quieter accommodation available?
  • Is group play optional?
  • How do you assess compatibility between dogs?
  • What happens if my dog refuses food?
  • How do you respond to pacing, howling or escape attempts?
  • What handling and training methods do staff use?
  • What vaccination records and health checks are required?
  • Can you follow written veterinary instructions?
  • What bedding and toys may I bring?
  • What updates can you provide?
  • Who contacts the vet in an emergency?
  • Can my backup person collect my dog early?

Red flags on a tour

Be cautious about vague answers concerning overnight staffing or alone time. “Someone checks them” needs a clear explanation.

Avoid any facility that uses punishment, anti-bark devices or forced greetings, or claims that distressed dogs simply need to get used to it.

Other concerns include unsafe fencing, poor ventilation, inaccessible water or staff dismissing food refusal and repeated escape attempts. A polished reception area cannot compensate for inadequate care.

The trial stay

Start early enough to change plans. A “daycare day” should begin with a manageable visit rather than an automatic full day. Progress only when your dog shows comfort, including during quiet periods.

What to pack

Check first: some facilities limit bedding and toys or provide their own.

  • Your dog’s usual food, measured and labeled.
  • Feeding instructions and dietary restrictions.
  • An allowed familiar washable bed.
  • An approved spare towel or home-scented fabric.
  • Approved, intact toys your dog already uses safely.
  • Written instructions for any vet-prescribed treatment.
  • Required vaccination and health records.
  • Your vet’s contact details.
  • Your emergency contact information.
  • A local backup person authorized to collect.
  • A secure, well-fitting leash and harness.
  • Current identification and microchip information.
  • A short behavior and handling care sheet.
  • A list of packed belongings.

Avoid fabric items your dog might shred or swallow. If your vet has prescribed anything, supply it as the facility requires with clear written instructions.

Drop-off day

Keep the morning familiar. Offer a suitable walk, sniffing time and a potty opportunity without exhausting your dog.

Use the caregiver and handover routine from successful trials. Share any new health or behavior changes before leaving, and give a brief, kind goodbye.

If your dog is clearly panicking, pause and discuss the backup plan. Do not ask staff to drag them into accommodation or assume the distress will disappear once you drive away.

While you are away

Agree on update frequency and what triggers an immediate call. Ask about eating, drinking, toileting, sleep and time spent alone—not only playtime.

A short clip of relaxed resting can be more informative than a lively play video. Ask staff to report difficulties honestly rather than describe everything as “fine.”

Confirm what happens if the setup stops working: extra appropriate support, veterinary advice or collection by your backup person. Keep that person reachable.

Pick-up and the first days home

Ask for a factual summary before leaving: appetite, sleep, accidents, unusual behavior and any incidents. Check belongings and mention any apparent discomfort promptly.

At home, offer water, familiar meals, ordinary potty opportunities and quiet rest. Avoid immediately scheduling a long absence.

Review current comfort with short, manageable separations. Our Separation Anxiety After Vacation guide explains readjustment, while the training plan covers gradual absence work.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not choose solely by appearance, group-play videos or the promise of “constant supervision.” Ask what those terms mean.

Avoid hiding escape attempts or handling concerns to secure a booking. Staff need accurate information to protect your dog.

Do not introduce a new crate or unfamiliar equipment immediately before the trip. Never assume that a tired dog is comfortable, or that eating one meal establishes a successful stay.

When to get extra help

Contact your vet promptly for injuries, unsafe escape attempts, severe distress or significant changes in drinking or health.

If your dog has not eaten for more than a day, seek veterinary advice; call sooner for puppies, medically vulnerable dogs or accompanying illness signs. Do not treat that period as a required wait.

Dogs Trust advises avoiding absences beyond what your dog can cope with and using friends, family, sitters or daycare where possible. If boarding cannot provide that support, change the care arrangement.

A qualified behavior professional can help assess options. Bring observations and videos using our Vet Visit Preparation guide.

Frequently Asked Questions: Boarding a Dog With Separation Anxiety

Is it cruel to board a dog with separation anxiety?
Boarding is not automatically unkind, but continuing a stay that causes severe distress is a welfare concern. Some dogs settle with caregivers, while others need a quieter home arrangement. Trials and a realistic backup plan help you choose.
Is a pet sitter better than boarding for an anxious dog?
An in-home sitter may suit a dog who finds familiar surroundings reassuring. However, confirm how much time the sitter actually stays, including overnight. The better option is the one that provides comfortable, appropriate care for your individual dog.
How do I prepare my anxious dog for boarding?
Discuss their needs honestly, check health requirements with your vet and arrange gradual visits before an overnight trial. Send familiar items only where allowed and safe. Leave written care instructions and an early-collection backup plan.
Should I visit or video call my dog during boarding?
Base this on your dog’s response during trials and the caregiver’s observations. Hearing your voice without being able to reach you may unsettle some dogs, and another departure after a visit may be difficult. Staff updates and short recorded clips may be more useful.
Will my dog think I abandoned them?
We cannot know exactly how your dog interprets the stay, so avoid assuming either that they feel abandoned or that they will automatically adjust. Focus on observable comfort, familiar care and manageable separations. If they remain distressed, change the arrangement and seek help.
