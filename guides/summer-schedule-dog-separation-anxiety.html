Summer Break and Vacation Schedule Changes for a Dog With Separation Anxiety

Help your anxious dog through summer schedule changes with short practice absences, care for outings, heat safety and a gentle back-to-school ramp-up.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 31 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Keep short, comfortable practice absences in the summer routine, even when someone is usually home. Plan care for longer outings, protect your dog from heat and gradually bring back school-year routines before August or September departures.

  • Practice comfortable absences a few times a week throughout summer.
  • Book company for outings longer than your dog can manage.
  • Prepare backup care for cooling failures and delayed returns.

Summer rarely follows one schedule. There are slow mornings with everyone home, camp drop-offs, pool afternoons and day trips that run late. For a dog who struggles alone, those changes can quietly remove familiar practice—or create an unexpectedly long absence.

We are dog owners who researched this topic, not vets, trainers or product testers, and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. This guide focuses on maintaining comfortable alone time through summer itself, with practical plans for outings, vacations, hot weather and the gradual return to school and work.

Why summer can make separation anxiety harder

When someone is nearly always home, your dog may get fewer opportunities to experience a comfortable absence. Family company is enjoyable, but the sudden contrast when everyone leaves can be difficult.

Summer also changes departure patterns. One day nobody leaves; the next begins with camp drop-off and ends with dinner out. A dog who copes with a familiar morning absence may struggle with an unfamiliar evening departure.

That does not mean every dog resting near the family has separation anxiety. Watch what happens when people actually leave. Repeated pacing, whining, howling, panting, drooling, exit scratching or an inability to rest suggest distress.

Do not assume summer caused a sudden behavior change. Pain, illness and other discomfort can affect behavior, so new or worsening symptoms deserve veterinary attention.

Keep practicing alone time when everyone is home

Plan a few short sessions each week rather than waiting until you need a long absence. Choose calm moments when your dog's toilet, activity and comfort needs have been met.

For example, after the kids leave for camp, an adult might practice a tiny departure. Another option is a brief family walk without the dog—but only if its duration is comfortably within what the dog can manage.

If your dog worries within seconds, the starting step needs to be smaller. Moving briefly out of sight may be enough, although barriers are difficult for some dogs too.

Record practice and return before worry develops whenever possible. If distress appears, return calmly and make the next attempt easier. Do not wait outside for a panicking dog to become quiet.

Keep ordinary affection and family time. Practice is about safe experiences apart, not ignoring your dog. A calm session also does not automatically justify a much longer outing later that day. The same approach works when snow days keep everyone home; our winter guide adapts it for the cold months.

Camps, day trips and irregular summer days

Put the whole absence on the calendar, including driving, lines, meals and possible delays. Camp drop-off may be short; adding errands afterward can turn it into something your dog cannot manage.

For beach days, pool visits, BBQs and long outings, decide who provides care before the family leaves. Options include a familiar sitter, family member, friend or suitable daycare; our care options guide compares them.

A walker can provide a toilet break and activity, but a short visit does not cover a dog who panics before and after it. Some dogs need someone present throughout the unsupported gap.

Assign one adult to confirm the plan and another as backup where possible. Do not assume a teenager is staying home without checking.

If plans change, contact the caregiver before extending the outing. “We thought we'd be back by now” is exactly the gap a summer backup plan should cover.

Planning a summer vacation around your dog

Decide early whether your dog will come, remain home with a sitter or stay elsewhere. Choose according to where your dog actually feels comfortable, rather than assuming more people or other dogs will help.

Planning an earlier trip in March or April? Our spring break guide covers choosing between bringing your dog and care at home, a spring break week planner and the return to school and work.

Book ahead and arrange a trial visit or stay. Give the caregiver clear notes about alone-time limits, distress signs, daily needs and who to call.

If bringing your dog, plan activities that include company when needed. For hotels, vacation rentals and staying with family, use our guide to traveling with a dog who has separation anxiety; our holiday travel guide also compares bringing the dog, a sitter and boarding.

Keep this summer plan going before and after the trip. If a long stretch together has already made departures difficult, our separation anxiety after vacation guide provides a separate re-entry plan.

Heat safety while you're out

Keep your dog in a reliably cool indoor space with fresh water and a resting place away from direct sun. Check the cooling system before leaving and consider how sunlight moves through the room during the absence.

There is no single safe temperature for every dog. Age, health, coat, body shape and individual needs affect comfort. Ask your vet about your dog's particular risks.

The ASPCA's hot weather safety tips advise giving pets plenty of fresh, clean water and a shady place to get out of the sun, being careful not to over-exercise them and keeping them indoors when it's extremely hot. The page also warns against letting dogs linger on hot asphalt, because sensitive paw pads can burn, and suggests keeping walks to a minimum when it is very hot.

Move suitable outdoor activity to cooler parts of the day, checking conditions rather than relying on the clock alone. On difficult days, use gentle indoor food searches or familiar training games instead of trying to exhaust your dog.

Never leave your dog outside in the heat while you are gone, whether in a yard, a garage or on a porch, even for a short outing.

Have a cooling-failure plan. A nearby adult needs working access and somewhere cool to take your dog safely if necessary. A camera may lose power or Wi-Fi during an outage, so an offline feed needs a real-world check.

Know the signs of overheating. The ASPCA's hot weather safety tips list excessive panting or difficulty breathing, an increased heart and breathing rate, drooling, mild weakness, stupor or even collapse. If you suspect overheating, contact a vet immediately and get your dog into a cool indoor space; difficulty breathing or collapse needs emergency care.

Do not assume heavy panting on camera is anxiety when the room may be hot. Send your backup person to check right away.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

Never leave your dog in the car

Never leave your dog alone in a parked car, in any weather. That includes a quick stop, cracked windows or an engine left running.

The ASPCA's hot weather safety tips say never to leave your animals alone in a parked vehicle: it can lead to fatal heat stroke, and it is illegal in several states. Taking your anxious dog along is only workable when the entire outing includes safe supervision and suitable conditions.

For summer errands, take turns with another adult, use services that let you remain with your dog or arrange care at home. Do not replace an uncomfortable home absence with an unattended-car situation.

The last weeks of summer: ramping up for school and work

Start restoring the coming routine during the last 2–3 weeks of summer, earlier if your dog needs substantial support. This is preparation time, not a deadline for reaching a full school or workday.

Gradually bring walks, meals and rest closer to the expected schedule. Practice at relevant departure times while keeping absences comfortably short.

Use video to judge what is manageable now. Do not increase duration simply because the calendar says school starts soon. Continue easier sessions alongside small increases, and step back when your dog struggles.

Arrange care for the first school or workdays before they arrive. For morning logistics, children's jobs and after-school returns, use our back-to-school guide rather than trying to solve everything through longer practice.

Getting kids involved

Give children simple, supervised jobs: helping fill a water bowl, preparing a food toy with an adult or marking a practice session on the family calendar.

Older children can help notice whether the dog is resting or needs space, but adults remain responsible for training decisions, heat safety and care cover.

Keep greetings and departures friendly and calm. Children should not hug, crowd or follow a worried dog into their resting area.

Let kids enjoy summer with the dog without making them responsible for preventing anxiety. A shared adult plan is more dependable than expecting a child to cancel activities or monitor a camera.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

Contact the vet early if your dog refuses food, seems unwell or develops a sudden behavior change. Strong separation distress also deserves veterinary attention, and our separation anxiety red flags guide explains which signs are urgent.

A qualified reward-based behavior professional can help when departures trigger rapid panic, escape attempts or repeated setbacks. Never shut a panicking dog in a crate or room and leave, and do not punish noise, damage or accidents. Our guide to finding separation anxiety help in the US explains the options.

Frequently Asked Questions: Summer Schedules and Dog Separation Anxiety

Why does my dog get separation anxiety after summer break?
A home-heavy summer can mean fewer comfortable experiences alone, followed by a sudden change when school and work resume. That may make existing difficulties more noticeable or departures harder. Film what happens and ask the vet about sudden changes.
How do I keep my dog used to being alone when we're home all summer?
Plan short, comfortable practice absences a few times a week and record them. Start at a level where your dog stays relaxed, even if that means seconds. Arrange company for outings that exceed their current comfort level.
Can I leave my dog in the car for a few minutes in summer?
No—never leave your dog alone in a parked car, even briefly. Cracked windows or a running engine do not make it safe. Arrange supervision or leave your dog safely at home with appropriate care.
How do I keep my dog safe and cool at home while I'm out in hot weather?
Maintain a cool indoor room, fresh water and a shaded resting spot. Confirm that a nearby adult can check on your dog if the AC, power or camera fails. Suspected overheating needs immediate veterinary advice.
When should I start getting my dog ready for school starting again?
Use the last 2–3 weeks of summer to gradually restore familiar routines, and start earlier for a dog with significant difficulty. Increase practice only while your dog stays comfortable. Book care cover if the coming school-day absence is still too long.
