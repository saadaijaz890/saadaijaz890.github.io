Leaving Your Dog Alone for the First Time: A Calm First-Week Plan

Leave your new dog alone gradually, starting with brief, calm practice. Use this flexible first-week plan for puppies, rescues, and adult dogs alike.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 24 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Start with a few minutes only if your dog stays relaxed; some need seconds or easier indoor practice first. Prepare a familiar safe space, observe on camera, and increase gradually while arranging company for longer absences; stop increasing if your dog becomes distressed.

  • A new dog may need company and reassurance during the first days or weeks.
  • Introduce the resting area, barriers, and food toys before using them during departures.
  • Let comfortable behavior—not the day number—decide your next step.

The first departure can feel like a milestone: your puppy is asleep, your rescue has found a favorite bed, and you wonder whether now is the right moment to step outside. There is no universal first-day duration that fits every dog.

We are dog owners, not vets or trainers, and the products below are chosen from research, not from personal testing. This plan helps you prepare and observe carefully, without treating the first week as a deadline for independent living.

Before the first time you leave

Choose a familiar room or gated area where your dog already rests comfortably. Let them explore it while you remain nearby before closing a barrier or stepping away.

Provide fresh water, a suitable bed, comfortable temperature, and an unhurried potty opportunity. Offer age-appropriate movement and sniffing, then time to settle rather than departing immediately after exciting play.

A food toy can be useful only after you have checked that your dog enjoys it and uses it safely while supervised. Do not introduce new equipment, unfamiliar food, and the first departure together.

Dogs Trust's separation anxiety advice recommends starting with a few minutes and building slowly only while the dog is relaxed. It also recognizes that puppies commonly need support for their first days or weeks and that older newcomers may initially follow their person between rooms.

The ASPCA's separation anxiety guide describes the same idea: plan each absence to be shorter than the time it takes your dog to get upset, start with a few seconds out of sight, and shorten the absences again if you see stress signs.

If even a few minutes looks difficult, begin much smaller.

Before you leave the first time

Print or copy this checklist.

  • My dog has had time to explore and rest in the chosen area.
  • Any gate or closed door has been introduced without distress.
  • My dog has had a recent potty opportunity.
  • Appropriate activity and settling time have been provided.
  • Fresh water is accessible.
  • The room temperature and ventilation are comfortable.
  • Cords, cleaners, medications, and unsafe food are inaccessible.
  • Blind cords, windows, stairs, and exit routes are secured.
  • Small objects and swallowable materials have been removed.
  • Bedding and toys are suitable for this dog's chewing behavior.
  • Any food toy has been introduced under supervision.
  • The camera view, sound, and connection have been checked.
  • I can return promptly if concern develops.
  • Longer necessary absences have suitable care coverage.

If crate confinement is being considered, check that nothing your dog wears can snag on gate bars, wire, or furniture. Our Crates for Anxious Dogs guide explains why comfort with confinement matters more than simply containing the dog.

Your first day

Prioritize orientation, toilet opportunities, food, sleep, and calm company. There is no requirement to leave home on arrival day.

Notice whether your dog can rest while you move nearby. Try standing, taking a small step, or briefly moving toward an interior doorway without making every movement a formal exercise.

If your dog remains comfortable, you may try a moment out of sight and then a very brief departure. Stay close enough to return, using live observation where possible.

Do not wait outside to find the longest duration your dog can endure. The first observation should help you choose an easy starting point.

Following you initially does not automatically establish a separation problem. Equally, being quiet from exhaustion or uncertainty does not prove readiness for a long absence.

Do not skip ahead because you need to return to work. Arrange care while practice continues at an appropriate level.

Keep leaving and coming back low-key

Use an ordinary, friendly goodbye and return calmly. You do not need to ignore your dog or withhold affection.

Avoid turning departures into an exciting ritual, but also avoid trying to sneak away as a substitute for teaching comfortable separation. The dog still experiences your absence once you are gone.

If shoes, keys, or bags trigger concern before departure, simplify that preparation. Our Departure Cues guide explains how to make these actions less significant.

If worry appears during a test, return calmly and make the next practice easier. Do not wait for a frightened dog to become silent.

When to slow down

Stop increasing time if signs appear earlier, become more intense, or take longer to resolve. Return to an easier stage after your dog has fully settled.

Check basic needs again: a puppy may need the toilet, an adult may be uncomfortable in the room, or outside noise may have changed the exercise.

Do not automatically diagnose separation anxiety from one difficult observation. However, repeated inability to settle deserves support rather than more challenging tests.

Dogs Trust notes that it is never too late to help an older newcomer feel calmer when left. Progress depends on the individual, not whether you started perfectly.

For persistent concerns, use our Separation Anxiety Training Plan with appropriate professional guidance.

Covering longer absences in the first weeks

Plan real cover before workdays, appointments, or travel. Family, a trusted friend, or a suitable sitter may provide company while your dog settles and learns.

Introduce the caregiver while you are present, then check that your dog can relax with them. Some dogs remain worried when one particular person leaves.

Confirm actual coverage hours, including commuting and handovers. A quick midday visit does not cover hours of distress before and afterward.

If possible, adjust the first weeks to reduce unavoidable absences. That may mean sharing household responsibilities, changing an errand, or arranging someone to stay.

Support at the beginning is normal. It does not prevent gradual independence practice.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Leaving for a full workday to see what happens.
  • Assuming a rescue dog's history explains every behavior.
  • Introducing a crate and departure simultaneously.
  • Shutting away a distressed dog to force adjustment.
  • Using food to distract from an overwhelming absence.
  • Increasing time after one quiet recording without checking posture.
  • Scolding or punishing accidents, damage, or vocalizing, which can make worry worse; use reward-based methods instead.
  • Leaving new toys unattended without safety assessment.
  • Treating the seventh day as a required milestone.

Keep the next step small enough to be useful.

When to get extra help

Contact your vet for sudden behavior changes, illness signs, reduced appetite, unusual panting, or new toilet problems.

Seek prompt help for self-injury or dangerous escape attempts. Do not repeat the confinement or absence that caused them.

A qualified, reward-based behavior professional can help if your dog cannot tolerate tiny separations or repeatedly becomes distressed. Ask your rescue or adoption organization about available support too.

Bring existing video and notes without provoking another episode.

Bottom line

Prepare a safe, familiar space and begin with short experiences your new dog can manage comfortably. A few minutes may suit one dog; another needs seconds and more support first.

Use the week to learn rather than meet a deadline. Arrange company for longer absences and seek help when distress persists.

Frequently Asked Questions: Leaving Your Dog Alone for the First Time

How long can I leave my dog alone the first time?
Start briefly and let observed comfort guide you. A few minutes may suit a relaxed dog, while others need seconds or easier indoor practice first. Remain available to return promptly rather than testing a maximum.
Is it normal for a new dog to cry when left alone?
Brief vocalizing can occur during adjustment, but it should be considered alongside body language and whether the dog genuinely settles. Persistent or escalating crying with pacing, drooling, or escape behavior needs an easier plan and possibly professional help. Do not wait out clear distress.
Should I crate my new dog when I leave?
Only consider confinement your dog already finds comfortable. A crate introduced suddenly can add stress, particularly when the dog's history is unknown. A familiar dog-proofed area may be more suitable, assessed while you are home.
How long does it take a rescue dog to get used to being alone?
There is no universal adjustment period. History, health, the new environment, and the individual dog's response all matter. Build comfortable experiences gradually and arrange care rather than assuming independence will appear by a particular date.
Can I leave a new puppy alone while I work?
A young puppy usually needs much more frequent care and company than a full workday allows. Arrange suitable support for toilet needs, feeding, rest, and reassurance. Practice brief separations separately instead of using the workday as training.
