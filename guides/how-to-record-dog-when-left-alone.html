How to Record Your Dog When You Leave: A Simple Camera Guide for Separation Anxiety

Record your dog when you leave with a phone or pet camera. Learn where to place it, what to watch for, and how to share useful clips with your vet.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 24 Oct 202611 min read
Quick answer

Use a phone, tablet, webcam, or pet camera to record your dog's resting area and main exit, starting before departure. Watch a short, safe absence live when possible, return if distress develops, and save timestamped clips showing both relaxed behavior and concerning changes.

  • Test the picture, sound, storage, and connection before leaving.
  • Record behavior without trying to provoke or prolong distress.
  • Compare patterns across safe recordings; one clip cannot establish a diagnosis.

You close the door, hear nothing, and hope your dog settles. But a quiet doorway cannot show whether your dog rests, paces silently, or starts barking after a hallway noise. Recording fills that gap.

We are dog owners, not vets or trainers, and we have not tested these products ourselves. This guide explains how to gather useful footage safely and honestly; it does not diagnose separation anxiety or replace an individualized training plan.

Why recording your dog matters

A recording is often the most useful information you can collect about behavior during absences. It shows the sequence before an accident, barking bout, or damaged doorway, rather than only the aftermath.

If the footage shows self-injury, escape attempts or distress that never settles, stop leaving your dog alone for now and see our separation anxiety red flags and when to call the vet.

Once you have footage, write down what you saw. Our free printable separation anxiety log gives you one row per absence, sign codes and a weekly review checklist.

Dogs Trust's separation anxiety advice recommends filming with a phone or remote camera, placing it where your dog spends time, and trying different positions to find a useful view. It also encourages checking footage for anxiety signs and bringing videos, diaries, or notes to a veterinary appointment.

Your first recording is for learning, not training or testing endurance. You are asking what happens, when it begins, and whether your dog settles—not how long they can withstand being alone.

What you can use to record

An old phone or tablet may work as well as a dedicated camera for an initial recording. Use its camera app for local footage, or a suitable free camera or video-call app for remote viewing.

Check whether the app actually records. A live call does not necessarily save video, and free services may limit recording length or storage.

A laptop webcam is another option if it provides a stable view. Confirm that sleep settings, a closed lid, or a screen lock will not stop recording.

A dedicated pet camera may offer a wider view, night vision, alerts, and convenient playback. Some recording features need a subscription or compatible storage.

For every option, check battery, available space, sound, and Wi-Fi where relevant. Our Best Pet Camera for Separation Anxiety guide compares camera types in more detail.

Where to put the camera

Start with the area your dog normally chooses for resting. Include the main exit door or the route toward it if possible.

Place the lens high enough to see your dog's whole body, but not so high that posture becomes difficult to judge. A stable shelf angled slightly downward often works better than a floor-level view blocked by furniture.

Avoid pointing directly toward a bright window: your dog may become a dark silhouette. Test lighting at the time you actually leave.

A second angle can help if your dog moves between rooms, but is optional. Do not restrict your dog to an uncomfortable area simply to keep them on camera.

Walk through the view while home and watch where your dog naturally goes.

Camera setup checklist

How to Record Your Dog When You Leave: A Simple Camera Guide for Separation Anxiety: camera setup checklist

  • Show the usual resting spot and a useful view toward the exit.
  • Keep the dog's whole body visible when standing and lying down.
  • Check lighting and avoid strong window glare.
  • Test sound recording without increasing volume excessively.
  • Confirm whether the setup saves footage or only streams live.
  • Check battery, storage capacity, and recording settings.
  • Test remote viewing from outside the home Wi-Fi connection.
  • Secure the device, stand, cables, and small accessories out of reach.
  • Confirm the device time and note any difference from your phone.
  • Plan a short absence and how you will return promptly.

Save a brief test recording and play it back before relying on the setup. A successful preview does not always mean the recording was stored.

What to film and for how long

Start before you pick up keys, put on shoes, or lift your bag. Preparation can trigger concern before the door closes; our Departure Cues guide explains that pattern.

The first 30–60 minutes can be especially informative during an ordinary absence that your dog can safely manage. Later footage matters too if barking or restlessness occurs after a delay.

That recording window is not a minimum test length. For an unfamiliar or worried dog, begin with a much shorter observation and remain close enough to return.

Watch live when possible. Stop and return if your dog clearly becomes distressed; do not wait for a dramatic episode or a predetermined duration.

If your dog has previously injured themselves or made dangerous escape attempts, ask your vet how to gather information safely. Existing footage may be enough.

Add context such as a noisy delivery, changed routine, or recent illness. Missing days should stay blank rather than being reconstructed from memory.

Reading the footage honestly

Watch the sequence before choosing an explanation. Ask when behavior starts, how long it lasts, what precedes it, and whether your dog recovers.

A calm clip shows comfort during that recording, not proof that every absence is comfortable. One difficult clip is useful information, not evidence that you or your dog failed.

Quietness does not necessarily mean relaxation. A dog lying rigidly facing the exit differs from one resting loosely and changing position normally.

Eating is also only one clue. Check what happens when the food ends.

Compare a few safe, ordinary recordings where available, including different circumstances. Do not manufacture repeated difficult departures to build a larger sample. Our Separation Anxiety Training Plan explains the separate process of building comfortable absence time.

Privacy and safety

Use a strong, unique password and two-factor login where available. Keep software updated and review who can access recordings.

Tell housemates, sitters, and other caregivers where cameras are placed and when they operate. Agree on privacy expectations and check applicable recording requirements, particularly for audio.

Turn cameras off or use their privacy controls when home recording is unnecessary. Avoid capturing neighboring homes or shared spaces.

Keep cables, chargers, stands, and small parts away from chewers. Use equipment according to its instructions and never cover a charging device with bedding.

Decide how long to retain recordings, share only what is needed, and avoid posting distress footage publicly.

How to share clips with your vet or trainer

  1. Ask for the preferred format. Check whether the professional wants an upload, private link, or files sent before the appointment.
  2. Choose representative footage. Include preparation, the start of the absence, a concerning change, and any settling. Do not select only dramatic moments.
  3. Preserve context. Keep a short lead-in before an event and the recovery afterward. Retain the original recording.
  4. Label clearly. Include date, departure time, absence length, and timestamps of key moments.
  5. Add a brief written summary. Describe the setup, who was home, outside triggers, and what you observed without assigning a diagnosis.
  6. Check access. Confirm the link opens and share footage through the agreed private method. Keep a backup available.

Common mistakes

  • Recording a doorway while the dog spends time elsewhere.
  • Assuming motion alerts capture every bark or quiet stress signal.
  • Forgetting that live viewing may not save footage.
  • Watching only the most dramatic section.
  • Leaving longer to obtain a “complete” test.
  • Treating silence, stillness, or eating as conclusive reassurance.
  • Using the speaker to scold or issuing repeated remote commands.
  • Relying on monitoring without anyone able to respond.

Keep speakers and automatic treat tossing off during an initial observation so they do not change the behavior you are trying to understand.

When to get help now

Stop observation and seek prompt veterinary help for self-injury, dangerous escape attempts, or signs of illness. Active bleeding, breathing difficulty, collapse, or suspected poisoning requires urgent attention.

Call your vet about sudden behavior changes or refusal to eat or drink. Explain whether this happens only during absences or also when you are home.

Do not leave a panicking dog in a crate or other confinement simply because a camera lets you watch. Arrange safe company and ask for an interim care plan.

A recording can support assessment, but it should never delay care.

Bottom line

Set up a clear, stable view, start before departure, and keep observations safe. Record what happens without guessing what your dog feels or pushing for a longer test.

A simple log and representative clips can give your vet or trainer a useful picture. The camera gathers information; an appropriate care and training plan addresses the problem.

Frequently Asked Questions: Recording Your Dog

How do I record my dog when I'm not home without a pet camera?
Use an old phone, tablet, or laptop with a camera app or suitable video-call setup. Check whether it saves footage, has enough storage and battery, and allows remote viewing if needed. Secure it out of reach and test playback first.
Where is the best place to put a dog camera?
Aim for a wide view of your dog's usual resting area and the main exit or route toward it. A stable shelf angled slightly downward often works, provided the dog's whole body remains visible. Try different positions while home and avoid strong window glare.
How long should I record my dog when I leave?
Start before departure and preserve the first 30–60 minutes of an ordinary, safely manageable absence when available. Test absences should be much shorter if comfort is uncertain, and you should return when distress appears. Never prolong panic to meet a recording target.
What signs of separation anxiety should I look for on video?
Repeated pacing, persistent vocalizing, drooling, panting, exit-directed behavior, food abandonment, and difficulty settling may warrant concern. Timing and combinations matter, and other causes are possible. Share footage with your vet or qualified behavior professional rather than diagnosing from a checklist.
Should I talk to my dog through the camera speaker?
Keep it off during initial observation so you can see the natural response to your absence. Hearing your voice may comfort some dogs but make others search or become more unsettled, so test any use while home first. Never use the speaker to scold, interrupt panic, or replace returning when your dog needs help.
