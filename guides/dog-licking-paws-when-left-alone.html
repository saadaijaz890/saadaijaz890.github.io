Dog Licking or Chewing Paws When Left Alone: Stress, Itch or Pain?

Dog licking paws when left alone? Learn clues for stress, itch or pain, when to call the vet, how to protect sore skin and ways to rebuild calm alone time.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 25 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Paw licking around departure may be linked to separation distress, but itch, injury and pain can look similar. Check for changes and contact your vet about persistent licking or sore skin; then address any separation stress with manageable practice and care cover.

  • Departure timing offers clues, but does not rule out itch or pain.
  • Raw, bleeding or open skin needs prompt veterinary attention.
  • Protect paws with veterinary guidance, never punishment or an unfamiliar barrier to licking.

You return to find your dog’s front paws wet, their light fur stained or one patch looking sore. Perhaps they rarely lick while you watch, making anxiety seem like the obvious explanation. But being alone can also give a dog uninterrupted time to attend to an itchy or painful paw.

We are dog owners, not vets or trainers. This guide helps you check gently, record useful details and recognize when veterinary care comes first. If separation distress is involved, the aim is comfortable alone time alongside protection for the skin.

Why dogs lick or chew their paws when left alone

Brief grooming is different from repeated licking or chewing that is difficult to interrupt or damages skin. Some dogs may use repetitive licking as a self-soothing activity when worried.

Some worried dogs shake or tremble instead of licking their paws. Our guide to dog shaking or trembling when left alone explains how to tell fear about being left from feeling cold, pain or illness.

Concern about separation increases when licking begins with your departure and accompanies pacing, whining, drooling or food refusal. It still does not establish the cause.

The ASPCA’s separation anxiety advice recommends seeing a vet to rule out medical issues before treating a behavior problem as separation anxiety. Itch and worry can also occur together.

Signs that need the vet

Contact your vet for persistent or excessive licking, even without obvious damage. Redness, swelling, odor, scabs, hair loss, limping or a broken nail deserve assessment.

Raw, bleeding or open skin needs prompt attention because continued licking can worsen injury. Do not wait for a training plan to solve a wound.

Dogs Trust’s separation anxiety advice notes that physical health can influence behavior without obvious pain or illness, and advises a vet check when behavior has recently changed. A sudden new licking habit belongs in that conversation.

How to check your dog's paws

Choose a calm moment, including after walks. Let your dog remain in a comfortable position and begin with what you can see without lifting or squeezing the paw.

Compare paws, pads, nails and the skin between toes only as your dog comfortably allows. Stop if they pull away, stiffen, growl or show pain. A sore dog may bite even when normally gentle.

Do not force a detailed examination or dig for an object. Photograph visible changes if easy, and let your vet perform the examination your dog needs.

Quick paw check

  • Notice whether your dog stands evenly.
  • Look for limping or a lifted paw.
  • Compare both front and back paws.
  • Look for redness or swelling.
  • Check for wet fur and saliva staining.
  • Look for scabs, hair loss or open skin.
  • Notice unusual odor or discharge.
  • Look for a split or broken nail.
  • Check between toes only if comfortably permitted.
  • Note visible debris without probing.
  • Stop handling if your dog seems uncomfortable.

What to film and note

Record the first 10–20 minutes only if your dog remains comfortable that long. This is an observation window, not a required absence. Return earlier if distress develops, and never prolong licking that is damaging skin.

Protecting the skin kindly

Ask your vet whether a soft cone, recovery suit or dog booties would appropriately protect the affected area. These options are not interchangeable: some do not prevent access to paws, and fit matters.

Introduce any recommended equipment gently while you are home, using comfortable short sessions and rewards. Follow the vet’s instructions about use and supervision.

Never use a cone as punishment or improvise tight coverings. Tell your vet if equipment causes panic, rubbing or difficulty moving, drinking or resting. Protection can prevent further damage, but does not address the underlying itch, pain or fear.

A calmer set-up

Choose a familiar resting space with water, comfortable bedding and suitable room conditions. Keep floors and surroundings free of sharp debris, accessible chemicals and objects your dog might chew apart.

Meet appropriate exercise, sniffing and potty needs before leaving, then allow time to settle. More exercise is not automatically helpful, particularly with a sore paw.

Avoid introducing unfamiliar confinement to stop licking. Never shut a panicking dog in a crate. If no setup prevents distress or self-injury safely, arrange company and professional support.

Calm set-up checklist

What to do when you get home

Offer a calm greeting and a quick visual paw check. Avoid repeatedly grabbing a sore foot or turning every reunion into a lengthy examination.

Note wetness, redness, any new damage and how your dog behaves after you return. Contact your vet about worsening skin or pain.

Record facts in our separation anxiety log: which paw, when licking started and what else occurred. If drooling accompanies episodes, our drooling when left alone guide covers that separate symptom.

Absences you can't avoid

Arrange a familiar caregiver before your dog exceeds their comfortable limit. Confirm that your dog actually relaxes with that person and explain the veterinary care plan.

A brief walker visit cannot cover distress before and afterward. A sore paw may also change what walking is appropriate.

Our care options guide helps organize company and backups. Cameras reveal behavior but cannot prevent self-injury or provide in-person care.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not assume licking only noticed on camera is caused by separation anxiety. Itch and pain may simply be less interrupted while your dog is alone.

Avoid punishment, scolding, bitter sprays and leaving your dog to cry. Do not use protective equipment to force a longer absence.

Skip unprescribed creams, home remedies and improvised coverings. Follow veterinary guidance rather than treating the appearance of the paw yourself.

Finally, a lick mat is enrichment, not treatment for inflamed skin or separation distress.

When to get extra help

Seek veterinary reassessment if licking persists, skin worsens or your dog cannot rest comfortably. Report any change in mobility, appetite or overall behavior.

Get a qualified, reward-based professional experienced in separation difficulties involved if panic begins quickly, your dog injures themselves or manageable practice is not helping.

Bring short clips and notes using our vet visit preparation guide. Medical care and behavior support may need to proceed together rather than treating them as competing explanations.

Bottom line

Persistent paw licking deserves a health check before being labeled anxiety. Protect sore skin with your vet’s guidance, observe the whole behavior pattern and rebuild comfortable separations gradually. The goal is healthy paws and a dog who feels safe.

Frequently Asked Questions: Dog Licking Paws When Left Alone

Why does my dog lick its paws when I leave?
Repetitive licking may be a self-soothing response to separation distress, especially with pacing or whining. Itch, injury and pain can also become more noticeable during uninterrupted time alone. Discuss persistent or new licking with your vet.
Is paw licking a sign of separation anxiety?
It may accompany separation distress, but paw licking alone cannot establish that diagnosis. Departure timing and other behaviors offer clues, while a veterinary assessment checks physical causes. Both discomfort and anxiety can be present.
Why are my dog’s paws wet or red when I get home?
Repeated licking can wet the fur and irritate skin, but existing itch or inflammation may have started the cycle. Check gently for visible changes and contact your vet. Raw or open skin needs prompt advice rather than another long observation absence.
When should I see a vet about paw licking?
Arrange a visit for persistent licking, staining, odor, scabs, hair loss or recurring redness. Contact the clinic promptly for open skin, bleeding, swelling, a broken nail or limping. Severe pain, heavy bleeding or inability to bear weight needs urgent assessment.
How do I stop my dog licking its paws when left alone?
Identify and address the cause with your vet, including any necessary skin protection. If separation distress contributes, use short comfortable practice and care cover for longer gaps. Food enrichment may support settling but cannot replace medical care or gradual training.
