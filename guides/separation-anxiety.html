Dog Separation Anxiety: Signs & Step-by-Step Treatment

Dog separation anxiety can turn an ordinary departure into a frightening experience for your dog. You might hear barking after closing the door, discover scratched doorframes, or notice your dog pacing whenever you pick up your keys.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 15 Oct 202620 min read
Quick answer

Dog separation anxiety is distress that occurs when a dog is separated from an important person or left alone. Signs may include persistent barking, pacing, drooling, destructive behavior, or escape attempts. Treatment focuses on identifying the dog's comfortable alone-time limit, practicing departures below that limit, and gradually increasing duration. Severe cases may require veterinary behavioral treatment and prescription medication alongside training.

The encouraging news is that separation-related distress can often improve with a structured, patient approach. The goal is not to force your dog to endure being alone. It is to help them learn, gradually, that short separations can feel safe.

The most useful clues are what happens, when it begins, and whether the behavior is linked to being left alone.

Common signs of separation anxiety in dogs

  • Persistent barking or howling: Vocalizing repeatedly after departure, sometimes continuing for much of the absence.
  • Pacing and restlessness: Walking repeatedly between doors, windows, or familiar locations without settling.
  • Destructive behavior: Scratching doors, chewing window frames, or damaging exit areas in apparent attempts to reunite with an owner.
  • Excessive panting or drooling: Physical signs of distress unrelated to heat or exercise.
  • House-soiling: Urinating or defecating during absences despite otherwise reliable house training.
  • Escape attempts: Trying to break through crates, doors, windows, or barriers, sometimes causing injury.
  • Predeparture anxiety: Becoming visibly upset when an owner picks up keys, puts on shoes, or approaches the door.
  • Difficulty settling: Remaining vigilant, trembling, whining, or repeatedly searching for a person instead of resting.

Not every dog shows every sign. Some dogs are relatively quiet but remain highly distressed.

Dog Separation Anxiety Severity Self-Check

This checklist is a practical way to organize observations before speaking with a professional. It is not a validated clinical diagnostic tool, and severity cannot be determined by counting symptoms alone.

Mild separation-related distress

  • Whines or briefly barks during departures.
  • Can settle after a short period.
  • Still accepts familiar food or enrichment.
  • Shows no repeated escape attempts or self-injury.
  • Can tolerate some short separations comfortably.

What to do: Start with manageable alone-time exercises, establish a predictable routine, and use suitable enrichment if your dog enjoys it.

Choosing an enrichment tool? Our comparison of lick mat vs snuffle mat vs puzzle feeder explains which one suits which dog. Enrichment can support training but does not treat separation anxiety on its own.

Mild signs can sometimes improve with consistent training, but monitor them rather than assuming they will disappear.

Moderate separation-related distress

  • Repeatedly barks, howls, or paces after departure.
  • Struggles to settle without company.
  • Reacts strongly to keys, shoes, or other departure cues.
  • Sometimes refuses food during absences.
  • Scratches doors or damages nearby objects.
  • Shows distress during relatively short separations.

What to do: Arrange veterinary guidance and consider a qualified separation-anxiety professional. Begin exercises below your dog's distress threshold and reduce unavoidable long absences.

Severe separation anxiety

  • Panics during extremely short absences.
  • Attempts to escape through doors, windows, or crates.
  • Risks injury through frantic scratching, biting, or struggling.
  • Trembles, drools excessively, or remains highly distressed.
  • Cannot relax even when the owner is briefly out of sight.
  • Shows repeated intense distress despite attempts at training.

What to do: Seek prompt veterinary assessment. Safety, preventing repeated panic, and an individualized treatment plan take priority over increasing alone time.

What your dog's severity tells you

A mild case may allow carefully managed home training. Moderate or severe symptoms typically justify professional guidance, particularly if you cannot identify a duration during which your dog remains comfortable.

Do not wait for serious damage or injury before seeking help.

Step-by-Step Alone-Time Protocol: Desensitization Training

Gradual desensitization is a central approach to treating separation anxiety.

Want the whole process laid out session by session? Our step-by-step separation anxiety training plan covers finding your dog's starting point, the stress signs to watch on camera, a week-by-week example schedule and a training log you can copy.

It involves exposing your dog to very brief, manageable separations and increasing the difficulty only as they remain comfortable.

Counterconditioning may also help by associating appropriate separation exercises with positive experiences.

The essential principle is simple:

Train below your dog's anxiety threshold, not beyond it.

The ASPCA's separation anxiety guidance describes gradual desensitization and counterconditioning as important treatment approaches, particularly for moderate or severe cases.

Step 1: Establish a safe, predictable routine

Before practicing departures, make sure your dog's basic needs are met.

Provide appropriate exercise, toilet opportunities, regular meals, suitable mental enrichment, and a comfortable resting area.

A predictable routine can reduce unnecessary uncertainty. However, physical exhaustion is not a cure for separation anxiety.

Avoid punishment, forced confinement, or deliberately ignoring a dog who is panicking.

If your dog becomes distressed in a crate, do not assume crating will make alone time safer. Some dogs need a different resting arrangement. For help choosing a crate, pen, or dog-proofed room as a management tool (not a treatment), see our guide to crates and pens for anxious dogs.

A move to a new house can also restart alone-time distress even in dogs who previously managed absences. See dog anxiety after moving for settling-in steps before you rebuild alone practice.

Step 2: Find your dog's current comfort threshold

Start by identifying the longest separation your dog can comfortably tolerate.

For some dogs, this may be several minutes. For others, it may be only a few seconds—or even the moment before the owner reaches the door.

Observe for early warning signs such as sudden stillness, staring at the exit, pacing, whining, panting, or an inability to settle.

A quiet dog is not necessarily a comfortable dog.

If your dog becomes distressed when you touch the door handle, your first exercise might involve approaching the door and walking away without leaving.

Do not begin with a five-minute departure simply because it sounds short to you. Begin where your dog can succeed.

Step 3: Separate departure cues from actual departures

Some dogs begin anticipating separation before their owners leave.

Practice familiar actions without following them with an absence:

  • Pick up your keys, then put them down.
  • Put on your shoes, then sit down.
  • Walk toward the door, then return to another activity.
  • Pick up your bag without going outside.

Keep these exercises calm and brief.

If a cue triggers obvious anxiety, make it easier. For example, move toward the keys without touching them.

The goal is to reduce your dog's expectation that every departure cue predicts a difficult experience.

Step 4: Practice tiny, successful separations

Once your dog is comfortable with departure cues, begin brief out-of-sight exercises.

A possible starting sequence might look like this:

PracticeExample actionWhat to observe
1Step toward an interior doorwayDog remains relaxed
2Step briefly out of sightNo distress
3Return after one secondDog remains comfortable
4Repeat with a different short durationRelaxed behavior continues
5Practice a brief exit-door separationNo pacing, panic, or escalating vocalization

These are examples, not a mandatory schedule.

If your dog reacts to one second alone, begin with an easier exercise. That might mean remaining visible, moving only a few steps away, or briefly touching the door.

Step 5: Increase duration gradually

Progress only after your dog demonstrates repeated comfort at the current level.

For example, a dog already tolerating several seconds might practice departures of two, four, three, and five seconds across separate successful repetitions.

Vary durations rather than making every exercise longer than the previous one.

Allow your dog to settle fully between departures. If you repeatedly leave while they are still excited or worried, stress may accumulate.

When your dog remains comfortable, increase the challenge in small increments.

If they show distress, return to an easier level.

There is no universal number of days or weeks required to reach a particular alone-time duration. Progress depends on the individual dog and may take considerable time.

Step 6: Build comfortable real-world departures

Once your dog reliably manages very short exercises, begin practicing slightly longer absences.

Change only one major difficulty at a time.

For example, avoid simultaneously increasing duration, changing the exit door, and introducing an unfamiliar location.

Continue using a camera where practical.

Look for relaxed body language, resting, and the ability to engage naturally with the environment.

Do not assume that a dog comfortable for a few minutes is ready for an entire workday.

Step 7: Manage unavoidable absences

Repeated experiences of intense panic can interfere with training.

While working on separation anxiety, consider arrangements that prevent your dog from repeatedly experiencing absences beyond their current tolerance.

Options include a trusted sitter, family member, suitable daycare, or flexible work arrangements when available.

These arrangements do not replace treatment. They give your dog opportunities to practice manageable separations without repeated overwhelming distress.

Dogs vary in whether another person or another dog provides enough reassurance. Observe your own dog's response instead of assuming companionship will solve the problem.

Step 8: Track progress and adjust

Keep a simple training record.

DateDeparture durationDog's behaviorNext adjustment
Example day 12 secondsRelaxedRepeat or make a small increase
Example day 25 secondsBriefly alert, then relaxedRepeat before progressing
Example day 310 secondsWhining and pacingReduce duration

Treat the example as a method for tracking behavior, not a recommended advancement schedule.

Progress means your dog is becoming more comfortable—not merely remaining quiet for longer.

If distress persists or worsens, consult your veterinarian or a qualified behavior specialist.

Training remains the foundation

For many dogs, carefully planned separation exercises are the most important long-term intervention.

A successful program teaches emotional tolerance rather than simply distracting the dog.

For dogs with moderate or severe separation anxiety, working with an appropriately qualified behavior professional helps reduce the risk of progressing too quickly.

Enrichment supports training but does not replace it

Food puzzles, safe chew toys, scent games, and calm activities may help dogs who already tolerate short periods alone. See slow feeders and lick mats for anxious dogs for what these tools can and can’t do.

They do not reliably resolve established panic.

If your dog refuses a favorite treat whenever you leave, that may indicate the training situation is too difficult.

If skipped meals or untouched food toys are the main thing you notice, our guide to why a dog won’t eat when left alone covers a cause check, a 48-hour observation checklist, and a calm alone-time feeding plan.

Pheromones, wraps, and supplements

These products may be considered as additions to a behavior plan, particularly when their practical features suit the individual dog.

The evidence for their effectiveness varies by product, condition, and study design. Avoid interpreting promotional claims as proof of treatment success.

Introduce one new product at a time where practical and monitor behavior carefully. A dog that tolerates fitted clothing may appear more comfortable in a gentle pressure wrap such as a ThunderShirt during a stressful event—but wraps are not well established as a primary separation-anxiety treatment.

When medication becomes part of treatment

Prescription medication should not be viewed as a personal failure or something that must always be postponed until every supplement has failed.

For significant anxiety, a veterinarian may recommend medication alongside behavior modification to improve welfare and make learning possible.

In the United States, fluoxetine and clomipramine are established veterinary options approved for canine separation anxiety in conjunction with behavioral treatment.

Other medications may be considered depending on the dog's clinical needs and veterinary assessment.

Medication has potential risks, interactions, and side effects. Some options take time to produce their intended effects, while others may be selected for specific circumstances.

The Merck Veterinary Manual's discussion of canine behavior problems explains how behavioral management and veterinary medication can work together.

Never give human anxiety medication or combine calming supplements with prescription drugs without veterinary guidance.

Puppy Separation Anxiety: Building Confidence Early

Puppy separation anxiety deserves a thoughtful approach because young dogs are still learning about their environment, routines, and relationships.

A puppy who cries when left alone is not automatically experiencing a clinical anxiety disorder.

Young puppies need frequent toilet breaks, sleep, food, comfort, and supervision. They also need age-appropriate opportunities to practice short periods of independence.

Start with tiny separations

Begin while your puppy is comfortable, has recently had a toilet break, and is ready for quiet activity or rest.

Step a short distance away, return calmly, and repeat only if your puppy remains comfortable.

Gradually introduce very brief out-of-sight experiences.

Build duration around your puppy's emotional tolerance and developmental needs rather than following an inflexible timetable.

Make resting spaces comfortable

Provide a suitable bed, safe toys, and a predictable resting location. Some puppies settle with a heartbeat comfort toy such as a Snuggle Puppy during supervised rest—supervise closely and remove it if your puppy chews or swallows components.

A puppy pen or crate can be useful when introduced positively, but confinement is not a treatment for separation anxiety.

If your puppy panics when confined, stop forcing the situation and reconsider the setup.

Avoid teaching through prolonged distress

Leaving a frightened puppy to cry for extended periods is not a reliable way to build healthy independence.

Instead, use short successful separations, reward calm behavior, and address distress before it escalates.

Seek veterinary or qualified behavioral guidance if your puppy shows persistent panic, injury risks, or worsening distress during ordinary departures.

Separation Anxiety in Dogs: Does Breed Matter?

Breed background can influence energy levels, sociability, sensitivity, and common behavioral needs. However, no breed automatically develops separation anxiety, and breed alone cannot diagnose the condition.

A people-oriented Golden Retriever may follow family members throughout the house without showing distress during absences. A working Border Collie may need substantial daily activity but still struggle with being left alone. A small companion breed may be affectionate and independent.

Every dog deserves an individual assessment.

When Calming Products Help—and When They Don't

Calming products can be useful when they support a clear, safe training goal.

They are less useful when owners expect a product to stop panic without addressing the underlying anxiety.

When products may help

A dog who already handles short absences comfortably may enjoy a stuffed KONG during training.

A puppy who settles with familiar bedding may benefit from a consistent resting environment.

A dog who tolerates fitted clothing may appear more comfortable wearing a gentle pressure wrap during a stressful event.

Some owners also consider pheromone diffusers or calming supplements as optional support, with realistic expectations.

When products are unlikely to be enough

A diffuser is not a substitute for desensitization.

A supplement cannot reliably prevent severe separation-related panic.

A pressure wrap cannot teach a dog that separation is safe.

A food puzzle will not help if your dog is too distressed to eat.

If products are not helping, reassess the behavioral plan instead of continually purchasing additional calming items.

How to evaluate whether an aid is useful

Ask three questions:

  • Does my dog willingly interact with or tolerate the product?
  • Does my dog show more relaxed behavior during manageable training exercises?
  • Is the product safe and practical for the intended situation?

Use observable behavior rather than assumptions about what your dog must be feeling.

When to See a Vet or Behaviorist

Professional help is appropriate when separation-related distress is persistent, worsening, or interfering with your dog's welfare.

Seek veterinary guidance if your dog:

  • Injures themselves trying to escape.
  • Shows panic during extremely short separations.
  • Develops sudden anxiety or major behavioral changes.
  • Refuses food during absences and shows other substantial distress signs.
  • Has repeated house-soiling accidents despite normal toilet habits.
  • Experiences signs that may relate to pain, illness, or cognitive changes.
  • Cannot progress with careful training or experiences repeated setbacks.

A veterinarian can evaluate medical factors and determine whether medication or specialist support is appropriate.

Planning a trip? See our guide on how to prep a pet sitter for an anxious dog — gradual introductions, a short trial absence, and a written care sheet so your dog is not left alone longer than they can manage.

A board-certified veterinary behaviorist can assess complex anxiety and coordinate behavioral and medical treatment.

A qualified reward-based trainer experienced in separation anxiety may also help, especially when following a carefully structured desensitization program.

Avoid trainers who recommend punishment, forced exposure, or deliberately allowing repeated panic as the primary treatment.

The Merck Veterinary Manual describes behavior treatment as a combination of management, behavior modification, and medication when indicated.

The goal is to reduce fear and improve welfare, not simply stop unwanted behavior.

Key Takeaway: Teach Comfort, Not Endurance

Dog separation anxiety is a distress problem, not stubbornness, spite, or a lack of affection.

The most helpful starting point is to observe your dog's behavior, identify how much separation they can comfortably tolerate, and build from that level using gradual, successful exercises.

Avoid punishing anxiety behaviors or forcing prolonged absences in the hope that your dog will eventually adjust.

Products may support a calm environment, but no chew, wrap, toy, or diffuser can replace a suitable behavior plan.

For dogs with significant distress, veterinary guidance and an experienced behavior professional can make treatment safer and more effective.

Your goal is not merely to leave your dog alone for longer. It is to help your dog feel safe when you are apart.

Dog Separation Anxiety: Frequently Asked Questions

What is dog separation anxiety?
Dog separation anxiety is significant distress associated with being separated from an important person or left alone. Dogs may bark, pace, drool, damage doors, or attempt to escape. Diagnosis requires considering when symptoms occur and ruling out other behavioral or medical explanations.
What are the main signs of separation anxiety in dogs?
Common signs include persistent barking, pacing, excessive panting, drooling, destructive behavior near exits, and distress when owners prepare to leave. Some dogs also refuse food or experience house-soiling accidents. Video observation and veterinary assessment can help distinguish separation anxiety from other problems.
What is the best separation anxiety treatment for dogs?
The best separation anxiety treatment for dogs generally combines gradual desensitization, management of unavoidable absences, and appropriate veterinary support. Training should progress only while the dog remains comfortable. Severe cases may benefit from prescription medication alongside behavior modification.
How do you treat puppy separation anxiety?
Begin with very short, comfortable separations while meeting your puppy's developmental, toilet, sleep, and social needs. Introduce resting spaces positively and gradually increase alone time without forcing prolonged distress. Persistent panic warrants professional advice.
Can dogs separation anxiety go away on its own?
Some mild separation-related difficulties improve as dogs adapt to new routines, but established separation anxiety should not be assumed to disappear without support. Repeated distress can continue or worsen. Early, appropriate intervention offers a more constructive path than simply waiting.
How long does separation anxiety in dogs take to treat?
There is no reliable universal timeline. Some dogs improve with consistent, manageable exercises, while others require a longer individualized treatment plan. Severity, medical conditions, daily circumstances, and the dog's response to training all affect progress.
Do calming chews help separation anxiety dogs?
Calming chews may provide some support for individual dogs, but evidence depends on the formulation and they are not established standalone treatments for separation anxiety. Dogs with significant distress may need structured behavior modification and veterinary care. Discuss supplements with your veterinarian, particularly if your dog takes medication.
Can separation anxiety in dogs be cured permanently?
Many dogs can make meaningful improvements and learn to tolerate appropriate absences, but permanent recovery cannot be guaranteed. Some dogs need ongoing management or occasional refresher training, particularly after major routine changes. The goal is sustained comfort and welfare, not simply preventing barking.
