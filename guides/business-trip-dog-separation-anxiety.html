Business Trips When You Live Alone With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety

Plan work-trip care for your anxious dog when you live alone, with trial stays, a clear handover, backup coverage and a calm routine when you return.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 31 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Book care that covers both days and nights, with company matched to your dog's needs and a trial before departure. Confirm backup coverage for travel delays; drop-in visits and camera checks are not an overnight care plan.

  • Confirm daytime coverage as carefully as the overnight booking.
  • Trial the caregiver before relying on a work-trip stay.
  • Keep a local backup who can take over if plans change.

When you live alone, a work trip removes the only person normally home with your dog. Even a one-night conference needs a complete care arrangement, including the hours before departure and any delay getting back.

We are dog owners who researched this topic, not vets, trainers or product testers, and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. This guide focuses on making solo-owner business travel workable: choosing care, testing the handover and protecting your dog's routine while you are away and after you return.

Why work trips are hard when you live alone with an anxious dog

There is no partner or roommate continuing the familiar routine. Your departure may mean a new person entering the home, moving to another household or staying at a boarding facility.

The ASPCA's page on separation anxiety describes separation anxiety as distress when dogs are left alone or separated from the people they're attached to, and explains that treatment focuses on gradually teaching the dog to be comfortable alone.

That distinction matters when choosing care. Some dogs settle with another trusted person; others remain distressed when their particular person leaves. A booking does not establish that your dog will cope—you need a trial.

Work travel can also be unpredictable. Early departures, evening meetings and delayed returns create gaps that are easy to overlook. Plan for the entire absence rather than just the nights listed on the itinerary.

Choosing care for a work trip

An in-home overnight sitter keeps your dog's surroundings familiar, but ask exactly when they will be present. “Overnight” may leave daytime hours uncovered. Confirm company during every period your dog cannot manage alone. Our care options guide compares sitters, daycare, boarding and family care in more depth.

A friend or family member your dog knows may stay at your home or host them. Check their actual schedule, other pets and ability to provide the needed support.

Boarding can suit some dogs who relax around staff, while others struggle with unfamiliar confinement or noise. A veterinary clinic that boards is another possibility, but confirm staffing and time spent alone rather than assuming medical premises provide continuous company.

Bringing your dog may work only if the whole trip allows suitable care. Meetings, transport and accommodation still need planning; never use an unattended car or outdoor space as the fallback. Drop-in visits alone are not enough for an overnight work trip, and a camera does not fill the gaps between them; our daycare vs dog walker guide explains what short visits can and can't cover. For fuller comparisons and introductions, use our boarding guide and our pet sitter prep guide.

Book early and do a trial run

Contact caregivers when travel first becomes likely. Explain the difficulty plainly: whether your dog paces, howls, scratches exits or struggles even with someone present.

Begin with a meeting while you are home. Let the person share a pleasant activity without forcing interaction. Next, try a short handover while you remain nearby and reachable.

If that is comfortable, arrange a practice stay or overnight before the actual trip. Ask what happened after you left, during bedtime and the following morning. Review brief recordings where appropriate.

A dog who cannot settle during a trial needs an easier arrangement and professional advice, not a longer test during the conference.

Confirm dates, daytime presence, cancellation arrangements and possible extensions in writing. Check the current terms of any booking. Introduce the backup too; their first meeting should not happen because your return has been delayed.

Talk through the essentials before leaving. Ask the carer to confirm the coverage hours and emergency plan, so misunderstandings surface while you can still resolve them.

Camera and phone check-ins from the road

Agree on predictable updates that fit the care routine and your work schedule. A morning message and an evening summary may be useful, with immediate contact for concerns rather than waiting for the next update.

Ask about eating, drinking, potty breaks, sleep and comfort with the carer. A relaxed photo is welcome, but it does not describe the whole day.

If using a camera, agree on its location and access beforehand. Watch for comfortable resting as well as repeated pacing, panting, drooling, howling or exit-focused behavior.

If distress appears, contact the carer promptly. They may need to return immediately, stay closer or activate the backup. If the arrangement cannot meet your dog's needs, change the care or travel plan.

Do not rely automatically on speaking through the camera. Hearing your voice without finding you can upset some dogs. A camera or phone call never replaces a person able to respond.

When work trips keep coming

One regular carer can make recurring trips more predictable, provided your dog is comfortable with them. Keep a second introduced person available rather than depending entirely on one calendar.

Build a repeatable trip-week routine: confirm coverage, check supplies, share any health changes and review the return date. Familiar feeding, walking and bedtime patterns help the carer provide consistent support.

Keep care notes current. A duration your dog handled previously may change after illness, a difficult trip or a schedule disruption.

Between trips, work on comfortable alone-time skills in tiny steps, recording sessions and increasing only while your dog stays relaxed. Necessary work travel is not a substitute for that practice. Our working full-time guide covers everyday workday cover and training around a job.

If repeated separations remain difficult despite suitable company, seek veterinary and qualified behavior support before the next booking.

The first evening and week back

Greet your dog warmly and calmly. Offer a potty break, water and ordinary company rather than turning the return into a prolonged, overwhelming event.

Ask the carer for observations before they leave. Note any changes in appetite, sleep, potty habits or behavior, including moments that were harder than expected.

Keep the first evening simple. Your dog may need rest after a different routine.

During the following days, begin with easier alone-time practice before assuming the pre-trip duration still applies. Record what happens and arrange support for necessary longer absences. Our readjustment guide for after a vacation has a gentle re-entry plan you can shorten after a work trip.

If your dog seems more watchful or follows you closely, review the whole pattern rather than treating following alone as proof of a setback. Persistent distress, health changes or difficulty settling deserve help.

Talking to your manager

Raise practical needs early, with a specific proposal. You might ask whether a shorter trip, later departure, earlier return or remote attendance for part of the event is possible.

Present it as a request rather than a demand, and explain the scheduling issue without promising that your dog will be ready by a particular date. For example: “I need confirmed overnight and daytime care before departure; could we finalize the travel dates now?”

Ask how itinerary changes are handled and check the current terms of relevant arrangements. Do not assume flexibility, reimbursement or permission to bring your dog.

If care falls through, contact work promptly with workable alternatives. Our pet sitter cancellation guide covers that immediate backup decision.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

A dog with strong distress should see the vet before the trip. Tell them about upcoming travel and share recordings of departures or caregiver trials.

Contact your vet early if your dog refuses food, seems unwell or develops a sudden behavior change while you are away. Give the carer clear permission and instructions for seeking help when you cannot be reached.

Escape attempts, self-injury and persistent panic need prompt attention. Serious injury, collapse or breathing difficulty require emergency veterinary care. Our separation anxiety red flags guide explains which signs are urgent.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

A qualified reward-based behavior professional can help adapt handovers and practice when your dog cannot settle with another person. Never punish barking, howling, damage or accidents, and never shut a panicking dog in a crate or room and leave.

Bottom line

Frequently Asked Questions: Business Trips With an Anxious Dog

What do I do with my dog when I travel for work and live alone?
Arrange a trialed in-home sitter, familiar friend or family member, or suitable boarding care that meets your dog's needs. Confirm both daytime and overnight arrangements. Have a local backup ready for cancellations or delayed returns.
Can I leave my dog with separation anxiety alone overnight if someone drops in?
No—do not use drop-in visits as the overnight care plan. Your dog needs an arrangement that covers their company and practical needs throughout the trip. A camera does not fill the gaps between visits.
Is a pet sitter or boarding better for a dog with separation anxiety?
It depends on where your dog actually settles and how much company each option provides. An in-home sitter preserves familiar surroundings, while some dogs cope well in an established boarding setting. Trial the arrangement and confirm time spent alone.
How do I check on my dog during a business trip?
Agree on routine messages or calls with the carer and immediate contact for concerning changes. A camera can add useful observations with everyone's consent. Someone local must remain responsible for responding to distress or illness.
How do I help my dog after I get back from a work trip?
Keep the reunion warm and calm, restore ordinary care and allow time to rest. Start with easier recorded absences before resuming longer ones. Contact the vet for food refusal, illness or significant behavior changes.
