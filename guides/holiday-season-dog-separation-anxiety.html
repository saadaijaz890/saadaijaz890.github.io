Christmas and New Year's Plan for a Dog With Separation Anxiety

Plan December outings, Christmas guests and New Year's Eve company for a dog with separation anxiety, with safety checks and a return to routine.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 30 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Plan December's absences before accepting commitments, keep alone time within your dog's comfort and book suitable company for longer gaps. Protect quiet rest during gatherings, and make sure someone your dog trusts is home during New Year's Eve fireworks.

  • Keep decorations, food gifts and busy doorways safely managed.
  • Plan company for New Year's Eve rather than relying on a camera.

December can fill quickly with office parties, shopping, services and family visits. For a dog with separation anxiety, these plans may mean longer absences at unusual times—or a noisy house that suddenly becomes empty.

We are dog owners, not vets or trainers, and we have no ties to any sitter, daycare or care service. This guide focuses on getting through the season at home, from early December to New Year's Day on Friday, January 1, 2027, with realistic care arrangements and familiar routines.

Why the holiday season is hard for a dog with separation anxiety

The difficulty is often the combination of changes. Children may be home from school, visitors stay overnight and meals or walks happen later. Your dog gets extra company, then everyone leaves for dinner.

Decorations also change familiar spaces. A tree may occupy a resting corner, furniture moves and delivery activity increases. A dog may need help finding a comfortable place to rest amid the bustle.

New Year's Eve adds another challenge: late outings alongside fireworks and unexpected bangs. A dog distressed about separation may also be frightened by noise.

A busy day does not mean your dog will cope with a quiet evening alone. Plan around your dog's observed comfort rather than assuming activity or company earlier will make a longer absence manageable.

Map out December before it starts

Use one calendar for outings and dog care. Include travel time, shopping stops, late finishes and the possibility of delays. Write down who leaves last and who returns first.

Mark any gap beyond what your dog currently handles comfortably. Arrange a familiar carer, suitable daycare or taking turns at home before the day arrives.

The ASPCA's separation anxiety advice recommends avoiding absences outside training while working on the problem, with options such as a family member, friend or sitter staying with the dog, or suitable doggy daycare.

Do not treat the holiday calendar as a deadline for training progress. If an event exceeds your dog's ability, change the care arrangement rather than rushing the dog.

Parties, shopping and nights out

Be specific when booking care. A drop-in visit is different from someone staying, and a sitter may have other holiday commitments. Explain what your dog does when alone and ask exactly when they would be without company.

A dog walker can help with exercise and toilet needs in milder situations. A brief walk does not cover a dog who panics before and after the visit.

Daycare suits some dogs, but only if they enjoy the setting and its hours fit the outing. Trial a new carer or arrangement before relying on it during a long evening.

Visitors and decorations

Give your dog a familiar place to rest away from the busiest activity. Keep their bed and water accessible, and avoid moving everything at once if possible.

A calm room can provide a break, but someone your dog is comfortable with may need to stay there too. Never shut a panicking dog away and leave them to cope. Introduce any gate or boundary gently while you are available.

Set clear guest rules: allow voluntary greetings, leave the resting dog undisturbed and check before offering food. Adults should supervise children around the dog; children should not hug, crowd or follow a stressed dog.

Choose an adult to manage arrivals and outside doors. Ask guests not to open gates or let the dog out without checking.

Holiday food and decoration dangers

Keep chocolate, grapes and raisins, xylitol-containing products and fatty scraps out of reach. Food gifts under the tree may smell inviting even through wrapping, so store them somewhere inaccessible.

Tinsel, ribbon, lights, cords and ornaments can create swallowing, injury or electrical hazards. Secure the tree, manage access and place tempting decorations where your dog cannot reach them. A pet gate is useful only if it safely prevents access and your dog does not climb or chew it.

Clear wrapping and ribbon as gifts are opened. Keep trash and unattended plates secure, and tell guests that the dog's food comes through the designated caregiver.

If your dog eats something risky, contact your vet or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435 promptly (a consultation fee may apply). Keep packaging or ingredient details and note what was eaten and when. Do not induce vomiting unless a veterinary professional tells you to.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

Christmas Day

Begin with a recognizable morning: a toilet opportunity, suitable walking or sniffing time, usual food and a chance to settle. You do not need a perfectly normal schedule, but familiar events can provide structure.

Assign someone to notice when the dog needs a break. Yawning, retreating, restlessness or difficulty settling are reasons to reduce activity rather than encourage more greetings.

Offer quiet company away from the crowd. A familiar food toy may help a comfortable dog enjoy that break, but do not use it to keep a worried dog in an overwhelming situation; it is enrichment, not a treatment for panic.

If the family goes out, use the care arrangement already planned. Christmas Day does not make an unsupported absence easier for the dog.

New Year's Eve: fireworks and late nights

Do not leave an anxious dog alone during midnight fireworks. Arrange for someone the dog trusts to be home, and plan around the whole noisy period rather than the countdown alone. Fireworks may begin earlier or continue afterward.

The same planning works in summer: see a July 4th plan for a dog home alone during fireworks, with a timeline from four weeks out to the next morning.

Talk to your vet well before New Year's Eve if your dog has a history of panic or an existing noise or anxiety plan. Follow that plan and clarify any questions in advance.

Prepare a familiar den or resting area with water and bedding. Close curtains, keep windows and outside doors secure, and offer company without forcing interaction or preventing safe hiding.

A fan, calm music or white noise may soften some sound. Test it beforehand at a comfortable volume; louder sound is not necessarily better. Sound masking does not treat separation anxiety or noise phobia.

Plan the final toilet trip before the expected fireworks begin, using secure equipment. If another trip is needed later, prioritize safety and keep it brief. Check that the ID tag and microchip contact details are current, because a startled dog can bolt.

Our noise phobia guide and fireworks protocol cover those topics separately.

Back to normal in January

After late nights and extra company, rebuild the usual walk, meal and sleep pattern gradually. Give your dog undisturbed rest and restore any resting space changed for decorations.

Check short departures before assuming your dog can immediately handle the previous workday routine. Use a camera, watch live and keep the first attempts easy enough that you can return before worry develops.

If distress appears, return calmly and make the next practice easier. Keep care cover available while you reassess.

Our after-vacation guide covers returning to routine, and the training plan explains gradual absence work. A difficult day is information to use, not a reason to push harder.

Holiday season checklist

Christmas and New Year's Plan for a Dog With Separation Anxiety: holiday season checklist

  • Map outings and dog care on one calendar.
  • Include travel time, late finishes and possible delays.
  • Identify gaps beyond your dog's current comfort.
  • Confirm carers and their actual availability.
  • Trial unfamiliar care arrangements before relying on them.
  • Keep regular toilet, food and activity opportunities.
  • Preserve a quiet resting place with company when needed.
  • Explain guest, child and doorway rules.
  • Secure the tree, lights, cords and ornaments.
  • Keep tinsel, ribbon and food gifts inaccessible.
  • Save your vet and emergency clinic contacts.
  • Arrange trusted company for New Year's Eve.
  • Discuss any existing noise or anxiety plan with the vet early.
  • Check ID tags and microchip contact details.
  • Prepare curtains, water and a familiar resting area before fireworks.
  • Plan care cover for the return to work in January.

When to get extra help

Contact your vet if your dog is not eating, suddenly changes behavior, seems unwell or develops severe panic. Do not assume every new problem is caused by holiday excitement.

Injury, dangerous escape attempts or possible ingestion of risky food or decorations need prompt veterinary advice. Severe signs such as collapse or trouble breathing require emergency care.

Ask a qualified, reward-based behavior professional for help if your dog cannot settle with available carers or struggles with tiny absences. It is reasonable to simplify plans or miss an outing while you arrange safer support.

Never punish barking, damage or accidents, use bark-control devices, or leave your dog to cry until quiet.

Frequently Asked Questions: Christmas, New Year’s and Separation Anxiety

Can I leave my dog alone on Christmas?
Only within what your dog has shown they can handle comfortably, accounting for changes in the day. If the outing exceeds that ability, arrange suitable company or take turns staying home. Do not assume a busy morning makes a longer absence safe.
How do I keep my dog calm during holiday parties?
Preserve toilet, food and activity routines and provide quiet breaks with familiar company. Let your dog choose whether to interact and ask guests to leave their resting place undisturbed. Manage children, other pets and outside doors carefully.
Is it OK to leave my dog alone on New Year's Eve?
Do not leave an anxious dog alone during midnight fireworks. Arrange trusted company through the expected noisy period and have a backup if plans change. A camera, sound machine or food toy does not replace that support.
How do I keep my dog safe around the Christmas tree?
Secure the tree and prevent access to lights, cords, ornaments, tinsel and ribbon. Keep food gifts away and clear wrapping as presents are opened. Supervise interactions and use a safe boundary your dog accepts if needed.
How do I get my dog back on routine after the holidays?
Gradually restore familiar meals, walks and rest, then check easy short departures on camera. Use easier practice if your dog becomes worried and keep care cover available. Seek help if distress continues or behavior changes suddenly.
