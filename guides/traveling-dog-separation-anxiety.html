Traveling With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety: Hotels, Rentals and Visiting Family

Traveling with an anxious dog? Plan hotels, rentals and family visits around comfortable company, familiar routines and safe care when you need to go out.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 25 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Plan travel around keeping your dog with a trusted person, checking accommodation policies and allowing quiet settling time. Assume alone time will be harder somewhere new, and never leave your dog in a room where they may panic or in a parked car.

  • Pet-friendly accommodation does not necessarily allow dogs to stay alone.
  • Bring familiar belongings and keep meals, walks and rest reasonably consistent.
  • Arrange suitable company before booking activities your dog cannot join.

Taking your dog on vacation can solve one problem—finding care at home—while creating another. Dinner reservations, museums, weddings and even checking in can involve separations your dog is not ready to handle in an unfamiliar place.

A workable trip starts with those ordinary moments, not just the destination. We are dog owners, not vets or trainers. This guide helps you plan accommodation, company and familiar routines so traveling together does not depend on leaving your dog to struggle.

Why travel is hard for dogs with separation anxiety

New rooms bring unfamiliar smells, footsteps, doors and sleeping arrangements. Your dog also loses the familiar surroundings that may help them rest at home.

The ASPCA’s separation anxiety advice identifies changes such as moving to a new residence or a changed schedule as possible triggers. A temporary stay is different from moving, but unfamiliar surroundings still deserve a cautious plan.

Assume your dog will manage less alone time away than at home. Comfortable home absences do not automatically transfer to a hotel, rental or relative’s house.

Should your dog come at all?

Consider the whole trip: transport, accommodation, activities, sleep and care. A quiet stay with familiar people may suit your dog better than a busy itinerary involving repeated handovers.

Traveling for Thanksgiving or another holiday? Our guide to Thanksgiving and holiday travel with a dog who has separation anxiety has a holiday decision table (bring your dog, sitter, boarding or a friend) and a Thanksgiving week checklist.

Ask whether someone can remain with them whenever needed. If the answer depends on them suddenly managing several hours alone, change the plan.

A familiar sitter or carefully trialed boarding arrangement may be kinder for some dogs. Compare our boarding guide and care options guide. Neither travel nor boarding suits every anxious dog.

Before you book

Ask the property directly whether dogs may be left unattended, including with a sitter. Confirm restrictions involving crates, shared areas, size, other animals and housekeeping. Read the current written policy rather than relying on a booking-site label.

Choose practical surroundings: easy potty access, reliable cooling, secure exits and room to rest away from busy corridors. Ground-floor access may help some households, but check the actual layout.

Plan dog-friendly activities and identify a backup caregiver. Review our car travel anxiety guide before departure if the journey itself is difficult.

Trip packing list for an anxious dog

Pack familiar items your dog already uses safely. Check accommodation restrictions before bringing bedding or equipment.

  • Usual food with enough for unexpected delays.
  • Familiar food and water bowls.
  • Fresh water for the journey.
  • Familiar bed or blanket.
  • A suitable stuffed rubber food toy.
  • Safe, familiar enrichment supplies.
  • A secure leash and well-fitting harness.
  • Current identification and microchip contact details.
  • Waste bags and cleanup supplies.
  • Towels and a spare washable blanket.
  • Relevant health and vaccination records.
  • Your vet’s contact information.
  • Details of an emergency clinic near the destination.
  • A written care sheet for anyone helping.
  • Confirmed sitter details and a backup plan.
  • Any familiar travel restraint or comfortable crate used safely.

A crate belongs on the list only if your dog already relaxes in it. Never use one to contain panic in an unfamiliar room.

The first hour after arrival

If practical, let one person check the room while another stays with your dog. Look for accessible rubbish, dropped food, cords, unsecured windows and doors that open unexpectedly.

Offer a quiet potty and sniffing opportunity, then let your dog investigate the accommodation at their own pace. Avoid a stream of greetings or an immediate outing to a crowded attraction.

Set out familiar bedding and water, and stay nearby while they rest. Offer their familiar food toy if they want it. Refusal can indicate worry, although appetite changes can also have physical causes.

Hotels

Never leave your dog alone in a hotel room if they may panic. Barking, scratching, door damage and escape attempts can affect their safety, neighboring guests and staff.

Coordinate housekeeping so someone responsible remains with your dog or takes them out. A “do not disturb” request does not replace a care plan or override property procedures.

Ask where potty breaks are permitted and how to reach them without overwhelming your dog. If a required crating policy conflicts with your dog’s comfort, choose different accommodation rather than forcing confinement.

Vacation rentals

A private rental may be quieter, but privacy does not establish that alone time is allowed or comfortable. Confirm unattended-pet rules and whether an outside sitter may enter.

Check fences in person. Photographs may not show loose panels, low sections or gaps beneath gates. Keep your dog supervised outside rather than assuming the yard is secure.

Choose a resting spot away from busy windows. Our guide to dog anxiety after moving offers ideas for introducing unfamiliar surroundings without rushing independence.

Staying with family or friends

Agree on dog care before arrival. “Someone will be around” is too vague if everyone assumes somebody else is responsible.

Moving between duty stations? a deployment and PCS plan for military families covers temporary lodging and the first weeks in a new home.

Explain door and gate routines, safe handling and whether your dog settles with other people. Keep children and resident pets from disturbing the dog’s resting or feeding space.

Let greetings be optional. Your dog does not need to accept hugs, lap time or play to be polite.

Name the adult taking over before you leave a room or the property. Trial that handover while you remain available to return.

When you need to go out without your dog

Plan coverage before booking the activity. Take turns, choose a dog-friendly alternative or use a caregiver your dog has met and tolerated comfortably.

Do not treat the vacation as a chance to test a long absence. Where property rules permit and your dog is comfortable, any practice should begin with very short separations and immediate ability to return.

Dogs Trust’s separation anxiety advice describes plans progressing to very brief absences and gradually changing how the dog feels about being left. Our separation anxiety training plan explains that work; an unfamiliar room calls for an easier starting point.

General guidance in How Long Can a Dog Be Left Alone? cannot establish your dog’s limit in a new setting. A camera can provide information, but cannot replace company or stop an escape attempt.

The trip home

Protect rest before departure and plan suitable potty and water stops. Use your dog’s familiar travel restraint, and secure them before opening vehicle doors.

Never leave your dog in a parked car, including during meal or shopping stops. Take turns or choose an arrangement where the dog can safely accompany you.

At home, return to familiar routines without immediately testing a long absence. Travel fatigue does not prove emotional readiness. If separation seems harder, resume easier practice and appropriate care cover.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not assume “pet-friendly” means unattended dogs are permitted, or that another person’s presence automatically reassures your dog.

Avoid a packed first day, forced greetings and unfamiliar confinement. Never shut a panicking dog in a crate.

Do not punish barking or damage, use aversive collars or leave your dog to cry until exhausted. A food toy cannot make an overwhelming absence manageable.

Finally, avoid keeping an unsuitable itinerary simply because it is booked. Shortening or changing a trip can be the most practical response to significant distress.

When to get extra help

Talk to your vet before travel if your dog panics quickly, has injured themselves or is not improving. The ASPCA advises ruling out medical issues before behavior work.

Dogs Trust also recommends veterinary advice when behavior has recently changed. Do not assume every new symptom away from home is anxiety.

Seek prompt veterinary help for injury, dangerous escape attempts or illness signs. A qualified, reward-based behavior professional can help assess travel and care arrangements.

If your dog remains very distressed, shorten the trip or switch to a previously trialed, suitable care option. Do not move them into unfamiliar boarding without assessing whether that would be safer.

Bottom line

Frequently Asked Questions: Traveling With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety

Can I leave my dog alone in a hotel room?
First check whether the hotel permits unattended dogs. Even where allowed, do not leave your dog if they may panic, damage the room or attempt escape. Arrange suitable company rather than relying on a camera or food toy.
How do I travel with a dog that has separation anxiety?
Plan transport, accommodation and activities around keeping your dog with someone they trust. Bring familiar belongings, preserve ordinary routines where practical and allow quiet settling time. Trial caregivers before relying on them for outings.
Is it better to board a dog with separation anxiety or take them?
Neither option suits every dog. Compare how your dog handles travel, unfamiliar places, caregivers and overnight arrangements through manageable trials. A familiar in-home sitter may be preferable when both travel and boarding are difficult.
How do I help my dog settle at a relative’s house?
Start with a quiet potty opportunity, gradual exploration and familiar bedding and water. Keep greetings optional and protect a resting area from interruptions. Agree who watches your dog and how doors, gates and resident pets will be managed.
What if my dog panics on the trip?
Reduce demands, keep them safely with a trusted person and reconsider the immediate plan. Contact your vet for severe distress, injury, illness signs or sudden changes. Shortening the trip or using suitable, previously trialed care is preferable to repeatedly leaving your dog overwhelmed.
