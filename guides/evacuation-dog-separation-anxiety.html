Hurricane and Wildfire Evacuation With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety

Plan a hurricane or wildfire evacuation with a dog who has separation anxiety: a go-bag, safe places to stay, a caregiver handover and settling back in.

Quick answer

Evacuating with a dog who has separation anxiety means planning for more than transport. You may need somewhere quiet to rest, a caregiver who understands separation distress and a workable alternative if you cannot stay together.

We are dog owners who researched this topic, not vets, trainers, product testers or emergency officials, and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. Prepare ahead where possible, but always follow local officials' evacuation orders and current safety instructions. Your dog's care plan should help you leave safely, not hold you back.

Why evacuation is hard on a dog with separation anxiety

A sudden departure can remove nearly everything familiar at once: home, routine, resting places and sometimes the person your dog relies on most.

Crowded spaces, strangers and unfamiliar confinement may add difficulty. A dog who manages short absences at home may panic when left in temporary accommodation.

Some dogs settle with another trusted person. Others remain distressed when a particular family member leaves, even with company present. Knowing that difference beforehand helps you choose caregivers.

Watch for pacing, howling, panting, drooling, exit scratching or attempts to escape. Quietness alone does not prove comfort.

During an emergency, prioritize safe transport and supported care. This is not the time to test how long your dog can manage alone.

Make your evacuation plan before the season

Know your evacuation zone, where applicable, and the routes recommended by local officials. Identify more than one possible destination because access and availability can change.

Ready.gov's “Prepare Your Pets for Disasters” page says pets should be included in your family's emergency plan. It says that if local officials ask you to evacuate, your pet should evacuate too, and that pets left behind may end up lost or injured. It also notes that many public shelters and hotels do not allow pets inside, so know a safe place you can take your dog before an emergency happens.

Call ahead and check possible arrangements: friends or relatives outside the affected area, pet-friendly lodging, boarding facilities or an emergency shelter that takes pets. Do not assume any place will accept your dog or have space.

The ASPCA's disaster preparedness page also says not to leave your pets behind and to arrange a safe haven ahead of time: ask your vet for a list of boarding kennels, ask your local animal shelter whether it provides emergency shelter or foster care, identify pet-friendly hotels or motels outside your immediate area, and ask friends and relatives outside the area whether they could take your pet. It also suggests choosing a designated caregiver.

Ask specifically whether you and your dog can remain together, what records are required and how an anxious dog would be managed.

Dog evacuation go-bag

Keep supplies accessible, check them regularly and replace expired or damaged items. Prepare enough for delays rather than assuming an immediate return.

Hurricane and Wildfire Evacuation With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety: dog evacuation go-bag

  • Usual food in a sealed container.
  • Clean drinking water.
  • Food and water bowls.
  • Familiar blanket or washable mat.
  • A safe, familiar toy.
  • Rubber food toy, if your dog uses it safely.
  • Well-fitting collar with current ID tag.
  • Harness and leash.
  • Spare leash.
  • Familiar crate or carrier, if comfortable and suitable.
  • Waste bags and cleanup supplies.
  • Absorbent towels and spare bedding.
  • Recent printed and digital photos.
  • Vaccination and relevant medical records.
  • Microchip number and registration contact information.
  • Regular and emergency veterinary contacts.
  • Destination and backup caregiver contacts.
  • Written caregiver handover sheet.
  • Copies of any existing veterinary care instructions.
  • Pet first-aid supplies selected with your vet.
  • Secure document pouch.
  • Phone charging equipment with the family emergency supplies.

A ready bag saves decisions, but do not delay an ordered evacuation to locate missing comfort items or paperwork.

ID, records and practice runs

Check that your dog's tag displays a current phone number and that microchip registration details are accurate. Keep a recent photo showing identifying features and another showing you together.

Store records in accessible paper and digital forms. Include health needs and handling information a caregiver might need quickly.

The ASPCA page also recommends a rescue alert sticker, placed on or near your front door, so rescue workers know pets are inside, and writing “EVACUATED” across it if you leave with your pets and time allows. Keep its information current; it does not replace taking your dog when you evacuate.

Before the season, practice calmly gathering supplies, loading the car and taking short drives. Introduce a crate or carrier gradually, using pleasant experiences without forcing the dog inside; our crates for anxious dogs guide covers this.

If a crate causes panic, discuss a suitable alternative transport arrangement with your vet ahead of time. Never shut a panicking dog in a crate and leave; a panicking dog can injure itself trying to escape. Our crate escape guide explains the warning signs.

Arrange caregiver introductions and small practice handovers while conditions are ordinary. A familiar person is easier to rely on than a stranger met during an evacuation.

Leave early, together

Where officials advise or allow early departure, use that opportunity rather than waiting until conditions deteriorate. Wildfires can change quickly, so do not assume there will be time for every planned step.

Assign responsibilities: who secures the dog, who collects the go-bag and who checks the destination. Keep the dog away from open doors during loading.

Secure your dog appropriately for travel and keep supplies reachable. Plan stops so a responsible person remains with the dog throughout. If your dog is also nervous in the car itself, our car travel anxiety guide can help before the season.

Never leave your dog behind, tied up, outside or alone in a car. If you are away when evacuation becomes necessary, contact your designated caregiver and follow official instructions for assistance. Nobody should enter an unsafe area or bypass restrictions.

For ordinary transport logistics, use our guide to traveling with a dog who has separation anxiety. During evacuation, current official directions take priority.

If you have to be split up

Tell staff or the caregiver clearly that your dog struggles with separation. Describe behavior rather than relying only on a label: “tries to chew the exit when left” is useful safety information.

Prefer a familiar designated caregiver when available, while following emergency placement instructions. Our care options guide compares sitters, boarding and family care in more depth. Confirm who receives the dog, where they will be and how you will reconnect.

Give them a concise handover covering:

  • Your contact details and a backup contact.
  • Food, water, potty and rest routines.
  • Veterinary contacts and relevant health information.
  • Current alone-time comfort and whether continuous company is needed.
  • Early distress signs, escape risks and handling sensitivities.
  • What helps the dog settle and which barriers cause difficulty.
  • Supply locations, update arrangements and reunification plans.

Ask about visits if allowed and safe. Watch whether they help your dog settle or lead to renewed distress afterward.

Keep a written record of the placement. Do not assume a busy caregiver will remember every detail from a hurried conversation.

At a shelter, hotel or family home

Call ahead and check whenever possible. Confirm whether pets are accepted, whether people and animals stay together and what procedures apply.

Create a quiet corner with familiar bedding, water and a person nearby. Keep routines recognizable without expecting exact home conditions.

If your dog already rests comfortably in a crate, it may provide familiarity. A partial cover may reduce visual activity for some dogs, but only use it if familiar, safe and not restricting ventilation or increasing heat.

Do not leave a dog who panics alone in a strange room. Coordinate errands and care between adults, or ask staff about available arrangements.

Offer food without pressure. A toy may provide calm enrichment, but it is enrichment, not a treatment for panic. Our boarding guide and guide on preparing a pet sitter cover fuller caregiver arrangements; use only what fits the emergency setting.

Coming home and settling back in

Return only when local officials say it is safe. Before your dog enters the home or yard, check for broken glass, sharp debris, damaged fencing, exposed wires and other hazards. Follow official guidance about utilities, water and access.

Keep your dog leashed during the first inspection and outdoor trips. Familiar surroundings may have changed.

Restore ordinary meals, potty breaks and rest gradually. Avoid immediately adding a long absence while you handle cleanup or errands.

When your dog is comfortable, restart alone-time practice at an easy level and record it. Increase only while they remain relaxed, arranging company for longer gaps.

A difficult return does not mean previous progress has disappeared. It means the plan needs to account for what your dog has just experienced. If you end up moving to a new home instead, our dog anxiety after moving guide covers settling in.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

Ask your vet before the season about strong distress and travel stress. Preparation is especially important for a dog who panics quickly or injures themselves during escape attempts.

Contact your vet early if your dog refuses food, seems unwell or shows a sudden change. Coughing or breathing changes after smoke exposure also need prompt veterinary advice.

Breathing difficulty, collapse or serious injury require emergency care. Do not dismiss physical symptoms as anxiety.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

Seek a qualified reward-based behavior professional when distress persists or prevents workable care arrangements. Our separation anxiety red flags guide can help you organize observations, but it does not replace urgent veterinary attention.

Never punish barking, accidents or damage. Review safety, health and the support available instead.

Frequently Asked Questions: Evacuating With an Anxious Dog

How do I evacuate with a dog who has separation anxiety?
Prepare transport, destinations and a designated caregiver ahead of time. Follow local officials' evacuation orders and take your dog with you. Keep familiar company and supplies available without delaying departure for an ideal arrangement.
Can I take my dog to an evacuation shelter?
Call ahead and check; do not assume a shelter accepts pets or houses them beside their people. Ask what records and equipment are needed and how anxious dogs are managed. Keep alternative destinations in your plan.
What should be in a dog's evacuation go-bag?
Include food, water, bowls, identification, records, leashes, cleanup supplies and familiar bedding. Add caregiver instructions, veterinary contacts and any suitable transport equipment your dog already knows. Keep everything accessible and review supplies regularly.
What if I have to be separated from my dog during an evacuation?
Use the designated caregiver where possible and follow emergency placement procedures. Provide written care and safety information, confirm contact and reunification arrangements, and ask about visits if allowed. Explain escape risks and distress signs clearly.
How do I help my dog settle after we come home?
Check the home and yard for hazards first, then restore predictable care and quiet rest. Restart alone-time practice gently rather than immediately resuming long absences. Contact the vet for food refusal, illness or significant behavior changes.
