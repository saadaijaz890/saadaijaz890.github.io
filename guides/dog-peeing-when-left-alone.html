Dog Peeing or Pooping When Left Alone: Anxiety, House-Training or Medical?

Dogs may pee or poop when left alone because of training gaps, illness, or anxiety. Learn what to check, how to clean, and how to plan better potty breaks.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 24 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Accidents when alone can reflect incomplete house-training, too long between potty breaks, illness, or separation distress; marking is different. Start with a vet check, especially for new accidents, then improve the toilet schedule, clean thoroughly, and practice gradual alone time if anxiety contributes.

  • New accidents in a previously house-trained dog deserve a vet check.
  • Record when accidents happen and whether other distress signs appear.
  • Offer more potty opportunities; never punish accidents or restrict drinking water.

Coming home to a puddle or a soiled crate is frustrating, especially when your dog seems reliably house-trained while you are home. The mess alone cannot tell you whether your dog needed an earlier break, felt frightened, or has a medical problem.

We are dog owners, not vets, and this guide offers practical guidance rather than a diagnosis. The useful starting point is to investigate the pattern, protect your dog's comfort, and make the next absence easier to manage.

Why dogs pee or poop when left alone

There are three main explanations, and they can overlap.

Incomplete house-training or a long wait: Your dog may not understand the outdoor-only rule in every situation, or the gap between breaks may exceed their current ability. Puppies, newly adopted dogs, and dogs adjusting to a new schedule often need a refresher.

Medical problems: Urinary infections, bladder stones, kidney or hormone problems, diarrhea, age-related incontinence, and some medications can change toilet needs. A dog who previously waited comfortably may suddenly need much more frequent access outside.

Separation-related distress or fear: Some dogs eliminate while panicking after their person leaves. Accidents alongside pacing, drooling, barking, escape attempts, or damage around doors make distress more concerning. Outside noises can also frighten a dog who is alone.

Marking is separate from emptying a full bladder. It often involves small amounts of urine on particular objects or vertical surfaces. However, appearance alone cannot exclude illness.

Rule out medical causes first

Book a vet visit before treating new accidents as a training problem, particularly in a previously house-trained adult or senior dog. VCA's house-soiling guidance explains that illness, discomfort, and medications can affect elimination.

Tell your vet when the change started, whether urine amounts or drinking have changed, and whether stool is loose. Mention accidents during sleep, discomfort, appetite changes, and every medication or supplement your dog receives.

Repeated straining with little or no urine needs urgent attention. If your dog cannot pass urine, seek emergency veterinary care rather than waiting for a training appointment.

Do not restrict water to prevent puddles or stop prescribed medication without veterinary advice.

How long can a dog hold it?

Bladder capacity, bowel habits, and emotional comfort are different limits. A dog might physically hold urine longer than they can comfortably cope alone.

As general planning guidance:

  • Young puppies often need a trip after waking, eating, drinking, or playing, sometimes every 30–60 minutes while awake.
  • Puppies around three to four months may manage roughly one to two hours between daytime opportunities, but many need more frequent trips.
  • Healthy adult dogs often benefit from daytime breaks about every four to six hours. Some need shorter intervals.
  • Seniors and dogs with illness, incontinence, diarrhea, or medication-related changes may need substantially more access outside.

These are starting points, not targets or guarantees. Overnight sleep does not prove that the same interval is comfortable during an active day.

For a long workday, arrange a walker, sitter, trusted neighbor, or lunchtime visit. Count commuting time too.

Before-you-leave potty checklist

  • Offer an unhurried potty trip immediately before leaving.
  • Confirm your dog actually peed or pooped; being outside is not enough.
  • Reward outdoor elimination immediately after your dog finishes.
  • Plan the next break around your dog's observed needs.
  • Leave fresh drinking water available.
  • Provide a comfortable, easy-to-clean area your dog already accepts.
  • Remove access to repeatedly soiled spots after cleaning them.
  • Set a camera to observe departure behavior and accident timing.
  • Arrange a separate indoor toilet area if outside breaks cannot meet your dog's needs.

Accidents in the crate

A crate accident does not automatically mean your dog needs a smaller crate. The stay may be too long, your dog may be ill, or confinement may be causing panic.

A very roomy puppy crate can allow a sleeping area at one end and a toilet corner at the other. A divider may help house-training, but your dog must still be able to stand, turn around, and lie comfortably.

Never reduce space to force a frightened or physically uncomfortable dog to hold on. If your dog drools, bites bars, scratches frantically, or tries to escape, stop using that setup during absences. Consider a dog-proofed room or pen they tolerate while seeking help.

Clean it properly

Blot fresh urine or lift solid waste without grinding it into the surface. Apply an enzyme stain and odor cleaner according to its label, including the required contact time.

Treat the depth of the contamination: cleaning the carpet surface may leave urine in the backing or padding. Patch-test delicate materials and keep pets away until the area is ready for use.

Avoid ammonia-based cleaners, whose odor can resemble urine and may encourage re-marking. Never mix cleaning chemicals.

Old, soaked-in stains may need repeat treatment, a carpet extractor used according to product instructions, or professional cleaning. Some damaged padding needs replacement. A black light can help locate possible old stains, but other substances fluoresce too.

Cleaning removes lingering cues; it does not resolve urgency, incomplete training, or fear.

Alone-time plan

If medical needs and the potty schedule are addressed but distress remains, rebuild comfort with absences.

  1. Establish a comfortable starting point. Offer a potty break, gentle activity, and settling time. Observe your dog while you move around the home. Identify the point where they remain relaxed rather than waiting for an accident or panic.
  2. Practice independence while present. Reward resting on a bed while you sit nearby, then take a small step away. Return before worry builds. A gate is useful only if your dog remains comfortable behind it.
  3. Make departure cues ordinary. Pick up keys or put on shoes without leaving every time. Keep these rehearsals brief and calm. If a cue already causes pacing, simplify the exercise.
  4. Start with absences your dog can manage. This may mean stepping outside for seconds. Use a camera and return before distress begins. Food can be offered, but eating alone is not proof of relaxation.
  5. Adjust to the whole dog's behavior. Watch posture, breathing, pacing, vocalizing, and whether your dog settles. Increase time cautiously after comfortable repetitions. If distress appears, make the next practice easier.
  6. Arrange care beyond current tolerance. Use a sitter, familiar caregiver, or another suitable arrangement for necessary longer absences. Potty access and emotional comfort both matter; a midday break may address only one.
  7. Track progress and involve professionals. Record absence length, toilet timing, accidents, and distress signs. Our separation anxiety guide and Separation Anxiety or Boredom? 10 Signs can help you organize observations for your vet or behavior professional.

There is no required weekly progression. For true panic, ask your vet whether anti-anxiety medication could support the behavior plan.

When you come home to a mess

Take a breath, check your dog, and offer a calm potty opportunity. Look for blood, diarrhea, injury, or signs that your dog is unwell before cleaning up.

Scolding afterward does not teach the outdoor toilet rule. A lowered head or avoidance when you discover the mess is not evidence of spite or an understanding of your complaint.

Clean calmly and change the next day's plan. An earlier break, medical appointment, or easier absence is more useful than a stronger reaction.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming every accident means separation anxiety.
  • Waiting for repeated accidents before investigating a sudden change.
  • Withholding water or expecting your dog to “learn to hold it.”
  • Leaving a dog longer because a camera or pee pad is available.
  • Using a crate that triggers panic.
  • Punishing, rubbing your dog's nose in waste, or using frightening devices.
  • Treating diapers or cleaning products as solutions to the underlying cause.

A house-training refresher should create more opportunities to succeed: accompany your dog outside, reward promptly, supervise indoors when home, and keep a predictable schedule.

See the vet if

  • Your dog strains repeatedly, passes very little urine, or cannot urinate; inability to urinate is an emergency.
  • You notice blood in urine or stool.
  • Drinking or urine volume increases noticeably.
  • A previously house-trained adult suddenly starts having accidents.
  • A senior dog develops accidents, wet bedding, or other behavior changes.
  • Accidents accompany vomiting, persistent diarrhea, pain, lethargy, or reduced appetite.
  • Panic, escape attempts, or self-injury accompany accidents; request behavior support too.

A qualified reward-based trainer can help refresh house-training. Separation panic may need your vet and a veterinary behaviorist working together.

Bottom line

Accidents while alone are information about your dog's needs. Start with health, provide realistic toilet access, and investigate departure behavior before deciding anxiety is responsible.

Clean thoroughly, reward the right toilet location, and make absences manageable. Products can reduce mess and improve observation, but lasting progress depends on addressing the cause.

Frequently Asked Questions: Dog Peeing or Pooping When Left Alone

Why does my dog pee in the house only when I leave?
Your dog may be waiting too long for a break, have incomplete house-training, or experience separation distress. A medical problem can also become noticeable during longer absences, so arrange a vet check, especially if this is new. A camera can reveal whether accidents follow pacing, barking, or other distress.
Is my dog peeing out of spite?
Spite is not a useful explanation for house-soiling. Investigate toilet access, health, marking, and fear instead. Punishment after an accident can make your dog afraid of your response without teaching where to go.
Can separation anxiety cause pooping in the crate?
Separation-related distress can cause bowel accidents, including in a crate. However, diarrhea, an overdue break, or confinement distress can look similar. If your dog panics in the crate, stop that arrangement and seek veterinary and behavior guidance.
How long can I leave my dog without a potty break?
That depends on age, health, activity, and the individual dog; emotional tolerance may be shorter than bladder capacity. Many healthy adults benefit from breaks about every four to six daytime hours, while puppies and some seniors need much more frequent access. Use your dog's comfortable pattern rather than stretching toward a maximum.
Should I use pee pads for a dog who has accidents when alone?
Pads can provide a practical toilet option when outside breaks cannot meet your dog's needs. Put them in a consistent location away from sleeping and feeding areas, and reward appropriate use. They should support a care plan while you address illness, training gaps, or anxiety.
