Babysitting the Grandkids With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety

Plan babysitting days with familiar dog care, slow and gentle practice, overnight backup and calm returns when your dog has separation anxiety.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 5 Nov 202610 min read
Quick answer

Arrange familiar care for the full babysitting absence, including travel, school pickups and delays. Build comfortable alone time separately before the schedule starts; bringing your dog only works when the household suits them and an adult supervises whenever dogs and children are together.

  • Trial familiar care before regular babysitting begins.
  • Count travel, pickups and possible late finishes.
  • Arrange overnight company when the parents travel.

Helping with your grandchildren can bring a lovely new rhythm to the week. But regular afternoons, school pickups or overnight sitting may mean much longer absences for a dog who has become used to your company.

We are dog owners who researched this topic, not vets, trainers or product testers, and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. This guide focuses on building a workable babysitting routine while keeping your dog's care covered, rather than expecting them to adjust to long absences immediately.

Why babysitting days are hard on an anxious dog

After retirement or years of a flexible routine, your dog may expect you home for most meals, walks and quiet afternoons. Several babysitting days each week can change that suddenly. If your dog is most attached to you, our guide for a dog attached to one person may help too.

The ASPCA's page on separation anxiety describes separation anxiety as distress when dogs are left alone or separated from the people they're attached to, and explains that treatment focuses on gradually teaching the dog to be comfortable alone.

Babysitting adds uncertainty too. A school pickup can become a long afternoon, and parents may arrive home later than expected.

Your dog needs a plan based on what they comfortably manage today. The fact that you are helping family does not make the absence feel shorter to them.

Plan your dog's care before the schedule starts

Write down your actual departure and return times, including driving, pickups and handovers. Agree with your adult children how changes will be communicated.

Then book care for the periods your dog cannot comfortably manage alone. Options include a familiar sitter, neighbor, family member or daycare tried beforehand.

A walker can meet walking and potty needs, but a short visit cannot cover panic during the gaps. Pair walking with a familiar helper who stays when needed.

Trial the arrangement before regular babysitting starts. Check whether your dog settles after you leave, rather than relying on a friendly introduction while you are present.

Name a backup for late finishes or a helper becoming unavailable. Our care options guide and daycare vs dog walker guide explain the broader choices.

Build alone time slowly

Start before the new schedule if possible, but do not make the first babysitting day a training deadline. Arrange care while practice continues.

Below your dog's threshold means keeping practice short enough that they remain comfortable. That might initially involve seconds rather than minutes.

Record short departures and watch for relaxed behavior. Repeated pacing, vocalizing, panting, door scratching or inability to settle suggest the session needs adjusting.

Increase in small steps only while your dog stays comfortable, with easier sessions included. If distress appears, return calmly and make the next session simpler; do not wait for silence before helping.

Practice separately from real babysitting commitments, when you can return promptly. Our separation anxiety training plan covers the fuller process, and our guide on how long a dog can be left alone explains why there is no single safe number of hours.

Should your dog come along?

Bringing your dog is not automatically easier. Toddlers moving around, baby gear, unfamiliar sounds, naps and a different household routine may make settling difficult.

Discuss the idea with your adult children first. Consider their house rules, your dog's response to children and whether another adult can help when your attention is occupied.

Always keep dogs and children supervised by an adult whenever they are together. Never assume a familiar, gentle or previously relaxed dog is safe with a baby or toddler.

A quiet resting area may help, but never shut your anxious dog away there to panic. Plan how they will have suitable company when you are busy elsewhere.

Try a short visit before committing to a full babysitting day. If the dog remains stressed or the supervision plan is impractical, familiar care at home may be the better arrangement. Our aging parent care guide covers a similar question when you help other family members.

Car keys, bags and other departure cues

Your dog may notice the car keys, packed tote or diaper-bag-style supplies before you reach the door. Repeating the same sequence each morning can make those objects meaningful.

Handle individual cues during ordinary home time without leaving. Pick up your keys and sit down, or put an item in your bag and continue your afternoon.

Keep practice mild enough that your dog remains comfortable. If they are already worried, simplify the activity instead of repeatedly rehearsing the whole departure.

Pack early in small, calm sessions. Let the helper arrive before you begin the final getting-ready routine, allowing time for your dog to settle.

Overnight babysitting when the parents travel

An overnight at your adult children's home needs a separate dog-care arrangement. Never leave your dog alone overnight with only brief visits planned.

A familiar sitter staying in your home or a trusted person hosting your dog may work, following a successful trial. Check daytime gaps as well as bedtime coverage.

Leave meals, walks, sleeping preferences and distress signs in writing. Include veterinary contacts and who takes over if the arrangement changes.

Confirm care through your actual return, especially when the parents' travel may be delayed. Our pet sitter prep guide covers a fuller handover.

Never alone in a car, a crate all day or the yard

Never leave your dog alone in a car during babysitting or school pickup, in any weather.

Never leave your dog tied up outside or alone in a yard as a substitute for company, and never leave them crated alone for the whole day.

A crate only has a role if your dog is already comfortable using it. Never confine a panicking dog and leave them to struggle.

A camera cannot make any unsupported arrangement workable. Arrange a person who can provide care.

Staying in touch with the sitter

Agree on a manageable update routine. A message after settling and a brief report later may be more useful than repeated requests throughout the afternoon.

Ask about resting, eating, potty breaks and any difficult moments. A photograph alone cannot show how the whole day went.

Use a camera for observation, with the helper's knowledge, rather than directing every interaction remotely. Give clear instructions about when to contact you, the backup person or the vet.

If babysitting runs late, confirm extended care before the helper's agreed departure. Do not assume they can stay indefinitely.

Coming home after a long day

Greet your dog gently and offer a potty opportunity. A calm return can include ordinary affection; you do not need to ignore them.

Follow with familiar meals, a comfortable walk and quiet company. Avoid squeezing an ambitious training session into a tired evening.

You may notice more following, door-watching or difficulty relaxing. Review the caregiver's notes and recent video rather than assuming the behavior is deliberate or harmless.

Keep the next practice absence easy and adjust care if needed. Protecting predictable evenings can help make a recurring babysitting schedule more manageable for both of you.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

Contact your dog's vet early if they refuse food, seem unwell or show marked changes in sleep, energy or behavior. Strong distress, injury or escape attempts need prompt attention.

House soiling that happens when alone also deserves discussion rather than an assumption that your dog is misbehaving.

Share useful recordings and the helper's observations. A qualified reward-based behavior professional can help when comfortable absences remain short or distress continues despite care. Our separation anxiety red flags guide explains which signs are urgent.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

Never punish barking, howling, damage or accidents; they are signs of distress, not spite. Never shut a panicking dog in a crate or room and leave. Maintain suitable support while seeking help.

Bottom line

Plan dog care alongside the babysitting calendar, including travel, late finishes and overnight stays. Trial familiar help and keep alone-time practice below your dog's distress threshold.

Bringing your dog needs an honest assessment of the household and adult supervision. A dependable routine lets you help your family while keeping your dog's needs covered.

Frequently Asked Questions: Babysitting the Grandkids With an Anxious Dog

Can I leave my dog home alone while I babysit my grandkids?
Only for an absence they currently handle comfortably, including your travel time. Arrange suitable company for the remaining hours. A walking visit cannot cover distress between visits.
Should I bring my anxious dog when I babysit?
Consider the household, children, unfamiliar equipment and your ability to supervise. Try a short visit first, with your adult children's agreement. Familiar care at home may be more manageable if your attention will be occupied.
Is it safe to have my dog around a toddler or baby?
No one can promise how any dog will behave around children, even a dog who has been relaxed before. Always keep dogs and children supervised by an adult whenever they are together. If you cannot provide that supervision, arrange another adult and a workable dog-care setup.
Who can watch my dog on babysitting days?
Consider a familiar sitter, neighbor, family member or daycare tried beforehand. A walker can work alongside a helper who stays during unsupported periods. Trial the arrangement and confirm backup coverage.
Why is my dog so clingy when I get home?
Following you more closely can accompany a changed routine or difficulty during your absence, but it does not identify the cause by itself. Review how the day went and restore familiar quiet time. Contact your dog's vet early if they refuse food, seem unwell or have other marked changes.
