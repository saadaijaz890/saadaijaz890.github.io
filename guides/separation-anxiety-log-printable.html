Separation Anxiety Log for Dogs: Free Printable Daily Tracking Sheet

Track your dog's alone-time behavior with a free printable separation anxiety log. Record worry signs, daily changes, and honest weekly progress.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 24 Oct 20269 min read
Quick answer

Log each absence with its timing, location, observed behavior, first sign of worry, and any changes that day. Review patterns across one to two weeks rather than judging a single day; leave missing records blank and share concerns with your vet or qualified behavior professional.

  • Record every absence, including difficult ones, using specific observations.
  • Blank days are missing information, not evidence of improvement.
  • Compare behavior across similar absences before deciding your dog is progressing.

After a difficult week, it can feel as though every departure went badly. After one quiet day, it is tempting to assume everything has improved. A simple written record gives you something more reliable than either impression.

We are dog owners, not vets or trainers. This free separation anxiety log is an observation tool, not a diagnostic test or training schedule. Copy or print the table below and use it alongside your dog's care plan.

Why keep a separation anxiety log

Memory tends to favor dramatic moments. You may remember the shredded cushion but forget that your dog rested comfortably during a shorter absence the previous day.

Once you have a few weeks of entries, look at the trend rather than single sessions. Our guide to separation anxiety progress signs, plateaus and setbacks has a weekly progress review checklist and a plateau, setback or red flag table to help you read your log.

Your log is especially useful if your vet starts a medication plan. Our guide on separation anxiety medication: what to ask your vet shows what to track week by week and what to tell your vet.

If your log shows injuries, escape attempts, missed meals or a sudden change, that is not a slow week. Read our separation anxiety red flags and when to call the vet.

A log keeps those observations together. It can show whether worry appears earlier, whether particular circumstances matter, and whether comfortable behavior becomes more consistent.

Dogs Trust's separation anxiety advice recommends filming dogs during absences and gathering videos, diaries, or notes to share with a vet. Your written log provides context for that footage.

The ASPCA's separation anxiety guide lists stress signs to watch for as absences get longer, including dilated pupils, panting, yawning, salivating, trembling, pacing and an over-the-top greeting. Those are useful things to note in each log entry.

Keep entries brief enough to maintain. Aim for about one to two minutes of writing a day when practical, although reviewing recordings or logging several departures may take longer.

What to record each day

Use one row for each absence. If you leave several times, add rows rather than combining different experiences into a single “good” or “bad” day.

Record:

  • The date and actual departure time.
  • How long you were gone, including brief practice absences.
  • Where your dog stayed and whether anyone else was present.
  • Signs observed, with the source identified.
  • When the first concerning behavior appeared.
  • Whether your dog settled: yes, no, or partly.
  • What happened before departure and what changed that day.

In the final column, note relevant activity, a potty opportunity, food offered, visitors, weather or unusual noise, illness, or medication changes made by your vet. Record changes without deciding that they caused the behavior.

If worry starts while you prepare, write “before departure.” Do not force every observation into a minute after leaving.

How to fill in the log

Write the entry shortly after returning, once your dog's immediate needs are met. If you reviewed footage later, add that information without pretending you knew it earlier.

Use descriptions rather than judgments. “Whined near door from minute 1 to minute 2” is useful. “Terrible again” tells you little.

Use seconds for very brief absences. For longer recordings, note the approximate minute without inventing precision.

Identify your source:

  • “On camera” for directly recorded behavior.
  • “Found on return” when the timing is unknown.
  • “Neighbor reported” for information from someone else.
  • “Not visible” when your dog leaves the frame.

Define settling consistently. “Yes” means you observed comfortable resting; “partly” means some settling with periods of concern. Quiet but rigid door-watching should not automatically become “yes.”

If you have no usable observation, write “unknown” rather than choosing an answer. Our How to Record Your Dog When You Leave guide explains camera placement and recording limitations.

Weekly review

At the end of the week, spend a few minutes reviewing the entries together.

Separation Anxiety Log for Dogs: Free Printable Daily Tracking Sheet: weekly review

  • How many days were logged, and how many absences were recorded?
  • Which days or departures have missing information?
  • Were the absences similar enough to compare?
  • Did the first sign of worry start later or earlier than last week?
  • Did your dog settle more consistently during comparable absences?
  • Were concerning behaviors briefer, less intense, or more persistent?
  • Did recovery after your return become easier or take longer?
  • Were there new signs or changes in health?
  • Did routine, visitors, noise, weather, or caregiver arrangements change?
  • What should you discuss with your vet or behavior professional?

After one week, look for preliminary patterns and gaps in the record. After two weeks, compare the overall direction under similar conditions.

One to two weeks provides a useful review window, not a deadline for improvement. Some dogs need much longer observation and professional support.

A later first sign of worry is encouraging only in context. A dog who appears increasingly tense before that point is not necessarily becoming more comfortable.

When the log says slow down

Step back when signs repeatedly start earlier, recovery takes longer, or new behaviors appear. Do not wait for damage or loud barking before adjusting.

The useful question is whether the exercise remains comfortable, not whether your dog completed a planned duration.

Reduce the next practice to an easier level and arrange suitable care for necessary longer absences. Our Separation Anxiety Training Plan explains gradual absence work; this sheet records the response rather than prescribing the next increase.

If worry appears during shoes, keys, or bags, note it and read our Departure Cues guide. For repeated setbacks or unclear signals, ask a qualified professional to review the footage.

Sharing your log with a vet or trainer

Bring the complete record, including difficult entries and missing days. A polished-looking diary is less useful than an accurate one.

Add a short summary: what changed, what concerns you most, and which entries have matching video. Label clips with dates and timestamps so the professional can find relevant moments.

Our Separation Anxiety Vet Visit Prep guide includes a fuller history checklist.

Ask how often updates should be sent and which observations matter most for your dog. A log supports assessment and decision-making; it does not replace veterinary care or qualified behavior guidance.

Common mistakes

  • Logging only successful or particularly difficult departures.
  • Treating a missing day as a comfortable day.
  • Guessing when behavior started without footage.
  • Combining camera observations with assumptions.
  • Increasing absence length just to create a better-looking record.
  • Counting silence, eating, or stillness as automatic relaxation.
  • Ignoring changes in health or daily circumstances.
  • Treating the log as a score for your dog instead of a record of how they cope.

If there was no absence that day, write a separate note such as “not alone; sitter present.” That is useful management information, not a successful alone-time trial.

When to get help now

Contact your vet promptly about self-injury, dangerous escape attempts, sudden behavior changes, or refusal to eat or drink. Explain whether appetite and drinking changes occur only during absences or also while you are home.

Active injury, breathing difficulty, collapse, or suspected poisoning requires urgent veterinary attention.

Stop distressing observations rather than continuing for a complete entry. Do not keep a panicking dog in a crate because you are documenting it.

A partial log is enough to request help. Your dog's safety comes before collecting a full week of data.

Bottom line

Keep the record short, specific, and honest. Log every absence, preserve missing information as missing, and review patterns across comparable days.

The purpose is to understand your dog and support better decisions—not to produce a perfect diary or a steadily rising absence duration.

Frequently Asked Questions: Separation Anxiety Log

What should I write in a separation anxiety log?
Record departure time, absence length, location, observed signs, first sign of worry, settling, and relevant daily changes. Include preparation such as activity or a potty break. Identify whether information came from footage, another person, or what you found on return.
How long should I keep a log for my dog?
Start with one to two weeks to establish a useful pattern, then continue as your vet or behavior professional recommends. Keep logging during changes to the plan or routine. The review period is not a promise that improvement should appear within that time.
What if I miss a day?
Leave it blank and mark it as unrecorded if helpful. Do not count it as a comfortable day or reconstruct details you cannot remember. If your dog was not alone, record that separately rather than labeling it a successful absence.
How do I know if my dog's separation anxiety is getting better?
Look for a repeated trend toward comfortable behavior during comparable absences, such as easier settling and fewer or briefer worry signs. One calm day, less barking alone, or a longer absence does not establish improvement. Ask your professional to help interpret uncertain patterns.
Should I share the log with my vet?
Yes, especially if behavior is new, worsening, or accompanied by health changes. Bring the complete log and relevant timestamped clips, including entries that did not go well. Do not delay an appointment while waiting to complete the sheet.
