Working Full Time With a Dog That Has Separation Anxiety: A Realistic Plan

Working full time with an anxious dog takes care cover and separate training. Build a workday schedule, plan office transitions, and prepare backups.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 24 Oct 202611 min read
Quick answer

Cover workday absences that exceed your dog's current comfort with suitable people or care, while practicing short, manageable separations separately. A midday walker suits only dogs comfortable during the remaining gaps; others need continuous company while a veterinary and behavior plan develops.

  • Workday care prevents overwhelming absences; training happens in separate, manageable sessions.
  • Count commuting, meetings, overtime, and handovers when arranging coverage.
  • Trial caregivers and keep a backup ready before cancellations happen.

A nine-to-five job rarely means being out for only eight hours. Add commuting, a delayed meeting, or an errand, and a dog who struggles alone may face a much longer absence than you intended.

We are dog owners, not vets or trainers, and the products and services mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal testing. This guide focuses on organizing the working week: dependable care during work, realistic training outside it, and a backup when plans change.

Why a full workday is hard for a dog with separation anxiety

A dog who panics after a short separation cannot simply jump to an office day because your schedule requires it. Long absences beyond current tolerance can cause distress and disrupt progress.

Know your dog's current limit

Start with existing footage or a short, safe camera observation. Watch when concern begins, what it looks like, and whether your dog settles.

Pacing, repeated whining, panting, drooling, door scratching, and food abandonment can be concerning. Quiet but rigid door-watching may also deserve attention.

Do not deliberately wait for panic to discover a maximum. If your dog has injured themselves or made dangerous escape attempts, ask your vet how to proceed safely.

Use our Separation Anxiety Signs guide to organize observations. Our How Long Can a Dog Be Left Alone? guide covers general care needs, but an age-based range cannot establish an anxious dog's tolerance.

The "suspend absences" approach while you train

“Suspending absences” means avoiding separations beyond what your dog currently handles while practicing easier ones within a structured plan. It does not mean abandoning training.

The ASPCA's separation anxiety page explains that during treatment a dog with separation anxiety should not be left alone except during training sessions. It suggests that you take your dog to work if possible, having a family member, friend, or dog sitter stay with your dog, or using a sitter's home or doggy daycare.

Think of two linked schedules:

  • Workday management: company and care during necessary hours away.
  • Training practice: brief, planned absences your dog can manage comfortably.

Care arrangements may change as your dog progresses, but there is no dependable end date. Some dogs need any suitable person present; others struggle when a particular person leaves.

Trial the arrangement before relying on it. A booked sitter is useful only if the dog can settle with them.

Workday cover options

Family and friends

A familiar person may provide comfortable company with relatively little disruption. Agree on specific hours, access, and what happens if you are late.

Check that help is sustainable rather than assuming availability every week.

Pet sitter at home

An in-home sitter preserves familiar surroundings. Confirm whether they remain throughout the booking or leave for other clients and errands.

Use our Prepare a Pet Sitter guide to document routines, triggers, escape precautions, and contact instructions.

Dog walker for mild cases

A walker can work when your dog comfortably manages the gaps before and after the visit.

Choose by observed tolerance rather than the label “mild.” A brief walk does not provide continuous cover for a dog who panics soon after departure.

Doggy daycare if your dog enjoys it

Daycare may suit social dogs who relax with staff and can rest in the setting. It can overwhelm dogs uncomfortable with noise, groups, or unfamiliar confinement.

Ask whether rest periods include being left alone and arrange a gradual trial.

Taking your dog to work

An approved dog-friendly workplace may help if your dog settles there. Consider travel, meetings, other animals, and who stays with the dog during breaks.

Permission alone does not establish suitability.

Swapping care with another owner

A reciprocal arrangement may reduce costs when households are reliable and compatible. Dogs do not have to share space if that causes tension.

Agree on schedules, handling, food separation, and cancellations.

Working from home on some days

Remote days or split household schedules can reduce paid coverage. They also create opportunities for brief practice without a rushed departure.

Plan coverage for errands or appointments on home days too.

Hybrid and shift work

Hybrid schedules need explicit planning. A dog comfortable during short remote-day errands may still struggle on office days.

Does your job also include overnight travel? Our business trip guide covers care for 1 to 5 night trips when you live alone, a trial run, check-ins from the road and how to ask your manager about schedule changes.

Keep feeding, toilet opportunities, activity, and rest reasonably predictable while allowing flexibility. Avoid using home days to push training harder in preparation for uncovered office days.

For shift work, write down actual departure and return times, including overtime. Overnight work may require someone staying with the dog, not just an evening visit.

Build overlapping handovers if household members work different shifts. Account for their sleep and other commitments rather than assuming someone technically “at home” can always provide care.

How to train around a full-time job

Use brief sessions when your dog is relaxed and you have time to observe. Before work can suit some households, but skip it if a deadline makes you rush.

Evenings may work after a potty break, appropriate activity, and settling. On weekends, choose a few manageable opportunities rather than repeated drilling.

Return before concern begins when possible, and simplify if worry appears. Do not wait out distress to finish a planned duration.

Our Separation Anxiety Training Plan explains starting points and gradual increases. Record a short note after practice so decisions do not depend on tired end-of-day memory.

Training is not a requirement at every available moment. Recovery, ordinary companionship, and your own rest matter too.

Budget-friendly ideas

Map the whole week before buying coverage. Family help on one day, remote work on another, and paid blocks elsewhere may be more sustainable.

Working out what workday cover and training might add up to? Our guide to separation anxiety treatment costs in the US shows published examples and how to ask for a written quote.

Consider a fair care swap, staggered household schedules, or a familiar person working remotely at your home. Consolidate errands and use delivery or pickup when practical.

Discuss temporary flexibility with your employer where possible. Make requests concrete: particular days or adjusted hours are easier to plan around than an undefined emergency arrangement.

Tell your vet or behavior professional about cost constraints early. An unrealistic plan is difficult to maintain, even with good intentions.

When the plan falls apart on a busy day

Keep a short backup list in priority order: a trialed caregiver, familiar family member or friend, suitable daycare with confirmed availability, and any approved workplace option.

If a sitter cancels, contact backup help before leaving. If delayed at work, arrange someone to stay before current cover ends.

If you discover distress on camera, arrange a prompt, safe response. Do not rely on talking through the speaker or a remote treat to manage panic.

When no option is available, contact your veterinary or behavior team for practical guidance and reassess work flexibility or emergency support. Avoid trying an unfamiliar confinement setup under pressure.

One difficult day is not proof that all progress has disappeared. Document it, protect your dog, and review the weakest part of the schedule.

Workday readiness checklist

Working Full Time With a Dog That Has Separation Anxiety: A Realistic Plan: workday readiness checklist

  • Have I observed my dog's current comfort during short absences?
  • Are concerning health or behavior changes being assessed?
  • Does coverage include the full commute and likely delays?
  • Has my dog successfully trialed each caregiver or setting?
  • Are continuous-care expectations clear?
  • Do handovers overlap where needed?
  • Are water, meals, toilet breaks, activity, and rest planned?
  • Is the care sheet accessible and current?
  • Are access details and emergency contacts available?
  • Have we agreed on updates and camera privacy?
  • Is backup care confirmed?
  • Are training sessions separate from unavoidable workday absences?

Mistakes to avoid

  • Treating a workday as a training exercise.
  • Assuming a lunchtime walk covers separation panic.
  • Using food or a camera as a substitute for company.
  • Choosing daycare without checking your dog's response.
  • Increasing absences because the next office day is approaching.
  • Scolding or punishing barking, damage, or accidents, which can make distress worse.
  • Using bark, shock, or spray collars.
  • Leaving a distressed dog to cry until quiet.

Keep care needs and training goals connected without confusing them.

When to get extra help

Check with your vet and a qualified, reward-based behavior professional for moderate or severe concerns, repeated setbacks, or distress during tiny separations.

Prompt veterinary contact is important for sudden changes, food refusal, heavy drooling, or persistent panting. Injuries and dangerous escape attempts require urgent attention.

Bring representative video and explain your actual work constraints. The plan should reflect both your dog's needs and the support available.

Bottom line

Working full time with a dog who struggles alone requires two plans: suitable workday care and separate, manageable training.

Trial support, cover handovers and delays, and keep backups ready. Reduce coverage according to demonstrated comfort and professional guidance rather than a calendar deadline.

Frequently Asked Questions: Working Full Time With a Dog That Has Separation Anxiety

Can I work full time with a dog that has separation anxiety?
Many owners make it workable through suitable care, schedule adjustments, and a separate training plan. The arrangement takes effort and may involve ongoing costs. Check that your dog actually settles with the chosen caregiver rather than assuming any company is enough.
How long can I leave my dog with separation anxiety while at work?
There is no general workday allowance for a dog experiencing separation distress. Keep absences within their demonstrated comfortable range, which may initially be seconds or minutes. Arrange coverage for longer periods and seek professional guidance.
Is doggy daycare good for separation anxiety?
It can provide useful cover for dogs who enjoy the setting and relax with staff. Others find noise, groups, or confinement overwhelming. Trial it gradually and ask whether any part of the day involves being left alone.
Will a second dog help my dog while I am at work?
Another dog does not reliably resolve distress about separation from a person. It also adds care needs and can introduce new stress. Make that decision for wider household reasons rather than as an assumed solution.
How do I prepare my dog for going back to the office?
Start weeks ahead where possible by observing current comfort, arranging caregivers, and rehearsing handovers. Practice short, comfortable absences separately rather than jumping to an office day. If your dog is not ready when the return date arrives, maintain suitable care coverage.
