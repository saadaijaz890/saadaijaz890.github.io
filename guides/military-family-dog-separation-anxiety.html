Military Families: Deployment, TDY and PCS Moves With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety

Prepare an anxious dog for deployment, TDY or a PCS move with gradual care handovers, steady routines, caregiver notes and support for the parent at home.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 31 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

Start transferring everyday care before departure, practice comfortable short absences and arrange company for longer gaps. During a PCS move, expect alone time to need rebuilding in each new setting, including temporary lodging.

  • Share meals, walks and settling routines before the service member leaves.
  • Give caregivers written instructions and a trial stay.
  • Recheck alone-time comfort after moving, rather than assuming it transfers.

Deployment, temporary duty and moving orders can change a dog's household quickly. The person who usually handles breakfast or bedtime leaves, familiar routines shift, and the parent at home may be managing children, work and the dog with much less help.

We are dog owners, not vets, trainers or military advisors, and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. This guide offers practical ways to share care, prepare for departures and protect comfortable alone time through military family changes.

Why deployment and PCS moves are hard for anxious dogs

A dog may struggle with the absence of one particular person, being physically alone, or both; our separation anxiety vs isolation distress guide explains the difference. A trusted adult remaining home helps some dogs, but others still become distressed when their favorite person leaves.

The ASPCA's separation anxiety page describes it as being triggered when dogs become upset by separation from the people they're attached to. It explains that treatment aims to teach a dog to enjoy, or at least tolerate, being left alone, starting with short separations that don't cause anxiety.

Military life can combine several difficult changes: a bonded person leaves, the remaining caregiver's schedule becomes busier, and a move removes familiar surroundings. Long field training can create similar disruptions even without a change of address.

Not every quiet or clingy dog has separation anxiety. Record a manageable departure to see whether your dog rests or shows repeated pacing, howling, panting, drooling, exit scratching or an inability to settle.

Before deployment or TDY

When notice allows, begin weeks ahead. Let the person staying home gradually take over enjoyable daily activities: meals, sniffy walks, play and the bedtime routine. The departing person can stay warm and affectionate throughout.

Start with shared activities, then let the remaining caregiver handle them independently. If your dog is comfortable, practice brief departures by the service member while that caregiver stays home.

Separately, practice tiny absences with nobody home, beginning below the point where your dog worries. These are different situations, and success in one does not prove comfort in the other.

Keep goodbyes calm and familiar. Packing or handling a duffel during ordinary moments can help you notice whether those actions already trigger worry.

Keep a familiar scent item, such as a worn shirt, if your dog will not shred or swallow it. It may offer comfort, but it cannot replace company or gradual practice. With short notice, prioritize care cover over trying to rush training.

For a dog strongly attached to the departing person, our dog attached to one person guide offers more detail on sharing care.

The at-home parent's routine

Build a routine that works on a difficult weekday, not only on an ideal one. Keep a recognizable order for meals, toilet breaks, activity and rest, even when exact times vary.

A simple morning might include a toilet trip, a sniffy walk, breakfast and settling before school or work preparations. Leave room for shorter activities on demanding days.

Ask for specific help: a friend covering an appointment, a sitter staying during errands or a walker handling an afternoon outing. A walker meets activity and toilet needs, but a brief visit does not cover a dog who panics throughout the surrounding absence. Our daycare vs dog walker guide and care options guide compare the choices.

Book regular support where possible and identify a backup for illness, late work or childcare changes. The remaining parent should not have to carry every task alone.

Keep notes short: what happened during an absence, what helped and what needs changing. Share useful updates with the traveling family member when possible without making constant monitoring another obligation.

Handing your dog to a family caregiver

A loving relative still needs preparation. Check that their household, other pets, visitors and work schedule suit your dog.

Arrange a short visit together, followed by time with the caregiver while you step away. If that goes comfortably, try a short stay before relying on an extended arrangement. Do not turn the trial into a test of how much distress your dog will endure.

Send familiar bedding, usual food and safe toys. Explain the dog's current comfortable alone-time duration and how you observed it. If the caregiver leaves for work, agree who covers that absence.

Provide a written handover sheet, veterinary contacts and any authorization the clinic requires. Agree when the caregiver should call you, contact the vet or change the arrangement. A family connection does not automatically make a placement suitable, and reconsidering a struggling arrangement is reasonable. Homecoming

Choose a secure setting for the reunion. Let your dog approach at their own pace, and keep doors managed while bags and people come inside.

A warm hello is fine. Avoid crowding, forced hugs or a greeting that keeps escalating when the dog is already overwhelmed. Allow sniffing, pauses and space.

Do not immediately abandon the routines that helped during the absence. The returning person can gradually rejoin walks, meals and play while the remaining caregiver keeps a familiar role.

Leave and reintegration schedules may change again. Continue comfortable short departures rather than spending every moment together and then returning abruptly to long absences. Watch how your dog responds when the service member next leaves the house.

PCS moves with an anxious dog

Plan the dog's needs alongside travel and lodging, rather than after everything else is booked. Gather veterinary records, discuss travel concerns and confirm arrangements for every stop.

Temporary lodging deserves its own care plan. Check whether dogs may be left unattended at all. A dog who manages an absence at home may struggle in an unfamiliar room, especially with corridor noise and changing activity. Our guide to traveling with a dog who has separation anxiety covers hotels and rentals.

Plan meals, errands and appointments so someone can remain with the dog when needed. Do not assume a food toy makes an unfamiliar-room absence manageable.

On arrival, introduce a familiar bed, water and a quiet resting area. Allow supervised sniffing and calm time together before expecting independence. Keep exits secure during unloading and prevent access to packing materials.

Start alone-time practice at an easy level in the new home. That may mean seconds rather than the duration your dog handled before. Record practice and return before worry develops; if distress appears, make the next attempt easier.

Base housing and overseas moves

Pet rules in base and privatized housing vary, so check your installation's pet policy early with the housing office. For an overseas move, ask your vet and check the destination country's official pet import rules, because requirements differ from country to country.

Ask about housing restrictions, required records, identification requirements and any travel-related steps that apply to your dog. Confirm transport arrangements directly with the provider rather than assuming a previous move's process still applies.

Keep copies of important records accessible during travel. Confirm pet arrangements for temporary lodging and any backup accommodation.

This is planning guidance, not advice on military regulations. Recheck requirements when dates, routes or destinations change, and allow for the possibility that your dog's travel and the household's travel need different arrangements.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

See the vet for strong distress, sudden behavior changes or signs your dog is uncomfortable or unwell. Contact them early if your dog refuses food; do not assume missing a person explains every change.

Escape attempts, damaged teeth or nails, and self-injury need prompt veterinary attention. Breathing difficulty, collapse or serious injury require emergency care. Our separation anxiety red flags guide explains which signs are urgent.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

A qualified reward-based behavior professional can help when your dog panics quickly, cannot settle with the remaining caregiver or is not improving. Share footage, the household schedule and upcoming transitions so the plan is practical. Our guide to finding separation anxiety help in the US explains the options.

Never shut a panicking dog in a crate or room and leave. Arrange company and ask for help adapting the setup. Do not punish barking, damage or accidents after returning; respond by checking health, safety and the difficulty of the absence.

Frequently Asked Questions: Military Families and Dog Separation Anxiety

Do dogs get separation anxiety when an owner deploys?
Some dogs become distressed after an attached person leaves, while others adjust without separation anxiety. Record manageable departures to understand whether your dog struggles with that person's absence, being completely alone or both.
How do I prepare my dog for deployment?
Gradually share daily care with the person staying home, practice short comfortable departures and arrange help for longer absences. Keep goodbyes calm and seek veterinary advice before departure if distress is strong.
How do I help my dog after a PCS move?
Provide familiar bedding, steady daily care and company while your dog explores the new setting. Rebuild alone-time practice gradually and record it rather than assuming the previous home's comfortable duration transfers.
How should a homecoming with my dog go?
Keep the reunion secure, warm and unhurried, allowing your dog to choose how to approach. Continue the routines established during the absence while the returning person gradually shares care again.
Can a family member care for my anxious dog during deployment?
Yes, if the dog is comfortable with them and their household can provide the needed company. Arrange a trial stay, written instructions and a backup plan before relying on the placement. Review the arrangement if the dog remains distressed.
