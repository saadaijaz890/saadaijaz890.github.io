Selling Your Home: Showings and Open Houses With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety

Plan home showings around your anxious dog with familiar care, calm outings, a protected resting spot and backup for short-notice requests and delays.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 5 Nov 202610 min read
Quick answer

Arrange for your dog to leave with a trusted adult or go to a familiar, trialed care setting during showings. Plan the routine before listing, ask about workable showing windows, preserve your dog's resting space between visits and keep backup care ready for delays.

  • Trial an outing or familiar care setting before listing.
  • Keep your dog's safe spot available between showings.
  • Never leave your dog waiting alone in a car or crate.

Selling a home can turn ordinary afternoons into hurried departures. When your dog struggles alone, clearing the house for a showing involves more than putting away toys and picking up your keys.

We are dog owners who researched this topic, not vets, trainers or product testers (and not real estate professionals), and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. This guide focuses on organizing the selling period around your dog's needs, from the first showing request to the evenings after open-house weekends.

Why showings are hard on an anxious dog

Short-notice requests, strangers entering the home and everyone leaving together can disrupt familiar routines. Repeated showings may also mean several departures a week over a long listing period.

The ASPCA's page on separation anxiety describes separation anxiety as distress when dogs are left alone or separated from the people they're attached to, and explains that treatment focuses on gradually teaching the dog to be comfortable alone.

A showing is not a controlled training session; our separation anxiety training plan covers that slower work. Your home may be changing, and you cannot assume your dog will be comfortable alone just because the visit seems brief. Our guide on how long a dog can be left alone covers daily needs as well as emotional comfort.

Suitable company and a practiced outing routine help separate the practical needs of selling from gradual alone-time training.

Plan your showing routine before you list

Decide who handles the dog, where they go and what happens if the showing extends. Prepare an outing bag with walking equipment, water, familiar bedding and care instructions.

Ask the people arranging your showings about windows and notice that would give you time to get the dog out calmly. Explain your practical needs and ask what can be arranged rather than assuming a particular schedule is available.

Trial the complete routine before listing: packing the dog bag, leaving, arriving at the care setting and returning. Review what your dog finds comfortable.

Keep a primary option and a backup. A familiar helper who is usually available may still be unable to cover a sudden request.

Our care options guide covers broader arrangements; the showing plan needs to work repeatedly at short notice.

Who takes your dog during showings

An adult can take the dog on a calm sniffing outing if the weather, duration and dog's needs make that comfortable. A quiet walk is different from trying to keep an anxious dog moving for hours.

A familiar sitter's home may suit longer visits after a successful trial. Check whether your dog settles there with the person, rather than assuming a friendly greeting establishes comfort.

A walker plus a familiar helper can cover activity and resting time. Arrange the handover away from the showing home when everyone needs to leave.

A daycare day may work for a dog who already enjoys that setting. Trial it beforehand and confirm availability.

Our daycare vs dog walker guide explains the differences. Choose a comfortable arrangement, not simply somewhere the dog can wait.

Staging without losing your dog's safe spot

Keep your dog's familiar bed, water and resting area available during ordinary home life. Tidying should not mean repeatedly removing the things they rely on for comfort.

Use a simple storage basket for spare toys and walking supplies. Take familiar bedding to the sitter if helpful, then restore the usual setup promptly when you return.

If furniture must move, make changes gradually where practical. Keep walking routes clear and let your dog explore calmly with you present.

A crate that your dog already enjoys may remain part of their everyday setup, but it is not somewhere to leave them alone while strangers tour.

Avoid changing the resting area, departure routine and care setting all at once if smaller adjustments are possible.

Packing boxes without the panic

Boxes, tape and furniture moving may become signals that the household is about to leave. Work in short, calm sessions rather than one frantic packing burst.

Practice a small action during ordinary home time, such as moving an empty box, then return to a familiar activity. Start where your dog notices without becoming worried.

Keep boxes out of resting areas and walking routes. Arrange familiar company during especially busy staging or packing periods.

If your dog paces, pants or becomes distressed, simplify the activity. Repeating the full sequence until the dog stops responding is not the goal.

Never the car or a crate alone during a showing

Never leave your dog in a car to wait out a showing, in hot or cold weather, even for a visit you expect to be short. A short visit can still run long, and a vehicle is never a care setting.

Never leave your dog crated alone while strangers tour the home. Familiar confinement does not remove separation distress or make unexpected visitors comfortable.

Never leave your dog tied up or outside alone either. A camera cannot provide company or manage an escape attempt.

If the planned arrangement falls through, activate the familiar backup and discuss another workable showing time rather than improvising an unattended waiting place.

Open-house weekends

Book care for the full door-to-door period, including preparation, travel and any delay before returning. A longer block may need a sitter's home or another trialed setting rather than an extended walk.

Holding a spring yard sale or garage sale instead? Our yard sale and garage sale guide covers a familiar helper indoors, a tested quiet room and a door routine while shoppers come and go.

If several showings occur close together, one comfortable care visit may be easier than repeated rushed departures and returns. Base that choice on your dog's response.

Confirm meals, potty opportunities and rest during the care period. Enrichment can add a pleasant activity, but it is not a treatment for panic.

Ask for a brief update rather than frequent messages that interrupt care. If using a camera at the sitter's home, agree on its placement and use beforehand.

Coming home after a showing

Offer a gentle greeting, a potty opportunity and quiet company. Calm does not mean ignoring your dog or withholding ordinary affection.

Restore familiar bedding and bowls promptly. Give the dog time to settle before starting another round of packing or moving furniture.

During a long listing period, your dog may seek more contact in the evenings. Keep meals, walks and rest recognizable, and avoid assuming every behavior change is simply stress from selling.

Review what worked after each outing. If your dog struggles at the sitter's or remains unsettled afterward, change the arrangement before repeating it.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

Contact your dog's vet early if they refuse food, seem unwell or show a sudden behavior change. Do not assume the selling process explains everything.

Strong panic, injury, escape attempts or potty accidents only when alone need veterinary discussion. Share short clips and describe the care setting and recent household changes.

A qualified reward-based behavior professional can help if packing cues trigger strong worry or your dog cannot settle despite suitable company. Our separation anxiety red flags guide explains which signs are urgent.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

Never punish barking, howling, damage or accidents; they are signs of distress, not spite. Increase support and review the plan rather than pushing through distress.

Frequently Asked Questions: Home Showings and Open Houses With an Anxious Dog

Can I leave my dog home alone during a showing?
Plan for your anxious dog to leave with a trusted adult or go to a familiar, trialed care setting. Being alone while strangers enter is not a suitable showing arrangement. A camera does not replace care.
Can my dog stay in a crate while buyers tour?
Do not leave your dog crated alone during a showing. A crate does not remove separation distress or make unfamiliar visitors comfortable. Arrange supported care away from the home instead.
Where can my dog go during an open house?
Options include a familiar sitter's home, trialed daycare or a calm outing with an adult when appropriate. Longer periods need rest, water and suitable company, not hours of walking. Trial the arrangement before relying on it.
What if a showing is requested on short notice?
Check whether your primary or backup care arrangement is available. Discuss a workable window that gives you time to leave calmly with the dog. Never improvise by leaving your dog in a car or alone in a crate.
Why is my dog so clingy while the house is on the market?
Repeated outings, strangers and changes to the home may leave your dog seeking more contact. Preserve familiar routines and review whether the care arrangement is comfortable. Contact your dog's vet early if clinginess comes with appetite changes, illness or other unusual behavior.
