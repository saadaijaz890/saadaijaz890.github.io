Separation Anxiety in Foster Dogs: What Foster Carers Can Do

Help a foster dog cope alone with gentle practice, rescue-approved care cover, video and daily notes, plus honest information for future adopters.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 27 Oct 202610 min read
Quick answer

An unsettled foster dog does not automatically have separation anxiety. Record short absences, tell the rescue early, arrange approved company for longer gaps, and build comfortable alone time gradually while keeping useful notes for the rescue and future adopters.

  • Describe what you observe rather than diagnosing or labeling the dog.
  • Arrange rescue-approved company for absences beyond the dog's current comfort.
  • Share honest notes about what helps and what still needs support.

A foster dog may arrive with little history and plenty of uncertainty. Following you, crying at a doorway or struggling to settle can be worrying, especially when you also need to work, shop and care for your household.

You do not have to solve everything alone. We are dog owners, not vets or trainers. Your role is to provide safe care, notice patterns and work with the rescue on a realistic plan that can continue into the dog's adoptive home.

Is it separation anxiety or just settling in?

Everything may be unfamiliar: people, smells, sleeping arrangements and toilet routines. Some foster dogs stay close while learning where they are safe. Others are unusually quiet.

Neither closeness nor quietness tells you how the dog feels when left. A dog who follows you all evening might sleep comfortably during a brief departure. Another may remain silent but tremble or stand rigidly near the door.

Record a short absence before assuming. Watch for barking, howling, pacing, panting, drooling, exit damage, escape attempts or toileting that occurs specifically when alone.

These observations can suggest distress, but they do not establish a diagnosis. Medical problems, confinement discomfort and unmet needs can overlap. Our rescue dog anxiety guide covers broader settling-in support for adopted dogs.

The first few days in a foster home

Keep life manageable. Offer predictable meals, suitable walks, regular toilet opportunities and a quiet resting place. Avoid a stream of visitors or ambitious outings.

Let the dog explore safely at their own pace. Follow the rescue's instructions about introductions, household pets, secure equipment and any restrictions.

Choose a resting area based on observed comfort. A dog-proofed room or gated space may work, but a barrier can increase worry for some dogs. Test arrangements while you remain nearby.

Do not assume a supplied crate is suitable for leaving the dog alone. It should already be a place where the dog relaxes. Our crates for anxious dogs guide explains the limitations.

First week foster checklist

Separation Anxiety in Foster Dogs: What Foster Carers Can Do: first week foster checklist

  • Save the rescue's routine and emergency contacts.
  • Confirm veterinary authorization procedures.
  • Ask about known alone-time history.
  • Review secure handling and escape precautions.
  • Prepare a quiet, dog-proofed resting area.
  • Introduce barriers only while you can observe.
  • Establish regular meals and toilet opportunities.
  • Provide suitable movement and sniffing time.
  • Arrange approved company for necessary absences.
  • Test a camera with cables out of reach.
  • Record a tiny comfortable absence when appropriate.
  • Start a factual daily log.
  • Report concerns before they become unmanageable.

Working with the rescue

Tell your coordinator early. A useful message is specific: “When I stepped outside, she paced and whined, then scratched the door. I returned and have a short clip.”

Ask who assesses behavior, who authorizes veterinary care, and which trainers, sitters or facilities are approved. Confirm what to do outside normal hours and how urgent concerns should be reported.

Request a practical plan for unavoidable absences, not just training instructions. Be clear about your work schedule, household needs and what you can safely provide.

If the placement is becoming too much, say so. Asking for respite, additional support or a different arrangement protects both you and the dog. It is better to raise a limitation early than struggle silently.

Arranging care cover so they are not left alone too long

Care cover means someone suitable stays with the dog during absences beyond their comfort. Options might include another household member, an approved friend, a rescue-approved sitter or support arranged by the rescue.

Trial the arrangement before depending on it. Some dogs relax with another person; others remain distressed when a particular carer leaves. Our how long can a dog be left alone guide explains how age, health and anxiety change how long a dog can comfortably be left.

While you work on it, the ASPCA suggests avoiding leaving the dog alone except during training, for example by having a family member, friend or sitter stay with them, or using doggy daycare.

Daycare requires rescue approval and must suit the individual dog. A walker helps with toilet and activity needs but cannot cover a dog who panics before and after the visit.

Tiny absences: building alone time step by step

The ASPCA's separation anxiety advice describes treatment as helping a dog tolerate being alone by gradually getting used to short absences. Work within the rescue's agreed plan.

  1. Begin with comfortable rest. Meet toilet, food and activity needs, then allow time to settle.
  2. Try a small movement away. Step back briefly or move around the room without requiring the dog to stay.
  3. Practice a very short separation. Depending on the dog, this might be a moment beyond an inside doorway or a few seconds outside.
  4. Watch live. Use the camera and remain close enough to return promptly.
  5. Return before worry develops. If distress appears, return calmly rather than waiting for silence.
  6. Repeat easy successes. Increase only while the dog remains relaxed, mixing in shorter attempts.
  7. Adjust after difficult attempts. Reduce the duration or difficulty and update the rescue.

Food toys are optional support, not a way to distract the dog through an absence they cannot handle. Check what happens after the food finishes.

Keeping a log and video

Record every absence, including difficult ones. Note the duration, location, people present, behavior and any unusual events. Missing observations remain unknown; do not fill them with guesses.

Start filming before departure preparations. Include the resting area and exit, then save short clips showing important moments with timestamps. Do not prolong distress to obtain more footage.

Write “paced between bed and door” rather than “was naughty.” Note whether the dog ate, settled or became more worried.

Share recordings through the rescue's agreed channel and protect household privacy. Our best pet camera guide covers equipment options.

Writing honest notes for adopters

Useful notes describe the dog's needs without defining their personality by a difficulty. Avoid unsupported labels such as “cannot ever be alone” or promises that the issue has disappeared.

For example: “In this foster home, he has rested comfortably during several short practice absences. During a longer attempt, he paced and whined. Familiar company helps, and the rescue has recommended gradual practice.”

Include what has not been tested. Being comfortable with another dog present does not show how the foster dog copes completely alone.

Give notes to the rescue so they can coordinate accurate adopter information. A new home changes the situation, so adopters should begin with easy observation and gradual practice again. Familiar bedding and an agreed support plan can help the transition.

Honest adopter notes checklist

  • State where and when observations were made.
  • Describe comfortable absences actually recorded.
  • Include difficult attempts without minimizing them.
  • Explain what the dog does when worried.
  • Note when the first signs appear.
  • Describe whether the dog settles afterward.
  • Identify the resting arrangement used.
  • Explain which company has helped.
  • Note food toy use and refusal.
  • Describe reactions to barriers or crates.
  • List approaches agreed with the rescue.
  • State what has not been tested.
  • Explain that practice needs rebuilding after the move.
  • Include the rescue's follow-up contact.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not leave for a long period simply to test the dog. Avoid increasing duration according to your calendar rather than the dog's response.

Never punish crying, damage or accidents, use bark-control devices, or leave a distressed dog to cry until quiet. Never shut a panicking dog in a crate and leave.

Do not assume another dog, a food toy or a camera replaces suitable company. Activities from our enrichment guide can support calm time but do not resolve fear by themselves.

Avoid diagnosing the dog or hiding difficult observations to make adoption seem easier. Accurate information helps the rescue find a workable match.

When to get extra help

Dogs Trust's separation anxiety advice explains that changes in behavior are often influenced by physical health, so if your foster dog's behavior has recently changed, contact the rescue to arrange a vet check.

Severe distress, escape attempts, injuries or persistent food refusal also need prompt attention. Follow the rescue's emergency procedure; do not delay urgent veterinary help while waiting for a routine message response.

Ask for a qualified, reward-based behavior professional through the rescue when distress starts quickly, practice is not helping or safety is difficult to maintain.

Your wellbeing matters too. Tell the coordinator if lost sleep, work conflicts or constant supervision are making the placement unsustainable.

Bottom line

A foster carer's observations can make a meaningful difference. Keep the dog safe, arrange approved care, practice comfortable absences and tell the rescue what is happening. Honest records help the next household continue support without expecting the dog to manage everything immediately.

Frequently Asked Questions: Foster Dogs and Separation Anxiety

Do foster dogs get separation anxiety?
Some foster dogs experience separation-related distress, while others are temporarily unsettled by unfamiliar surroundings. Record short absences and describe the pattern to the rescue. Following you or crying once does not establish a diagnosis.
Can I leave my foster dog home alone?
Follow the rescue's policies and begin with very short, observed practice when appropriate. Do not assume the dog can manage your usual workday. Arrange approved company for absences beyond their current comfort.
How long does it take a foster dog to settle in?
It varies with the dog, their health, surroundings and previous experiences. Comfortable eating and sleeping may develop before comfortable alone time. Avoid using a fixed settling-in deadline to decide when longer absences should happen.
Should I crate my foster dog when I leave?
Only consider a crate if the rescue agrees and the dog already relaxes comfortably in it. Never leave a panicking dog shut inside. Discuss a dog-proofed room, suitable gate or other arrangement with the rescue if confinement increases distress.
What should I tell adopters about my foster dog's separation anxiety?
Share specific observations, recorded comfortable absences, difficult situations and what helps. Explain what has not been tested and what support remains necessary. Coordinate with the rescue and make clear that the dog will need gradual practice again in the adoptive home.
