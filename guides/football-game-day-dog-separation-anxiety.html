Football Season Game Days With a Dog Who Has Separation Anxiety

Plan football season care for your anxious dog, with familiar sitters, pre-game routines, watch-party breaks and backup cover when games run late.

Quick answer

Plan care for the whole door-to-door outing, including tailgating, travel and delays. Use a familiar sitter, suitable daycare or trusted person whenever the absence would exceed your dog's current comfortable limit, and arrange a backup for late returns.

A football game can become a much longer outing than the time on the field. Add a tailgate, parking, the walk back and traffic, and your dog may face an absence far beyond anything they currently handle.

We are dog owners who researched this topic, not vets, trainers or product testers, and the products mentioned here were chosen from research, not from personal product testing. This guide focuses on planning football season around your dog's needs, so each Saturday, Sunday or evening game has dependable cover.

Why game days are hard on an anxious dog

Your dog may be used to having everyone home on weekends. A sudden long absence, followed by a late meal or bedtime, can be difficult even when weekday routines seem settled.

The ASPCA's page on separation anxiety describes separation anxiety as distress when dogs are left alone or separated from the people they're attached to, and explains that treatment focuses on gradually teaching the dog to be comfortable alone.

A long game-day absence is not a suitable way to build that skill. A dog who copes with a short errand is not automatically ready for travel, a game and a post-game gathering.

Hosting can also be challenging. Shouting, doorbells and guests moving around may make resting harder, especially if the dog is then shut away from everyone.

Map the season before kickoff

Put expected game days, watch parties, away trips and holiday gatherings on one calendar. For each outing, write down when you actually leave home and when you could realistically return.

Fall weekends filling up with farm trips as well as games? Our fall harvest outing guide covers pumpkin patches, apple picking and hayrides with familiar care arranged at home.

Include the whole trip: meeting friends, tailgating, parking, travel and possible delays. Recheck arrangements when plans change.

Then compare each absence with your dog's current comfortable alone time, based on recent video rather than an old success. Our guide to how long a dog can be left alone explains why potty needs and emotional comfort are separate questions.

Mark every gap that needs company. If your dog currently panics soon after departure, plan care from before you leave until you return. A halfway visit leaves unsupported time on either side.

Book game-day care early

Ask a familiar person about recurring dates before the season gets busy. A regular sitter or trusted family member can make game days more predictable. If your weekdays already need cover too, our working full-time guide has a workday plan you can build on.

Trial the arrangement first. Let the helper spend time with your dog while you are home, then try a short departure that the dog can manage. A dog who likes someone during a visit may still struggle when you leave.

Daycare may suit a dog who enjoys that setting and rests comfortably there. Confirm availability and collection arrangements, and check the current terms. It should not be a first experiment on an important game day.

A walker can provide exercise and a potty break, but cannot cover hours of panic before and after the walk. Our daycare vs. dog walker guide compares those limits; our pet sitter preparation guide helps with the handover.

A pre-game walk and settle routine

Leave enough time for an unhurried walk with sniffing and a potty opportunity. Choose an activity your dog enjoys rather than trying to exhaust them before departure.

Keep meals close to their usual time. After the walk, allow a quiet settling period with water, a comfortable bed and familiar company. A rubber food toy can be offered if your dog uses it safely.

Have the sitter arrive before you start heading out. Give a brief update about eating, potty breaks and anything unusual that morning, then make your goodbye calm and ordinary.

Keep the same basic sequence across the season. Avoid squeezing the walk into the final rushed minutes before kickoff. If you cannot fit everything in, shorten the tailgate or leave later.

Keep every game day within your dog's limit

Your dog's limit is the absence they currently handle comfortably, not the length of your plans. Do not extend it because tickets are booked or the last game went well.

Options include a shorter tailgate, watching selected games at home, or taking turns with a partner or friend. Check that the dog actually settles with the person staying behind.

Record a typical game day with the sitter's knowledge. Review how the dog behaves after you leave, during any agreed brief absence and around your return. Our guide to recording your dog when left alone explains camera placement.

Look for pacing, repeated barking or howling, panting, drooling, scratching at exits or escape attempts. Quietness alone does not prove comfort.

If distress appears, the sitter or backup should respond and change the arrangement. Do not wait for the dog to endure it. For evening games, see our date night guide: daytime tolerance may not carry over unchanged.

Hosting or going to a watch party

At home, give your dog a familiar quiet room or resting area away from speakers and busy doorways. Arrange for someone the dog knows to stay with them if they need company. Brief checks are not enough if they panic between visits.

Introduce the space calmly before guests arrive. It should be a comfortable option, not somewhere the dog is shut away to struggle.

Tell guests who handles doors and who supervises the dog. Keep party food, drinks, serving dishes and trash inaccessible. Ask guests not to feed the dog, and supervise children so they do not crowd a resting animal.

If you attend someone else's party, arrange care for the full outing. Bringing your dog is only suitable if they enjoy that environment, you have permission and you can leave promptly if they become uncomfortable.

Big games: Thanksgiving, playoffs and the Super Bowl

Treat bigger games as separate care bookings, even if ordinary weekends are covered. Your usual helper may have family plans, and your own outing may run later than expected.

For Thanksgiving, account for meals and visits around the game. During the playoffs, tell helpers when plans are provisional and confirm them promptly once you know.

For a Super Bowl party, decide early whether you are hosting or going out. A familiar sitter can provide quiet company while guests gather, or cover your absence elsewhere.

Never rely on a camera as the fallback. Cell signal in a big crowd can be patchy, so the person on the ground needs clear instructions and authority to contact the backup.

For away games involving overnight travel, arrange overnight care with enough daytime company too. Our holiday season guide covers the extra household changes around winter gatherings.

When to see the vet or a behavior professional

Contact your vet early if your dog refuses food, seems unwell or shows a sudden behavior change. Do not assume a busy weekend explains everything.

Strong panic, injury and escape attempts need prompt attention. A qualified reward-based behavior professional can help assess what your dog tolerates and how to build comfortable absences.

Share short clips and simple notes: who was present, how long you were gone and what happened. If the dog struggles even with the sitter there, revise the care arrangement before another long outing.

Never punish barking, howling, damage or accidents; they are signs of distress, not spite. Never shut a panicking dog in a crate, pen or room and leave. Our separation anxiety red flags guide explains which signs are urgent.

US emergency help: If your dog is injured, struggling to breathe, has collapsed, or may have swallowed something dangerous, call your vet or the nearest 24/7 emergency animal hospital now, and call ahead so the team can get ready. For suspected poisoning you can also call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435, open 24 hours a day (a consultation fee may apply). Panic without injury, refusing food, or a sudden change in behavior: call your regular vet the same day. See our separation anxiety red flags guide for which signs are urgent.

Bottom line

Plan the whole game-day absence, then arrange care that matches your dog's current needs. A familiar helper, a calm pre-game routine and backup cover for delays make the season easier to manage.

Review what happens each time. Shortening an outing or watching at home is a practical adjustment when your dog's needs change.

Frequently Asked Questions: Football Game Days With an Anxious Dog

Can I go to a football game if my dog has separation anxiety?
Yes, with suitable care for the complete absence. Use a familiar sitter, trusted person or daycare your dog already enjoys, and arrange backup cover for delays. Do not make game day a test of a longer absence.
How long can I leave my anxious dog for a game day?
Base the decision on recent observations of comfortable alone time, not the game's length. Count travel, tailgating and possible delays. Arrange company for any time beyond what your dog currently handles. Contact your vet early if your dog refuses food or seems unwell after a long game day.
Should I book a sitter or daycare for game days?
A familiar sitter may suit a dog who prefers quiet company at home. Daycare may suit a dog who genuinely enjoys that setting and can rest there. Trial either option before relying on it for a long outing.
Can I host a watch party with a dog who has separation anxiety?
You can plan a quiet resting area with a familiar person providing company as needed. Manage doors, food and guest interactions, and reduce the gathering if your dog cannot settle. Being home does not automatically make a noisy party comfortable.
Can I bring my dog to a tailgate instead of leaving him home?
Only consider it if your dog enjoys that environment and the current rules allow it. Noise, crowds and a long outing may be harder than familiar care at home, so have a prompt exit plan. Never leave your dog in a car at a stadium or tailgate lot, or tied outside.
