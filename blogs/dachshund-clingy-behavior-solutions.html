Why Are Dachshunds So Clingy?

Learn why dachshunds become clingy, when attachment signals pain or anxiety, and how gentle alone-time practice can help your dog settle independently.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated 13 Oct 20269 min read
Quick answer

Dachshunds often form strong attachments, and following you can reflect companionship, habit, or uncertainty. Sudden clinginess may also signal pain or illness; check health concerns first, then build comfortable independence through gradual practice and suitable enrichment.

  • Following you is not automatically separation anxiety; watch what happens when you leave.
  • Sudden clinginess, back pain, or movement changes need veterinary attention.
  • Build independence gradually, returning before your dog becomes distressed.

Your dachshund follows you into the kitchen, waits outside the bathroom, and notices every movement toward the door. Being your dog’s favorite person can feel wonderful—until they cannot relax without you or become distressed whenever you leave.

This guide explains normal attachment, separation-related distress, and medical problems that can look like clinginess. We are dog owners, not veterinarians, and we have not lab-tested the products mentioned here. The aim is comfortable independence, not less affection or forced separation.

Why dachshunds become velcro dogs

Dachshunds developed as hunting dogs working in partnership with people. Their history also required independent problem-solving underground, so hunting ancestry alone does not explain clinginess.

Today, many live as close companions and form strong attachments to familiar people. Your presence may predict meals, walks, warmth, play, and reassurance. Following you can become a useful habit.

Their small size also shapes daily experiences. Being carried, sharing furniture, and receiving help around larger animals or unfamiliar settings can make staying close especially appealing. That does not mean small dogs are inherently anxious.

Changes in routine may increase shadowing. A dog accustomed to constant company can find a new work schedule difficult. An unfamiliar home or frightening experience may also increase proximity-seeking.

Physical comfort matters too. A dachshund who suddenly wants more contact may be uncomfortable rather than simply becoming a “velcro dog.” Look at changes in movement, sleep, appetite, and behavior alongside attachment.

Clingy vs separation anxiety

A normally attached dog may follow you, then settle when you become busy or leave briefly. They can enjoy your company without needing continuous access.

Separation anxiety involves distress associated with being apart. Possible signs include panic, persistent pacing or vocalizing, escape attempts, destruction, and house soiling that occurs during absences.

Compare these patterns:

  • Normal attachment: Your dog watches you leave the room, then returns to resting.
  • Possible distress: They repeatedly pace, whine, or scratch the barrier.
  • Normal adjustment: They pause an activity briefly, then resume.
  • Possible distress: They abandon food and cannot settle after your departure.

These observations are not a diagnosis. Chewing can reflect exploration or boredom, and accidents can have medical causes. Some distressed dogs are quiet, so barking is not the only signal.

A pet camera can show what happens when you are out of sight. Record manageable practice sessions rather than deliberately provoking panic. Note whether your dog struggles with separation itself, confinement, or particular departure cues.

Rule out pain and medical causes first

Sudden new clinginess needs a veterinary check, especially alongside movement changes, interrupted sleep, reduced appetite, or reluctance to be handled.

Dachshunds are predisposed to intervertebral disc disease, or IVDD. Problems with discs in the spine can cause pain and, in some cases, affect movement. Back discomfort may make a dog seek reassurance, hesitate to leave a bed, or resist being picked up.

Watch for a hunched posture, trembling, yelping, stiffness, or reluctance to move. Do not assume these signs are behavioral, and do not stretch or exercise your dog to see whether the problem disappears.

Weakness, dragging limbs, inability to walk, or difficulty controlling toileting needs prompt veterinary attention. Contact your vet for guidance and restrict unnecessary movement while arranging care.

Vision loss, hearing changes, other illness, and pain outside the back can also alter attachment. Follow any prescribed activity restrictions; an independence plan must fit the dog’s medical needs.

Build independence without flooding them

Flooding means exposing a dog to more than they can comfortably handle and waiting for them to get through it. A long absence is not useful practice if your dog spends it panicking.

Begin while you are home. Reward resting on a comfortable bed a short distance away. You can still offer affection; the goal is making another resting location feel safe.

Move briefly, then return while your dog remains relaxed. If they rush after you, make the next attempt easier rather than correcting them.

Practice departure cues separately. Pick up keys and put them down, or put on shoes before sitting nearby. Keep sessions ordinary and short.

During real absence practice, return before distress develops whenever possible. If your dog becomes worried unexpectedly, end the exercise calmly rather than waiting through panic for silence.

Build flexibility with easier repetitions between harder ones. Arrange company when unavoidable absences exceed your dog’s current ability. Exercise and enrichment help meet needs, but neither replaces gradual separation practice.

The independence ladder

This ladder is an example, not a deadline or a promise that every dachshund can manage hours alone. Start below 30 seconds if needed. Each larger step may require many smaller increases.

  1. About 30 seconds nearby: Let your dog rest while you sit slightly farther away in the same room.
  2. About one minute: Move around the room without encouraging your dog to follow.
  3. About two minutes: Practice small changes in position, returning to the resting area calmly.
  4. Briefly out of sight: Step through an interior doorway for seconds, gradually working toward a few comfortable minutes.
  5. About five minutes apart: Use a familiar setup while you remain nearby, checking that your dog truly settles.
  6. About ten minutes: Build short front-door departures through smaller increases rather than jumping straight to this duration.
  7. About 20–30 minutes: Repeat manageable departures at varied lengths, including easier sessions.
  8. About 45–60 minutes: Attempt only after shorter absences are comfortable, with toileting and health needs considered.
  9. Around one to two hours: Progress gradually and confirm behavior with recordings, not assumptions.
  10. A few hours, where appropriate: Consider only for an adult whose comfort, health, and bathroom needs allow it; arrange care otherwise.

Do not advance because a certain number of days has passed. Look for resting, comfortable movement, and normal engagement.

If pacing, whining, barrier scratching, or food refusal appears, step back. The ladder does not override veterinary advice or establish a universal maximum alone time.

Enrichment that fits a dachshund nose

Dachshunds’ hunting background makes scent-based activities a useful angle for enrichment. Start with easy searches on a level floor.

Scatter a small portion of your dog’s usual food in an accessible area or let them find visible pieces around a mat. Gradually make the search slightly more interesting without requiring jumping, climbing, or awkward stretching.

A snuffle mat or nose-work toy can provide a similar activity. Supervise first and remove it if your dog chews the fabric or tries to swallow parts.

A stuffed KONG Classic small may suit some dachshunds, but choose size for the individual dog and chewing style. Begin with easy-to-reach food before trying frozen stuffing.

Use tolerated, dog-safe ingredients and count fillings toward daily food intake. Avoid xylitol-containing products.

Introduce enrichment while you are present. If your dog stops engaging when you leave, shorten the separation; a more elaborate filling will not resolve panic.

Safe alone space (and IVDD-friendly setup)

Choose a familiar area that meets your dog’s comfort and safety needs. A crate can work for a dog who rests comfortably in it, but confinement is not a treatment for anxiety.

Build positive crate experiences with the door open before gradually practicing closure. Stop and reassess if your dog becomes frantic or attempts to escape.

A dog-proof room or pen may be preferable for some dogs. Check barriers, cords, swallowing hazards, and access to water as appropriate.

For an IVDD-friendly setup:

  • Use soft, supportive bedding with an accessible edge.
  • Keep essentials at floor level.
  • Prevent jumping onto or off furniture.
  • Provide secure traction on slippery floors.
  • Avoid opportunities to climb or fall.

Ramps or suitable steps may reduce jumping in everyday life, but they are not automatically appropriate during an active back problem. Ask your vet what movement is permitted.

Medical confinement requires a specific plan. If prescribed rest causes distress, contact your vet rather than changing restrictions independently or relying on an anxiety product.

When to get extra help

Use this checklist to decide when home practice needs professional support:

Why Are Dachshunds So Clingy?: when to get extra help

  • Clinginess appeared suddenly or accompanies pain, stiffness, or reduced appetite.
  • Your dog panics during even very brief separations.
  • Escape attempts cause injuries or put your dog at risk.
  • Destruction or house soiling repeatedly occurs during absences.
  • Progress stalls despite consistently manageable practice.
  • Your schedule makes avoiding overwhelming absences difficult.
  • Aggression, severe distress, or major sleep changes develop.

Begin with a vet for possible medical causes. A qualified reward-based trainer can help with settling skills, while significant separation distress may require a certified behaviorist or veterinary behaviorist.

Share recordings and a brief history. Explain what happens, when it begins, and what you have tried. Urgent neurological signs or injuries should not wait for a training appointment.

Looking after yourself

Living with a dog who struggles to be apart can limit ordinary activities and leave you feeling guilty. Difficulty does not mean you have failed.

Keep practice manageable and record small improvements. Resting while you move nearby may be a meaningful step even when longer departures remain difficult.

Arrange trusted help where possible. Give clear instructions about your dog’s comfort limits, enrichment, and any back-related restrictions.

You do not need to withdraw affection to prove you are serious about training. Predictable companionship and carefully practiced distance can coexist.

If the plan becomes overwhelming, ask for professional help adjusting it. A workable routine matters for both of you.

Bottom line

Dachshund clinginess can reflect companionship, habit, uncertainty, or discomfort. Normal attachment is different from panic during separation, and sudden changes deserve a veterinary check.

Build independence in small, comfortable steps, use accessible scent enrichment, and protect your dog’s back. Calming aids may provide support, but progress depends on an appropriate health and training plan.

Frequently Asked Questions: Clingy Dachshunds

Why does my dachshund follow me everywhere?
Following may reflect companionship, habit, or anticipation of activities such as food and walks. Look at whether your dog can settle without you rather than treating shadowing alone as anxiety.
Does being clingy mean my dachshund has separation anxiety?
No, a dog can enjoy constant company and still cope comfortably with absences. Panic, persistent pacing, destruction, or house soiling during separation warrants closer assessment.
Should I ignore my dachshund to make them independent?
You do not need to remove affection or ignore genuine distress. Reward comfortable resting and practice small changes in distance that your dog can manage.
Could sudden clinginess mean back pain?
Yes, pain can change proximity-seeking behavior, and dachshunds can develop IVDD or other painful problems. Contact your vet, particularly if movement changes, trembling, yelping, or weakness accompany it.
How long will independence training take?
There is no reliable timetable for every dog; starting ability, health, and daily circumstances differ. Advance according to comfortable behavior and seek help if very brief separations still cause distress.
