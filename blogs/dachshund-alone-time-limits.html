How Long Can You Leave a Dachshund Alone? (Age-by-Age Guide)

Dachshunds are bold, but that does not mean they cope well with long stretches alone. Many are strongly attached to their people, and every dog's comfortable limit is different. This age-by-age guide gives you practical starting ranges, the warning signs, and a practical protocol for every life stage.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated November 13, 20268 min read
Quick answer

For prepared adult Dachshunds, about four hours is a ceiling, not a target. Puppies under six months need one to two hours at most; seniors aged eight or older and miniature Dachshunds need shorter windows.

  • Practice short absences before attempting longer periods.
  • Shorten absences for puppies, seniors and miniature Dachshunds.
  • Watch for howling, door digging, chewing or house soiling.

Why Alone Time Can Be Hard for Dachshunds

Most dogs need to learn that being left alone is safe. Dachshunds were bred to hunt badgers and other burrowing animals, and many of today's Dachshunds are very people-focused companions that like to stay close to their owners. When a strongly attached dog is left for longer than it is ready for, the result can be real distress rather than simple boredom.

Not sure whether your Dachshund is bored or genuinely anxious? Our separation anxiety vs. boredom: 10 signs guide shows how to tell the difference.

What makes this particularly tricky is the breed's famous stubbornness. Owners often assume a confident, headstrong dog will handle solitude just fine. That is not always the case. Their independence is task-driven: a Dachshund following a scent trail is self-sufficient. A Dachshund sitting in an empty apartment is not performing a task — they're simply waiting for their people to return, and for many, that waiting looks a lot like distress.

Before working on alone-time tolerance, read the Dachshund Complete Anxiety Guide to rule out IVDD-related pain as a contributor. A dog in physical discomfort may cope less well with alone time, and training is likely to go better once pain has been checked and treated by your vet.

Important: If your Dachshund's alone-time distress has appeared or worsened suddenly — with no change in schedule — do not assume it is purely behavioral. Pain and illness can change behavior, and Dachshunds are prone to IVDD (Intervertebral Disc Disease), a painful spinal condition. Book a vet check early, and go straight away if you notice back pain, reluctance to move, wobbliness or food refusal.

The Age-by-Age Alone-Time Guide

Puppies Under 12 Weeks: 30–60 Minutes Maximum

Very young Dachshund puppies are still learning to cope on their own, and they need frequent meals, toilet breaks and company. The practical ceiling here is 30–60 minutes, and even that should be gradual, not immediate. These weeks are foundational: a puppy who learns that alone time ends predictably, without catastrophe, gets a good start for later alone-time training.

3–6 Months: 1–2 Hours

A Dachshund puppy between 12 weeks and 6 months can begin building toward 2-hour increments, but this is still a period requiring active investment. Bladder control is developing — standard guidance is one hour per month of age, so a 4-month-old should not realistically hold it for more than 4 hours. This is also an important socialization period, so short, positive alone-time practice now is much easier than fixing problems later.

Use this period deliberately: always leave a food puzzle (scatter-fed dry kibble on a lick mat works well) so that your departure predicts something good. Keep departures and returns calm and matter-of-fact. Low-key departures and returns give your dog less to get worked up about.

6–12 Months: Up to 3 Hours

Adolescent Dachshunds are often the most challenging stage for alone time. Physical maturity is outpacing emotional maturity: they have adult energy and adult stubbornness, but the impulse control of a teenager. Many owners find that anxiety behaviors they thought they had resolved at 4 months re-emerge at 8 months — setbacks like this are common in adolescence and are not a training failure.

The three-hour ceiling at this stage is a realistic working maximum, not a goal. If your adolescent Dachshund is showing distress inside 90 minutes, that is your actual current threshold, and that is where to begin desensitization work — not from 3 hours.

Go Slowly: When building alone-time tolerance, increase duration in small steps, and only after several calm sessions at the current length. For a Dachshund currently managing 60 minutes calmly, try a little longer — not 90 minutes. If your dog shows distress, go back to a length they handled calmly. Jumping too fast tends to cause setbacks.

Adult Dachshunds (2–7 Years): 4 Hours Ceiling

A well-prepared, well-exercised adult Dachshund with good alone-time conditioning can manage approximately 4 hours without significant distress. This is a ceiling, not an average. It assumes the dog has had 20–30 minutes of physical activity before departure, has a food puzzle to occupy the first 20 minutes of alone time, has access to their preferred den or burrowing space, and has been gradually desensitized to your departure cues.

Full workday absences of 8–9 hours are too long for most Dachshunds. Owners working full-time hours should plan for a midday check-in by a dog walker, neighbor, or family member. Alternatively, doggy daycare works well for socially confident Dachshunds — though some find group daycare settings overstimulating and more stressful than a quiet home. Know your individual dog.

Senior Dachshunds (8+ Years): Return to 2–3 Hours

Senior Dachshunds often regress in their alone-time tolerance, even dogs who were comfortable with longer windows in adulthood. There are two common reasons: the natural increase in anxiety that accompanies aging and declining sensory acuity (the world becomes less predictable when hearing and vision fade), and the increasing prevalence of IVDD-related pain in this age group. A senior Dachshund who has managed 4 hours comfortably for years but now shows distress at 2 hours warrants a veterinary check before a behavioral intervention. Treat pain first.

Building the Right Alone-Time Setup

The Pre-Departure Routine

Many dogs quickly learn to associate specific behaviors with your departure — picking up keys, putting on shoes, checking your phone before leaving. Once these cues predict the distress of being left, the anxiety begins before you are even out the door. Desensitize your pre-departure cues by performing them repeatedly throughout the day with no subsequent departure. Pick up your keys, put them down, sit on the sofa. Put your shoes on, walk to the kitchen, take them off. With steady repetition, these cues can start to matter less to your dog.

The Den Space

Many Dachshunds love to burrow under blankets, and a covered retreat can help them rest while you are out: a purpose-made cave bed, a low table with a soft pad underneath, or a crate with a blanket over it if your dog is already relaxed in a crate. Keep it low, with no jumping needed, and skip any confinement that leads to escape attempts.

White Noise or Calming Music

Many Dachshunds are alert barkers. Outside noise — a passing car, a neighbor's door — can set off alarm barking, which makes it harder for the dog to settle. Some dogs rest better with a white noise machine or calm music near their alone-time space; check on camera whether it helps yours.

Camera First: Before investing in any calming product or changing your routine, spend a week filming your Dachshund for the first 60 minutes after you leave (see our best pet cameras for separation anxiety guide for setup tips). You may find either that your dog settles much faster than expected — or that distress begins within 5 minutes, far earlier than assumed. Knowing the actual timeline changes which interventions are most appropriate.

When the Problem Needs Professional Help

If your Dachshund cannot settle within 30 minutes of your departure despite consistent training, or if they are injuring themselves attempting to escape (broken nails, bleeding gums from crate chewing, raw paws from digging), the severity of anxiety has exceeded what environmental management alone can address. Consult your veterinarian about options including short-term anxiolytic medication to allow behavioral training to take effect, or a referral to a veterinary behaviorist. Any medication decision belongs to your vet, and it is usually used alongside a gradual training plan. Progress often takes weeks to months, so a slow start is normal.

Frequently Asked Questions: Dachshund Alone Time

How long can you leave a Dachshund alone?
Adult Dachshunds (2–7 years) can typically manage up to 4 hours with proper preparation — exercise beforehand, a food puzzle, and a den space. Four hours is a ceiling, not a target. Puppies under 6 months should not exceed 1–2 hours, and senior Dachshunds (8+) often need shorter windows of 2–3 hours. Your own dog's calm limit matters more than any chart.
What happens if you leave a Dachshund alone too long?
Beyond their comfortable limit, Dachshunds may howl, bark, dig at doors and carpets, chew near exits, or have house-soiling accidents — not from spite, but from stress or simply needing a toilet break. Repeated long absences can make a dog more worried about being left, so it is easier to prevent this than to fix it later. If your dog hurts itself, stops eating or seems unwell, talk to your vet.
Can Dachshunds be left alone all day while I work?
A standard 8–9 hour workday is too long for most Dachshunds without intervention. Practical solutions include a midday dog walker, doggy daycare for socially confident dogs, or a second canine companion (introduced after the first dog's anxiety is stable). If none of these are feasible, a Dachshund may not be well-suited to a full-time solo office schedule.
Do miniature Dachshunds handle alone time differently than standard Dachshunds?
Size alone does not decide how well a Dachshund copes with being alone; age, health, training and temperament matter more. As small dogs, miniatures may need toilet breaks more often, especially as puppies. The age-by-age guidelines in this article apply to both sizes, but miniature owners may want to start at the lower end of each range.
How do I build my Dachshund's tolerance for being alone?
Start with departures measured in seconds. Leave, close the door, return before your Dachshund reacts. Reward calm behavior, not excitement. Increase duration in small steps, only after several calm sessions at the current length. Use a high-value food puzzle right before departure to build a positive association. Progress often takes weeks to months, and that is normal; if there is little progress or your dog panics, ask your vet or a qualified behavior professional.
