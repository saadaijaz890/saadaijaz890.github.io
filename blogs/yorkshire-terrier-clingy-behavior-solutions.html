Why Is My Yorkie So Clingy?

Many Yorkshire Terriers like to stay right next to their people. Here is why that happens, how to tell normal attachment from separation anxiety, and how to help your Yorkie settle on their own.

✍️ By the AnxietyFreePups team📅 Last updated November 17, 2026⏱ 7 min read
Quick answer: Why Is My Yorkie So Clingy?

Yorkies were bred as small working terriers and later became popular companion lapdogs; the AKC describes a "feisty, old-time terrier" under the glossy coat, and adults usually weigh around 7 pounds. A tiny dog who is often carried, cuddled, and rewarded for being close can easily learn that following you pays off, and that is not a problem by itself. Panic when you leave, such as persistent barking, scratching at doors, or accidents only when alone, is different and needs gradual training. If clinginess appears suddenly, or your Yorkie seems unwell or stops eating, call your vet promptly; very small dogs and puppies can go downhill quickly when they do not eat.

  • Normal: Following you while still eating, playing, and resting comfortably when apart.
  • Watch for: Barking, howling, pacing, escape attempts, or house soiling that happen mainly when your Yorkie is alone.
  • Vet first: Sudden clinginess, signs of pain, food refusal, or other changes in health.

Why are Yorkies so clingy?

The AKC says Yorkshire Terriers earned their living as ratters in mines and mills before they became lapdogs, and describes the breed as a "feisty, old-time terrier" under the long coat. Today most Yorkies live as close companions, and many enjoy being wherever their people are.

Size plays a part too. A dog who weighs around 7 pounds is easy to pick up, carry, and keep on your lap, so being close often earns attention, comfort, and treats. Over time, following you can become a strong habit. That attachment is not a problem in itself.

We could not find reliable data showing that Yorkies are more prone to separation anxiety than other breeds, and individual dogs vary a lot. Clinginess can grow when a Yorkie gets too little play or mental activity, when routines change, after a move or a new baby, or when following you is the most interesting thing in the house. Pain or illness can also make a dog stick closer than usual.

Is my Yorkie clingy or anxious?

A clingy Yorkie wants to be near you but can still relax when you leave the room or the house. Separation anxiety is distress about being apart. The ASPCA lists signs such as persistent barking or howling, chewing or digging at doors and windows, escape attempts, pacing, and urinating or defecating that happen when the dog is left alone or separated from their people.

Following you around the house does not tell you which one you have. The most useful check is to record your Yorkie for the first 20 to 30 minutes after you leave (our guide to recording your dog explains how). If your dog settles, you are mostly dealing with habit and attachment. If they bark, pace, or scratch at the door, treat it as separation distress and go slowly. Could my Yorkie be clingy because of a health problem?

Yes, sometimes. Dogs who are in pain, unwell, or losing hearing or vision may stay closer to their people. Talk to your vet first if the clinginess started suddenly, or if you notice limping, trembling, reluctance to jump, mouth or tooth discomfort, vomiting, diarrhea, changes in drinking or toileting, or less interest in food.

Do not wait on food refusal: Very small dogs and puppies can get into trouble quickly if they stop eating. If your Yorkie skips meals or seems weak or wobbly, call your vet promptly rather than treating it as a behavior problem.

How do I help my Yorkie be more independent?

The aim is not to push your Yorkie away. It is to teach them that being apart is safe and that calm rest on their own is rewarded. Keep sessions short and stop before your dog becomes worried.

  1. Pick a comfy resting spot. Put a bed or mat a few steps away from where you sit. Reward your Yorkie with a small treat or calm praise when they lie there.
  2. Add tiny bits of distance. Stand up, take one or two steps, and come back before your dog follows or fusses. Slowly build to walking into the next room for a few seconds.
  3. Practice departure cues. Pick up your keys or put on shoes, then sit back down. This helps those cues stop predicting that you are leaving.
  4. Keep real absences below the worry point. Start with absences your Yorkie stays relaxed through, even if that is under a minute, and lengthen them gradually. Our separation anxiety training plan lays out the steps.
  5. Make coming and going low-key. Calm hellos and goodbyes help your dog see departures as ordinary.
Tip: Short, upbeat training sessions of a few minutes work well for small dogs. Watch your Yorkie, not the clock, and end on a calm moment.
Warning: Never punish a clingy or anxious dog. Punishment can increase fear and make separation problems worse.

Which small-dog habits make clinginess worse?

Yorkies are often carried everywhere and picked up whenever they ask. Affection is fine, but if every whine or paw tap gets an instant cuddle, your dog may learn to ask more and more. Try these instead:

  • Let your Yorkie walk on four feet more often, at home and outside, where it is safe.
  • Reward quiet, settled behavior with attention, rather than responding mainly to pawing or barking.
  • Give daily play and short sniff walks, and use food puzzles or scatter feeding as enrichment. Enrichment helps fill the day but does not on its own teach a dog to be comfortable alone.
  • Keep a predictable daily routine for meals, walks, and rest.
  • If your dog is afraid of noises, set up a quiet resting area and see our Yorkie noise anxiety guide.

Some owners try a snug wrap such as a ThunderShirt. Evidence for pressure wraps is mixed, some dogs dislike them, and they do not replace gradual alone-time practice. Use one only if your Yorkie is relaxed wearing it, and never leave it on unsupervised for long periods.

When should I get professional help?

Ask for help if your Yorkie panics within minutes of being alone, hurts themselves trying to escape, has repeated accidents or destruction only when alone, or you cannot find a starting point where they stay calm. Start with your vet to rule out health problems and discuss options for severe anxiety. A qualified positive-reinforcement trainer or a veterinary behaviorist can then help with a step-by-step plan. Frequently Asked Questions: Yorkie Clinginess

Why is my Yorkie so clingy?
Yorkies are small companion dogs who are often carried, cuddled, and kept close, so many learn that being near you is rewarding. Boredom, routine changes, and pain or illness can make clinginess stronger. Following you is not automatically separation anxiety; watch how your Yorkie behaves when you are apart.
Are Yorkies clingy dogs?
Many Yorkies are very people-focused, but individual dogs vary a lot. We could not find reliable data showing Yorkies are more likely to have separation anxiety than other breeds. A Yorkie who follows you but stays relaxed when alone is simply attached.
Is clinginess the same as separation anxiety?
No. Clinginess means your dog likes to be close to you. The ASPCA describes separation anxiety as distress when a dog is left alone or separated from their people, with signs such as persistent barking or howling, chewing or digging at exits, escape attempts, pacing, and house soiling.
How can I help my Yorkie be more independent?
Reward calm rest on their own bed, practice very short absences your Yorkie stays relaxed through, and add food puzzles or sniffing games as enrichment. Increase time apart slowly and use a camera recording to check how your dog really copes.
When should I call the vet about a clingy Yorkie?
Call your vet if clinginess starts suddenly, or comes with pain, limping, trembling, vomiting, diarrhea, changes in drinking or toileting, or not eating. Very small dogs and puppies should be seen promptly if they stop eating. Your vet can also discuss options if separation distress is severe.
