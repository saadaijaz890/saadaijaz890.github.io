French Bulldog Puppy Crate Training: 5-Step Protocol to Stop Nighttime Crying

Many French Bulldog puppies cry the first nights in a crate. Here is a gentle, source-backed routine that builds crate comfort step by step, keeps a flat-faced puppy cool and calm, and never leaves them to cry it out.

By the AnxietyFreePups teamLast updated November 28, 20268 min read
Quick answer: How do I stop my French Bulldog puppy crying in the crate at night?

Start with the crate in or near your bedroom, as Humane World for Animals suggests for puppies. Build short, happy crate sessions in the daytime (meals and treats inside) before overnight use, give a calm, boring potty trip when a young puppy cries, and never force your puppy in or leave them to cry it out (AKC). Puppies under 6 months shouldn’t stay in a crate for more than 3 to 4 hours at a time (Humane World for Animals). French Bulldogs are a short-faced breed, so keep the crate cool and well ventilated; noisy or labored breathing that doesn’t settle needs your vet. A crate does not treat separation anxiety.

Why Do French Bulldog Puppies Cry in the Crate at Night?

The AKC describes the French Bulldog as “playful, alert, adaptable,” and one of the most popular small breeds, especially with city dwellers. Many Frenchie puppies come home used to constant company from littermates and people, so a first night alone in a crate is a big change. Whining and crying are normal ways for them to call out.

The AKC crate training guide gives a useful rule of thumb: vocalizing that begins within minutes of going in the crate is likely stress or anxiety, which is common in puppies who haven’t yet been taught to be alone. Barking or whining after your puppy has been settled for a while is more likely boredom, hunger, or a need to go to the bathroom. The answer to the first is a slower, more positive introduction to the crate; the answer to the second is a quick potty trip or a better pre-bed routine.

Check health first: Crying can also mean pain or illness. If your puppy refuses food, vomits, has diarrhea, strains or has accidents often, or seems unwell, call your vet early rather than treating it as a training problem.

Is Crate Training Safe for a Flat-Faced Puppy?

Yes, with a few precautions. The American College of Veterinary Surgeons (ACVS) lists the French Bulldog among brachycephalic (short-headed) breeds prone to obstructed breathing, and says exercise intolerance, blue tongue and gums, and occasional collapse are common in affected dogs, especially after over-activity, excitement, or excessive heat or humidity. That is one more reason not to let a Frenchie puppy cry or panic for long stretches in a crate.

  • Choose a crate with good airflow, and keep it out of direct sun and away from heaters.
  • Provide water in the crate, as the AKC recommends, and keep the room comfortably cool.
  • If you cover the crate for a den-like feel, leave plenty of sides open for air.
Call your vet right away if your puppy has noisy or labored breathing that doesn’t settle when they calm down, blue or gray gums, or collapses. For more on breathing and stress in this breed, see French Bulldog breathing and anxiety.

How Do You Crate Train a French Bulldog Puppy? 5 Steps

Step 1 — Start With the Crate in or Near Your Bedroom

Humane World for Animals suggests putting the crate in your bedroom or a nearby hallway at first, especially for a puppy. Puppies often need to go outside during the night, so you want to hear them, and keeping them close helps them avoid linking the crate with social isolation. The AKC also suggests a relatively quiet spot. Once your puppy sleeps comfortably through the night, you can move the crate gradually if you want it somewhere else.

Some puppies prefer a wire crate so they can see around them, while others settle better in a darker, more den-like crate (AKC). For a Frenchie, whichever style you choose, airflow comes first.

Step 2 — Make the Crate Comfortable (Optional Comfort Items)

Some owners add an item that smells like them, such as a worn T-shirt, or a heartbeat-style comfort toy. These are optional comfort items, not a cure for crying. Check that your puppy doesn’t chew and swallow fabric, and remove bedding or toys that are being destroyed.

Step 3 — Practice in the Daytime Before Overnight Use

Humane World for Animals recommends feeding meals near the crate, then inside it. Once your puppy is standing comfortably in the crate to eat, close the door during the meal and open it as soon as they finish. With each meal, leave the door closed a few minutes longer, until they stay in the crate for about 10 minutes after eating. If they start whining to be let out, you increased the time too quickly; go back to a shorter stretch next time.

The AKC also suggests tossing treats into the crate whenever your puppy approaches it and continuing to reward them every few seconds while they stay inside. Once your puppy can stay quietly in the crate for about 30 minutes with you mostly out of sight, Humane World for Animals says you can begin short absences and overnight crating. This may take several days or weeks.

Before bed: Plan the last meal early enough for a final potty trip, then take your puppy out right before crating. An empty bladder removes one common reason for night waking.

Step 4 — Use the Same Calm Bedtime Routine Every Night

A predictable wind-down makes the crate part of the evening rather than a surprise. For example: a short, gentle play session (not hard exercise, especially in warm weather), a final potty break, a safe chew inside the crate, then lights down. Repeat it at about the same time each night. For broader night-time worries, see nighttime anxiety in dogs.

Step 5 — Respond to Night Crying Without Making It Worse

Young puppies often can’t make it through the night. Humane World for Animals notes that puppies under 6 months shouldn’t stay in a crate for more than three or four hours at a time because they can’t control their bladders or bowels for that long. If your puppy cries and may need to go, make it a purposeful, boring trip: carry or leash them to the potty spot, wait, praise quietly, and return them to the crate with no play.

If you are sure your puppy doesn’t need to go, Humane World for Animals suggests waiting briefly to see if they settle and never punishing whining. If whining continues after several minutes, offer a potty trip. The AKC warns against leaving a dog to cry it out or crating them longer than they’re ready for. Watch for signs that the crate causes panic: pacing, shaking, scratching or biting at the door, long bouts of crying, drooling, or heavy breathing. If you see them, go back a step and get help from your vet or a qualified behavior professional.

What Mistakes Make Frenchie Crate Training Harder?

Skipping Daytime Practice

Going straight to a full night in the crate, before your puppy has had short, positive daytime sessions, makes the crate feel like being shut away. Build up gradually while you are home, as described in Step 3.

Using the Crate as Punishment

Both the AKC and Humane World for Animals say a crate should never be used for punishment. Keep it linked with good things: meals, treats, safe chews, and rest.

Crating Too Long or Expecting Too Much Too Soon

Crate training can take a few days to several weeks. A dog crated all day and night doesn’t get enough exercise or human interaction and can become depressed or anxious, according to Humane World for Animals. If you need to be gone longer than your puppy can manage, arrange a dog walker or sitter.

Using the Crate to Fix Separation Anxiety

The AKC and Humane World for Animals both note that a crate won’t solve separation anxiety, and dogs who panic may hurt themselves trying to escape. The ASPCA describes separation anxiety as distress triggered when a dog is separated from the people they’re attached to. If your puppy shows that kind of distress whenever you leave, not just at bedtime, talk to your vet or a qualified behavior professional, and see our separation anxiety guide.

Frequently Asked Questions: French Bulldog Crate Training

Why does my French Bulldog puppy cry all night in the crate?
Many puppies vocalize when they are new to being alone. The AKC says whining or barking that starts within minutes of going in the crate is likely stress or anxiety, especially in puppies who haven’t yet been taught to be alone, while crying after your puppy has settled is more likely boredom, hunger, or a bathroom need. Keep the crate near your bed at first, build crate time gradually in the daytime, and take a quick potty trip if your puppy may need to go.
Should I let my French Bulldog puppy cry it out in the crate?
No. The AKC advises never forcing your dog into the crate or leaving them to cry it out. Frenchies are also a brachycephalic (short-faced) breed, and the ACVS notes that breathing problems in these dogs are often worse after excitement or heat. If your puppy whines, first consider a potty trip; if you are sure they don’t need to go, Humane World for Animals suggests waiting briefly for them to settle and never punishing whining. Noisy or labored breathing that doesn’t settle, blue or gray gums, or collapse needs a vet right away.
How long does it take to crate train a French Bulldog puppy?
It varies. Humane World for Animals says crate training can take anywhere from a few days to several weeks, depending on how consistent you are, and that short, frequent sessions work better than long ones. There is no reliable night count for Frenchies, so go at your puppy’s pace.
What size crate does a French Bulldog puppy need?
The AKC says the crate should be just large enough for your puppy to stand up, turn around, and lie down comfortably, and suggests buying a crate for your puppy’s adult size with a divider so it can grow with your dog. Adult French Bulldogs weigh under 28 pounds (AKC), so check the crate maker’s size chart against your puppy’s expected adult size and make sure the crate has good airflow.
Is crate training cruel for French Bulldogs?
Not when it is done gradually and kindly. The AKC and Humane World for Animals both say a crate should never be used as punishment, and Humane World for Animals warns that a dog crated all day and night can become depressed or anxious. Keep crate time short, link it with meals, treats, and rest, and stop and get help if your puppy panics in the crate.
